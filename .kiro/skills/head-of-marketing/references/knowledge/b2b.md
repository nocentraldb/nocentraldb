# B2B and Technology Markets

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Tybout/Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018.

## Character of the sources for this topic area
**This area is heavily KOB-weighted, and that is not a selection decision but the finding.** KOB devotes three chapters (8, 9, 11) to the topic, with named frameworks, three worked strategy cases and an eight-stage process. **TIM treats B2B in a single but very substantial section** — the central question every business buyer asks themselves — plus one diffusion example. **When reading this area, keep the one-sidedness in mind: whoever applies only the KOB side loses the status dimension of the purchase decision, which TIM captures in one sentence.**
**The vintage of the data is the hardest limit of this area: KOB is from 2005.** The brand values, technology platforms and competitive relations named (Intel as co-branding power centre, .NET as an ingredient brand, Nokia as a reference customer) describe a market before cloud, SaaS standardisation, the app economy and platform consolidation. **The mechanics of the frameworks transfer; the examples and the magnitudes do not.**

## Core concepts and definitions

### Branding applies everywhere
"**Everything is branded — from oxygen to airplanes.**" And the observation that shows the status of B2B brands: **"Nike and Starbucks — brands routinely cited as models of branding — are OVERSHADOWED IN VALUE by Goldman Sachs, Pfizer, and Hewlett-Packard."**
The brand values named (Interbrand, as of 2005): **Coca-Cola just under USD 70 billion, GE up to almost 70, Microsoft 61.4, IBM 53.8, Intel 33.49, Nokia 24.04, Apple 6.85 (from 5.55 the year before).** The Apple jump: **"driven largely by the iPod, which represented 35 PERCENT OF APPLE'S REVENUE and increased the brand's value by 24 PERCENT."** [Source: KOB, ch. 9 and 11]

### The three differences between B2B and consumer brands
1. **Acceptance** — in B2B, branding is less established as a discipline and has to be justified internally.
2. **Purchase process** — several participants, formalised procurement, longer cycles, technical verifiability.
3. **Architectural freedom** — less freedom than in consumer goods. **Example: Boeing** — the model nomenclature is bound by customer expectation and history.
**The fundamental statement of value: "Brands help reduce that subjectivity and convey to customers information that they CANNOT EASILY OBSERVE through testing or analysis."** The decisive example of information that cannot be verified: **"What will be the supplier's RESPONSIVENESS to any unforeseen or unanticipated problems that occur in the customer's usage?"**
And the robustness statement: **"Greater brand familiarity also creates more confidence, increases purchase intentions, and can even OVERCOME NEGATIVE EXPERIENCES."** [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

### The value proposition
Definition: **"a persuasive one- or two-sentence answer to the question: WHY SHOULD I DO BUSINESS WITH YOUR FIRM AND NOT YOUR COMPETITOR?"**
**The prohibition list, verbatim: "highest performance", "best quality", "world class" — "Such statements are NOT persuasive because they are NOT DIFFERENTIATING; every competitor makes them."**
**The political diagnosis of why vague statements survive (the sharpest sentence of the chapter): "Firms often find it difficult to be specific about their target customers. Managers may be reluctant to identify a target because they would rather have the customers, AND THEIR OWN SALES FORCE, believe that ALL CUSTOMERS ARE EQUALLY GOOD PROSPECTS!"** This is the same mechanism that TIM calls "everyone" — but here with the internal cause named: **it is an avoidance of conflict with sales, not a thinking error.** [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

### The association hierarchy — focal, superordinate, subordinate
- **Focal associations** — the core meaning of the brand.
- **Superordinate associations** — the higher-order category or class in which the brand places itself. **The example that shows the status dimension in B2B: "Goldman Sachs signals 'a measure of success' BY PLACING THE ORGANIZATION IN AN ELITE CLUB."**
- **Subordinate associations** — concrete sensory or design elements. Examples: **Caterpillar's yellow, the IBM typeface, the Apple start-up chime.**
**The career statement that names the real purchase driver: "purchase decisions that make A MANAGER LOOK GOOD ... and thereby ADVANCE THAT MANAGER'S CAREER."** And the addition that makes it methodologically difficult: **this frequently happens UNCONSCIOUSLY.** [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
**TIM's version of the same thing, in one sentence: "The one question that every business buyer asks herself is, 'WHAT WILL I TELL MY BOSS?'"** And the spelling out: "Business buyers don't care about you. **They don't care about your dreams and your goals.** ... What they care about is themselves, and the risk they're taking when they buy from you." The required answer: **"a simple, resilient story about why she made the right choice on behalf of the company."** [Source: TIM, ch. 19]
**Note: These two formulations are the same finding from two traditions** — KOB has the construct (superordinate associations, career effect, unconscious), TIM the operative question. For use, TIM's question is the more usable test question, KOB's version the more precise explanation.

### The example hierarchy IBM ThinkPad X30
The fully declined structure from the company via the product family to the model shows how **superordinate (IBM), focal (ThinkPad) and subordinate (X30)** associations interact in a real naming system. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

### The three benefit dimensions in the technology market
**Emotional, functional and economic benefits**, connected by **usability** as the bridge between functional and emotional benefit. The rule: do not promote any dimension in isolation, because in B2B a purchase decision has to pass through all three (user, engineer, purchaser). [Source: KOB, ch. 11]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### The three competitive strategies for B2B brands
**STRATEGY 1 — SUPERIORITY.** Superior performance on a dimension relevant to the customer, maintained and promoted.
**The worked case Mondi Rotatrim:** In South Africa, copy paper was a commodity. In the **1980s** Mondi **was the first firm to switch from guillotine to rotary cutting** — more precise, no pressure on the paper edge, only four or five sheets at a time. That reduced copier jams. In the **1990s** **all major competitors** followed, and the technical advantage was gone.
**In 1997 the next step: an alkali-based formulation with precipitated calcium carbonate — EIGHT TIMES less abrasive than the ground calcium carbonate of the competitors.** The South African Bureau of Standards tested it: **Rotatrim was the ONLY brand that met the SABS standards for superior business paper.**
Result: "Rivals, disadvantaged with **lower prices and smaller volumes**, were unable or unwilling to match Mondi's spending. ... Mondi Rotatrim enjoyed a stable and comfortable **35 PERCENT MARKET SHARE.** Its perceived superior performance enabled Mondi Rotatrim to **sustain a pricing premium** in certain markets while maintaining its customer share."
**THE DANGER OF THIS STRATEGY, explicitly named: "arms race on superiority ... EVEN BEYOND THAT WHICH CUSTOMERS REQUIRE."** So: a superiority contest can lead to over-engineering that nobody pays for.
**STRATEGY 2 — DIFFERENTIATION.** Occupy a different dimension instead of the same one better.
**The worked case Dalloz Safety Products:** Instead of offering **more protection**, Dalloz turned safety glasses into **fashion eyewear**. The reasoning lies in a behavioural insight, not in the technology: **"workers must ACTUALLY WEAR the glasses"** — and young workers felt "**like a dork**" in the previous models. The source of value for the purchaser was therefore not protective performance but **compliance**: glasses that are worn reduce accidents and insurance costs. **This is the clearest case in the system that the relevant benefit dimension can lie with the end user while the buyer is someone else.**
**STRATEGY 3 — BUNDLING.** Combine commodity elements into a package that cannot be compared.
**The reasoning: "A commodity is a commodity because customers have come to believe, RIGHTLY OR WRONGLY, that there is no meaningful difference between brands. As a result, PRICE IS THE ONLY REMAINING BASIS FOR CHOICE. By bundling commodities together, ONE CREATES DIFFERENCES WHERE NONE EXISTED. Suddenly the basis of comparison MUST SHIFT FROM PRICE."** Additional benefits: time savings, lower transaction costs, a larger role for the brand name.
**The worked case Texas Instruments:** Semiconductors were considered a commodity with price and profit cycles. TI concentrated on **digital signal processors** for mobile phone makers such as **Nokia**, who were caught in a price war. "**TI recognized that FOR THEM SPEED WAS PERHAPS MORE IMPORTANT THAN PRICE**", and delivered bundles of **DSPs plus software, development tools and services.** Former CEO **Thomas Engibous: "It's the difference between MAKING A SPARK PLUG or BUILDING THE ENGINE."**
**The price of the strategy, explicitly named: "by focusing on DSPs, TI REDUCED THE NUMBER OF CUSTOMERS IT CAN SERVE. But in exchange for those limits, its margin, sales growth, market share, and profits have all grown faster than rivals' ... its market capitalization has increased FIVEFOLD."**
**AND THE RISK, explicitly named: "bundling REDUCES THE IMPACT OF INNOVATION IN ANY ONE PRODUCT."** So: whoever bundles protects their margin against price comparison, but loses the leverage of individual product breakthroughs. **This is the mirror-image danger to the superiority trap.** [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

### The four price sensitivity findings
1. **"Brand leaders tend as a result to ask for and OBTAIN HIGHER PRICES."**
2. **"Brand leaders tend to be better able to INCREASE prices without losing business, compared to rivals with weaker brands."** (Sivakumar/Raj 1997, *Journal of Marketing*.)
3. **The counter-intuitive one: "One might predict that leading brands have the most to lose and, therefore, would lose most when a weaker rival cuts price. Yet, research shows that PRICE CUTS BY RIVALS TEND TO DRAW THE MOST SHARE NOT FROM THE POWERFUL BRANDS BUT FROM THE WEAKEST."** (Carpenter/Cooper/Hanssens/Midgley 1988, *Marketing Science*.)
4. **Ingredient brands as legal protection: "A vague phrase, such as 'heavy duty' or 'high quality,' is EASY for competitors to copy. A branded ingredient or service is NOT, BECAUSE IT IS LEGALLY PROTECTED."** [Source: KOB, ch. 9 and 6 — elaboration in `pricing.md`]

### Technology market specifics
- **"Branding the Company, Not the Product."** In the technology market with short product life cycles, the corporate brand carries the continuity that individual products cannot.
- **Pre-Market Branding for Thought Leadership.** Brand building **before** the product is available, in order to define the category and be the reference when the market emerges.
- **Positioning across the adoption cycle AND across generations.** Example **SAP** with **Vectors of Differentiation** across three generations: **1972, 1979, 1992** — each generation with a different axis of differentiation. The lesson: the positioning must move with the maturity of the technology, otherwise one positions against the state of ten years ago.
- **The architecture conflict CONSISTENCY against CHANGE.** On the IBM example: three options for an existing brand architecture under pressure to change — **MODIFY, SOFTEN, DESCRIBE.** That is: change the element, weaken it, or extend it descriptively instead of replacing it.
- **Ingredient branding in software architectures.** Example: **Microsoft's 2003 architecture with .NET as an ingredient brand** across several product lines.
- **The co-branding asymmetry, a particularly useful finding: "HP and Dell would benefit LESS from co-branding with Intel"** than smaller or less well-known manufacturers. **The rule behind it: the benefit of co-branding is inversely proportional to one's own brand strength.** Whoever is strong themselves gives away more meaning in co-branding than they gain. [Source: KOB, ch. 11]

### The eight-stage decision process in the customer's voice (HP)
**1. Aware** "I know who you are and what value you offer." **2. Choose** "I can easily find the right products and services for my needs." **3. Order** "I can get all HP products easily from one place at competitive prices." **4. Install** "I can easily integrate your solution into my business environment." **5. Learn** "You provide me with the information I need and allow me to learn." **6. Use** "Your solution is easy to use and manage within my business environment." **7. Support** "You provide me access to knowledge to quickly solve my problems." **8. Dispose/Upgrade** "It's easy for my business to grow with HP."
The associated **Circle of Customer Experience** comprises **Demonstration, Buying experience, Fulfillment, Web site, Billing, Promotions, Deployment, Usage, Upgrade, Service** — all around "Quality of Customer Experience", with the guiding rule **"CONSISTENCY ACROSS BUYING STAGES."**
**What is methodologically valuable about it: the process is formulated throughout in the first person of the customer, not in supplier functions.** That turns every stage into a testable statement that the customer can confirm or deny. [Source: KOB, ch. 11]

### The CRM four-step for business relationships
**(1) Develop and profile sub-segments. (2) Set MEASURABLE goals per sub-segment. (3) Design and implement contacts. (4) Measure results** — "one of the benefits of a CRM-based contact is **its ability to measure results.**"
Bases of segmentation: **frequency** (number of previous purchases), **socio- and firmographic** characteristics ("easy to implement"), **cluster analysis.** And the **time-out scale** as an instrument for capturing the usage situation.
Two kinds of contact: **quid-pro-quo** against **discretionary rewards** — "**they are CARROTS: if the customer does something that has been agreed on.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 8]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **The value proposition must exclude the competitor, not merely describe one's own offer.** Test: Can a competitor say the same sentence? Then it is not a value proposition. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **Before positioning, clarify who the target customer is NOT — and factor in the internal resistance to that.** According to KOB, the resistance comes not from ignorance but from the wish not to have to tell one's own sales force that some customers are worse prospects. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **With every B2B message, answer the career question as well: "What will I tell my boss?"** And the quality test on it: **the unimaginative marketer ends the sentence with "bought the cheapest one".** Whoever cannot offer a better continuation competes on price. [Source: TIM, ch. 19]
- **Check whether user and buyer are the same person.** The Dalloz case shows: if the decisive dimension lies with the user (willingness to wear), the value argument must still arrive at the buyer in his currency (compliance, accident costs). [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **With superiority strategies, define the limit of willingness to pay before the competition defines it** — otherwise the race leads beyond the customer requirement. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **With bundling strategies, factor in the loss of innovation.** Bundling protects against price comparison and at the same time weakens the effect of one's own product breakthroughs. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **Focusing costs customers and delivers margin.** TI deliberately reduced the addressable number of customers; the return was a fivefold market capitalisation. **This is the B2B version of Godin's smallest viable market — and the only place where KOB explicitly follows that logic.** [Source: KOB, ch. 9; convergence with TIM, ch. 4]
- **With co-branding, check the asymmetry: who is the stronger brand, and who therefore gains more?** [Source: KOB, ch. 11]
- **With technology brands, let the corporate brand carry, not the product** — because product life cycles are shorter than brand-building times. [Source: KOB, ch. 11]
- **In B2B, the post-purchase and service experience decides, not the pre-purchase promise.** "Particularly in the business-to-business realm, **IT'S THE POST-PURCHASE USE AND SERVICE EXPERIENCE THAT CAN MAKE OR BREAK A BRAND** (which hinges on the involvement and buy-in of internal employees), **NOT the pre-purchase promises of advertising and imagery.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 12 — elaborated in `organisation.md`]

## Typical problems and approaches to solutions

**"Our market is a commodity, we compete only on price."** Three tested ways, each with one documented case: **build and prove real, testable superiority** (Mondi — with the note that the first advantage was copied within a decade and a second had to be added); **occupy a different dimension** (Dalloz — fashion instead of protection, because compliance was the real source of value); **bundle** (TI — commodity plus software, tools and services, which forces the yardstick of comparison away from price). **Each way has a named downside: arms race, dependence on a behavioural assumption, loss of innovation.**

**"Why should we invest in a brand when our customers can check technically?"** Because what is decisive cannot be verified: **"What will be the supplier's responsiveness to any unforeseen or unanticipated problems that occur in the customer's usage?"** And because brand awareness can **survive negative experiences.** Plus the price sensitivity findings: market leaders obtain and raise prices more easily, and price cuts by competitors hit **the weakest** brands hardest. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

**"Our value proposition sounds like that of all our competitors."** The diagnosis is usually not linguistic but political: the target customer definition is missing because nobody wants to tell sales that some customers are less suitable. Without this decision every formulation stays generic. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

**"How do you bring a new category into a conservative business market?"** The worked case from TIM: **LEED certification.** At the start **only two buildings per day** were submitted; "**at that rate, it would have taken A HUNDRED YEARS to hit the numbers they hit after just TWELVE.**"
**The mechanism that accelerated it is a status dynamic among business buyers: "a RACE TO THE TOP."** So not benefit argumentation, but the possibility for builders and architects to visibly outdo one another. **This is the best TIM evidence that status mechanics in B2B work not more weakly but more measurably than in the consumer market** — because the comparison group is small and the certification is public. [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

**"Our products change too fast for brand building."** The answer is the change of level: the corporate brand carries, the product does not. Plus **pre-market branding**, to define the category before the product is available. And for architecture changes, the three options **Modify, Soften, Describe** instead of a break. [Source: KOB, ch. 11]

**"Where do we lose our customers?"** The eight-stage process in the customer's voice is a diagnostic grid for this: every stage becomes a statement the customer can confirm or deny. Experience shows that the breaks lie not at **Aware** or **Order**, but at **Install, Learn and Support** — that is, where marketing is not responsible. [Source: KOB, ch. 11]

**"How do we systematically maintain relationships with many business customers?"** The CRM four-step, with the decisive second step: **measurable goals per sub-segment.** Without it, CRM becomes undifferentiated contact frequency. And the distinction between **quid-pro-quo** (agreed consideration) and **discretionary rewards** (rewards at discretion) determines whether a relationship works transactionally or builds loyalty. [Source: KOB, ch. 8]

## Concrete examples
- **Goldman Sachs** as a superordinate association: being hired signals membership of an "elite club" — status purchase in the purest B2B context. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **Caterpillar yellow, IBM typeface, Apple start-up chime** as subordinate associations that work without words. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **Glad FreshScent, Chrysler's Hemi, Westin's Heavenly Bed** as legally protected ingredient brands against copyability. [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **The iPod effect on the Apple brand value:** 35 percent of sales, plus 24 percent brand value — a single product that revalues a corporate brand. [Source: KOB, ch. 11]
- **Boeing** as evidence of restricted architectural freedom in B2B: the model nomenclature is bound historically and by customers. [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **Tesco Clubcard** and the **Tesco Baby&Toddler Club** ("Help and offers every little step of the way") as evidence for quid-pro-quo against discretionary reward. [Source: KOB, ch. 8]
- **The Nokia design commitment as a brand consequence:** Nokia's brand historically dictated that phone design must be slim and is a point of differentiation from competitors — so brand management that binds product development. [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **Slack as a B2B lifetime value example:** an early customer could have been worth **USD 50,000 or more** if one counts the effect on colleagues, displacement of competitors and equity value. **"The first thousand customers, if they're the right people, are practically PRICELESS."** [Source: TIM, ch. 19]
- **The Google margin finding as a warning for B2B acquisition calculations:** "Google might make **A HUNDRED DOLLARS PER SALE** in profit, where you, the advertiser doing all the work, are making **JUST TEN DOLLARS.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 19]
