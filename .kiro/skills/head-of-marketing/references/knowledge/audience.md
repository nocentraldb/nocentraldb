# Audience, Empathy and Status

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Tybout/Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018.

## Character of the sources for this subject area
**TIM is clearly the denser source here** and supplies the whole toolkit: the smallest viable market, the status mechanics after Johnstone, the basic list of dreams and wants, the empathy exercises. **KOB adds two experimentally grounded building blocks**: the target component of positioning and regulatory focus (promotion versus prevention), which gives TIM's affiliation/dominion axis a second axis operationalised through framing. **TIM carries a vested interest** (selling seminars) and works with case stories without a control group; **the counter-examples (JCPenney, Stew Leonard's, HugDug) are the more reliable part**, because they are documented failures with a measurable outcome.

## Core concepts and definitions

### Empathy as the basis of the profession
"**People don't believe what you believe. They don't know what you know. They don't want what you want.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 5]
And the condensed version from the executive summary: marketers "have the empathy to know that those they seek to serve don't want what the marketer wants, don't believe what they believe, and don't care about what they care about. **They probably never will.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 2]
**Sonder** — the term for the moment "when you realize that everyone around you has an internal life as rich and as conflicted as yours". The list: everyone has noise in their head; everyone thinks they are in the right and feels wronged; everyone is afraid and at the same time knows they are lucky; everyone wants something they cannot possibly have; everyone is lonely, insecure and a bit of a fraud; and everyone cares about something. **The consequence: not doing marketing TO others, but "dance with them instead".** [Source: TIM, ch. 5]
**And the second pass:** "**We're not faking it.** Your customers aren't faking it. Those who prefer your competition aren't either." Whoever accepts that people have embraced who they have become can dance with them more easily — "not transform them, not get them to admit that they were wrong. Simply to dance with them." [Source: TIM, ch. 5]

### The myth of rational choice
The microeconomic assumption of the rational agent is "a demonstrably false assertion". The better bet: "**When in doubt, assume that people will act according to their current irrational urges, ignoring information that runs counter to their beliefs, trading long-term for short-term benefits and most of all, being influenced by the culture they identify with.**" Two errors: (1) assuming the audience is informed, rational, independent and long-term in its thinking; (2) assuming everyone is like oneself. [Source: TIM, ch. 3]
**KOB's version of the same insight:** "Buyers do not always consider all the options, weigh them carefully, and reach a deliberate choice in the conventional sense of rationality. Moreover, **buyers do not always know what they want.** Instead, individuals **learn** what they like, and they **learn how to choose** — impulsively in some cases, driven by emotion in others, and deliberately at other times." [Source: KOB, ch. 4]

### Worldviews instead of demographics
"Begin by choosing people based on **what they dream of, believe, and want**, not based on what they look like. In other words, use **psychographics instead of demographics.**" The term **worldview** comes from the cognitive linguist George Lakoff: "the shortcut, the lens each of us uses when we see the world. It's our assumptions and biases and yes, stereotypes." Evidence: "Loyal Fox News viewers have a worldview. So do fox hunters. So do people who show up at the midnight screening of The Rocky Horror Picture Show." And the caveat: "Everyone deserves to be treated as an individual, with dignity and respect for their choices. But as marketers, we must begin with a worldview, and **invite people who share that worldview to join us.** 'I made this' is a very different statement than 'What do you want?'" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
**KOB uses the same combination** in the target component: "identifying characteristics, such as **demographics and psychographics** (activities, interests, opinions)". [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

### The smallest viable market
"**The relentless pursuit of mass will make you boring**, because mass means average, it means the center of the curve, it requires you to offend no one and satisfy everyone. It will lead to compromises and generalizations. Begin instead with the smallest viable market. **What's the minimum number of people you would need to influence to make it worth the effort?**" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
And the rationale through the mechanics of culture: "**The smallest viable market makes sense because it maximizes your chances of changing a culture.** The core of your market, enriched and connected by the change you seek to make, organically shares the word with the next layer of the market." [Source: TIM, ch. 9]
**The mnemonic: "The smallest viable market is the focus that, ironically and delightfully, leads to your growth."** [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
**The thief-detector-dye metaphor:** A teaspoon of coloured powder dyes a swimming pool permanently purple; in the ocean nobody notices. "That doesn't mean you give up hope. **It means you walk away from the ocean and look for a large swimming pool.** ... Once it works, find another swimming pool." [Source: TIM, ch. 4]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### The basic list of dreams and wants
The shared vocabulary from which everyone chooses when expressing their dreams and fears: **Adventure, Affection, Avoiding new things, Belonging, Community, Control, Creativity, Delight, Freedom of expression, Freedom of movement, Friendship, Good looks, Health, Learning new things, Luxury, Nostalgia, Obedience, Participation, Peace of mind, Physical activity, Power, Reassurance, Reliability, Respect, Revenge, Romance, Safety, Security, Sex, Strength, Sympathy, Tension.**
The author's calibration: "You could probably add ten more. But it's unlikely you could add fifty more." Conclusion: "**marketers, like artists, don't need many colors to paint an original masterpiece.**"
Procedure: begin with **assertions** — claims about what the audience wants and needs, "what's on their minds when they wake up, what they talk about when no one is eavesdropping, what they remember at the end of the day". Then claims about how one's own story interacts with that. **"Don't begin with your machines, your inventory, or your tactics."** [Source: TIM, ch. 7]

### Status roles (after Keith Johnstone, "Impro")
"There's always an alpha dog in the pack. And every litter has a runt. Status roles determine who gets to eat first in the lion pack, and who gets to drink first at the oasis. In human culture, **status roles are everywhere that more than one human is present**" — on a date (who pays the bill) and in the boardroom (who arrives first, who sits where, who speaks, who decides, who is accountable). [Source: TIM, ch. 11]
**The Godfather model case:** Amerigo Bonasera, the undertaker, "a washed-out, tired, small man in a nondescript black suit", visits the Don on his daughter's wedding day, when tradition requires granting every request. He asks for violence — and **makes it worse by offering money, thereby turning "a patriarch into a hoodlum".** "**The Godfather can't grant this favor and maintain his status, and status is his lifeblood.**" Within seconds the order is restored: the undertaker bows, kisses the ring, swears loyalty. "**Bonasera relieves the tension by returning to his place in the status hierarchy.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 11]
**What status does:** It is position **and** perception of position. It protects, helps one get what one wants, gives leverage for change, **is a place to hide**, can be a gift or a burden. "**And the desire to change our status, or to protect it, drives almost everything we do.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 11]
**The observation heuristic: "If you look closely at decisions that don't initially make sense, you'll likely see status roles at work. The decision didn't make sense to you, but it made perfect sense to the person who made it."** [Source: TIM, ch. 11]
Evidence from nature: penguins in the Galápagos fish **about two hours a day** and spend the rest of the time on social grooming and social positioning. [Source: TIM, ch. 11]

### Six things about status
1. **Status is always relative.** Unlike eyesight or a bank balance, the absolute scale does not count. "6 is bigger than 4, but lower than 11. **There is no highest number.**"
2. **Status is in the eye of the beholder.** Someone seen from outside as low status, but in their own narrative as high status, is both — at different times, for different people.
3. **Status that is attended to is the status that counts.** "**it only matters when the person we're engaging with cares about status.**"
4. **Status has inertia.** "We're more likely to work to **maintain** our status (high or low) than we are to try to change it."
5. **Status is learned.** The beliefs begin early — "and yet the cohort we are with can influence our perception of our status in very little time."
6. **Shame is the status killer.** "The reason that shame is used as a lever is simple: **it works.** If we accept the shame someone sends our way, it undermines our entire narrative about relative status."
"And when you bring your work to the market, **nothing is considered before status roles.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 11]

### The two status grids
**Grid 1 — perceived status versus self status:**
- Both high: **very rare.** "the person who is able to choose, not the one who is waiting to be chosen." Example: Oprah Winfrey.
- Perceived high, self low: **more common**, because high-status people often doubt themselves. "This can turn them into divas. **Impostor syndrome lies here.**" Example: Frank Sinatra.
- Perceived low, self high: "**here you can find artistic drive and a willingness to strive for better. But over time, it can also lead to bitterness.**"
- Both low: sees themselves as unworthy, and the world agrees. "While this appears to be a sad place, **it's also a consistent one**, which is why we've embedded this role in the hierarchical culture." Examples: Cinderella before the ball; the miner who fights for a dangerous, poorly paid job.
**Grid 2 — status movement versus method.** Preliminary remark: "**Moving down creates safety because there's more room and less threat.** Fewer people jostle here for a better view or the chance to eat lunch first."
- Moving up by empowering others: the philanthropist, the committed teacher, the social-justice advocate. "This is certainly how **Superman** came to be understood by us. **He could rob banks, but he saves lives instead.**"
- Moving down by empowering others: lets others go first and does not even apply because others are more deserving.
- Moving down by putting others down: "the antisocial character who presents a noxious, infantile narcissism ... he knows he can't measure up, but he's going to take down everyone around him." Examples: O. J. Simpson, Martin Shkreli.
- Moving up by putting others down: "the hard-charging selfish person who desperately wants to win every engagement, and who is willing to do it with a combination of creating value and tearing down the competition." [Source: TIM, ch. 11]

### Affiliation versus dominion — the second status axis
Personification: "Every search on 'nicest guy in Hollywood' brings up a picture of **Tom Hanks**. And every search on 'The Godfather' brings us a picture of the fictional **Don Corleone**."
**Affiliation questions:** Who knows you? Who trusts you? Have you made things better? What is your circle like? Where do you stand within the tribe? Can't we all get along?
**Dominion statements:** This is mine, not yours. Who has more power? I did this myself. My family needs more of what we already have. "**My side dominating your side means I don't have to be in charge, as long as my leader is winning.**"
**Context dependence — decisive for application:** On the playing field, a twelve-year-old may care only about winning and beating the opponent; **the same boy doesn't care at all about being top of the class, but cares a lot about who sits next to him on the bus.** In the jazz band one player counts his solos while another wants to keep the group in time. **Test question: "The people you're seeking to serve in this moment: What are they measuring?"**
**The mnemonic: "Dominion is a vertical experience, above or below. Affiliation is a horizontal one: Who's standing next to me?"**
**And the jurisdiction: "Affiliation or dominance is up to the customer, not you. ... their worldview is always stronger than the story you choose to tell."**
Signalling practice: **Uber built its brand on dominance** — the first years of rollout were marked by contentious relationships with municipalities, competitors and drivers, and "this signaling aligned with the view of some investors, employees, and users". Affiliation signals, by contrast: "How busy is the trade show booth? Who else is at the launch party? Who blurbed the book? Are 'people' talking about it?" And: "**Affiliation isn't as focused on scarcity as dominion is, because affiliation admires the network effect. More affiliation leads to affiliation for everyone involved. Abundance is welcome.**" Concrete levers: for an investment bank, the tombstone ads with the names of all the "right" firms; for a B2B salesperson, references; for a local tradesman, "hunkering down in a single neighborhood until a reputation is assured". [Source: TIM, ch. 11]

### Regulatory focus — the experimentally grounded complement
**Promotion focus:** orientation towards achievement, growth, aspirations; goals are pursued **with eagerness**; lens: what one **gains or does not gain**.
**Prevention focus:** orientation towards safety, security, responsibility; goals are pursued **with caution and vigilance**; lens: what one **loses or does not lose**. [Source: KOB, ch. 7; origin Higgins 1997]
**The framing rule that follows:** "A promotion message is more persuasive when it emphasizes **gain** (eat right and get energized) than when it emphasizes **nongain** (you won't get energized if you don't eat right), and a prevention message is more persuasive when it emphasizes **loss** (you will have clogged arteries if you don't eat right) than when it emphasizes **nonloss**."
**The application error KOB names:** In tampon advertising, one execution shows women swimming and cycling during their period (promotion), another shows the accidents avoided (prevention). "**Often such executions are aired as part of the same campaign. This practice glosses over an important distinction.**"
**The competitive evidence:** Clorox positions itself on the purest purity through brilliant whiteness (promotion), OxiClean on cleaning without harshness to clothes (prevention). "How the two brands perform depends on how well their message resonates with the regulatory focus of their target audience."
**Three sources of focus:** individual disposition; **cultural orientation** ("people from a western culture are more likely to be promotion-focused, whereas those from an eastern culture are more likely to be prevention-focused"); and environmental cues. Plus context dependence: "consumers shopping for **home-security systems** are inherently prevention-focused, but they may be promotion-focused when shopping for **flat-screen televisions**." And over time: "with the economic decline in 2000, the environment for investors changed from one where growth and achievement were the prevailing goals to one where **financial safety and security was paramount**."
**The follow-on rule on level of abstraction:** Promotion-focused people have a more abstract perspective (**why** something is done), prevention-focused people a more concrete one (**how**). Advertising should accordingly emphasise the why or the how. [Source: KOB, ch. 7]
**Connection to TIM:** Both grids are two-axis, context-dependent rather than fixed to the person, and both say that the customer's worldview counts. **KOB's version is operationalised through framing and experimentally supported; TIM's version is broader in cultural and status terms.**

### Aspirations as a message rule
"People are inferential, and they make judgments about advertising based on their **aspirations**, not on literal information. ... consumers **infer meaning beyond what is literally presented**."
Evidence: "**older consumers are typically more persuaded by advertising that depicts younger people, and younger children are often persuaded by advertising showing older children.**" And the Hallmark case: In ads aimed at men in their twenties, **pre-teen boys** were shown brooding over how best to ask someone on a date — "this reminds the 20-something men how it felt to be younger and just starting to date (without making the ad so emotional that it would make them uncomfortable)." [Source: KOB, ch. 7]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **The resource rule (adoption curve).** In any group of 100 people, on almost any measure, **about 68 are near the average, 27 are clearly further away, 4 are extreme outliers.** The **fifteen or sixteen on the left-hand side** are the **neophiliacs** — they want the better, smarter, more innovative, stand in line at film premieres, update their operating system immediately and **read Vogue for the ads**. An equally large group on the right defends the status quo to the last: "**They still read Reader's Digest and use a VCR.**"
  **The rule: "Good marketers have the humility to understand that you shouldn't waste a minute (not of your time or of their time) on anyone who isn't on the left part of the curve. ... It's not for them. Not right now."** One comes to them later — "horizontally. Person to person. Through earned media. **But not right now.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 14; source for the curve: Everett Rogers]
- **Early adopters are not adapters.** Adapters come to terms with change unhappily. Neophiliacs are addicted to the new: the thrill of discovery, the enjoyment of the tension "this might not work", boasting about finds. **"The neophiliacs are very forgiving of missteps from those who seek to innovate with them, and incredibly unforgiving after the initial thrill of discovery wears off."** And: "**You can't be perfect in the eyes of an early adopter; the best you can do is be interesting.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 5]
- **Different people want different things** — the catalogue of motives by type: neophiliacs want to go first, hope, possibility and magic, the thrill of it working **and** the risk of it failing, and the pleasure of showing off their innovation. **The typical corporate cog wants to avoid trouble with the boss — and if there is trouble, a watertight alibi.** The social fighter wants a glimmer of hope. Whoever measures dominion wants to win — "and if he can't win, he might be willing to settle for watching his opponent lose". The affiliation-seeking tribe member wants to fit in and keep time, **without the risk of being elected leader.** Some want a bargain, "while a few eagerly want to **overpay**, to prove that they can". **"Almost no one wants to feel stupid."** [Source: TIM, ch. 14]
- **Important correction of a widespread assumption:** "**It's a mistake to believe that everyone wants to make their status higher. In fact, few people do.** It's also a mistake to believe that no one wants to make their status **lower**." Someone conditioned to see themselves in a particular role may fight to keep their status **or even to lower it.** [Source: TIM, ch. 11]
- **Status is not wealth.** "In some circles, a Pulitzer-winning columnist has far more status than I do. A doctor in charge of a prestigious hospital might have more status than a wealthy plastic surgeon. And **the penniless yogi in a little village in India has more status than the richest man in town**, at least to some of his peers." Diagnosis of the times: "In the last few decades, we've gotten **lazier in our nuance** of awarding status, preferring it to be related to either the dollars in a bank account or the number of followers online." [Source: TIM, ch. 11]
- **The superuser calculation against the equal-treatment reflex.** Restaurants hang a photo of the critic in the kitchen; today everyone is a critic (Yelp), and the obvious conclusion would be to treat **everyone** better. "**The math here doesn't hold up. Treating everyone better is a bit like treating everyone worse — given your resources, you can't treat everyone better than you already are.**" The insight that holds: **everyone has a platform, but not everyone uses it.** Rule for action: "when you find someone who is adopting your cause, **adopt them back**. When you find someone who is eager to talk about what you do, **give him something to talk about.** When you find someone who is itching to become a generous leader, **give her the resources to lead.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 14]
- **The truth about customer contribution.** Marketing costs money (suit, shop premises, software, inventory, ads, PR) — **all fixed costs, spread across the whole customer base.** In the book's contribution chart, **only eight of about thirty customers contribute any profit at all.** Scope: book buyers, restaurant guests, political donors, philanthropists, stamp collectors "and just about any industry where some customers spend more than others". **The answer to "Who's it for?" must therefore be: "The kind of customers who are going to show up for us in a way that lets us keep going."** And: "**You'll serve many people. You'll profit from a few. The whales pay for the minnows.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 14]
- **The customer-service call as a touchstone.** The valuable customer arithmetically generates **eight times the average revenue**. For a freelancer with six clients that would be trivial — "when the big client calls, the freelancer instantly knows what's up". But in banks and retail, **"the least-respected and lowest-paid person"** answers the phone. Alternative to the script ("as stated", "our policy"): "Get in your car, drive across town, and show up. ... Have the CEO pick up the phone and call that customer that you accidentally triple-charged." **"I know you can't do it for every customer. But you can learn to see and act accordingly."** [Source: TIM, ch. 14]
- **The hardest empathy exercise: "Why don't people choose you?"** Those who don't buy, who mock the innovation and happily buy from the competition — **why are THEY right?** The sentence to fill in: "For people who want what you want (___) and believe what you believe (___), your choice of ___ is exactly correct." **Because it is.** For a career coach this means explaining why people without a coach have decided wisely. [Source: TIM, ch. 8]
- **Ask for advice instead of feedback.** "When we seek feedback, we're doing something brave and foolish. We're asking to be proven wrong." Better: "**I made something that I like, that I thought you'd like. How'd I do? What advice do you have for how I could make it fit your worldview more closely?**" Such advice "reveals a lot about the person you're engaging with. It helps us see his or her fears and dreams and wants." **Warning sign:** When people share negative stories, they often generalise ("nobody", "everyone") — what one is actually hearing is **one specific sore spot**. Examples: the one-star review because the book arrived too late for the baby shower; the angry customer because the wedding cost more than planned. [Source: TIM, ch. 8]
- **One-word transformation:** instead of prospects and customers -> **students.** "Where are your students? What will they benefit from learning? Are they open to being taught? What will they tell others?" Explicitly **not** the teacher-pupil relationship of exams and obedience, but pupil-mentor: **enrollment, choice, care.** [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
- **Enrollment as a condition of access:** "**There's no such thing as mandatory education. It's almost impossible to teach people against their will.**" Image: "Enrollment is hands raised, eyes on the board, notes being taken." And: "**Lazy marketers try to buy enrollment with flashy ads. The best marketers earn enrollment by seeking people who want the change being offered** — and they do it by connecting people to others who want the change as well." [Source: TIM, ch. 14]

## Typical problem statements and approaches

**"We don't know who it is for."** Tool: personas as shorthand. **Bargain Bill** (bargain hunting as a sport, wrestling with his own money narrative), **Hurried Henry** (always a shortcut, never waits, reads or thinks on a business trip), **Careful Karla** (distrusts the taxi driver, sure the receptionist will rip her off, never touches the minibar). "**Everyone has a problem, a desire, and a narrative.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]

**"We design for our own worldview."** The documented case: **Ron Johnson, as CEO of JCPenney in 2011, ended the permanent discounts and special sales**, because his worldview (previously Apple Retail, luxury, elegance, quiet respect) did not square a quality retailer with permanent clearance sales. **Result: sales collapsed by over 50 percent.** "He abandoned Penney's true fans: people who **loved the sport of bargain hunting**. Or the urgency. **Penney's customers were playing a game, one that made them feel like they were winning.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
The counter-test from the same logic: **dog food.** Americans spent 24 billion USD, with exploding prices and gourmet ingredients (sweet potato, elk, free-range bison). "**And yet, I've never seen a dog buy dog food.**" Whether dogs prefer it nobody knows — what is certain is that the owners prefer it: for the feeling of caring for a loyally responsive animal, for the status of the luxury purchase, for the generosity of sharing. Some want gluten-free dog food, "loaded with high-value placebos". **The generalised lesson: "there's almost always a disconnect between performance and appeal. The engineer's choice of the best price/performance combination is rarely the market's choice."** Two voices in the head: the dog voice (few words, knows what it wants) and the owner voice (nuanced, contradictory, complex, easily distracted). [Source: TIM, ch. 5]

**"Why don't they act rationally?"** Evidence for status through uniqueness: **Roland Imhoff's study (Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz)** on conspiracy theorists. Many who believe Lady Diana is still alive believe **at the same time** that she was murdered. Imhoff: "Adherence to conspiracy theory might not always be the result of some perceived lack of control, but rather **a deep-seated need for uniqueness.**" **The decisive experiment:** invented facts about a smoke-detector conspiracy in Germany. When participants heard that **81 percent** of the population believed the theory, they were markedly less interested than when they heard that only **19 percent** supported it. "They seek to be part of a small group, a minority group, an outspoken group. ... **That's not that big a leap from the countless micro-tribes that so many early adopters belong to.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 5]

**"They don't understand the price."** Empathy reversal in the fundraising case: The fundraiser fails because she thinks "I would never donate a million". But the donation is for the **donor**, who tells himself: "This million-dollar donation is a bargain. I'm going to get at least two million dollars' worth of joy, status, and satisfaction." **"Everything that we purchase — every investment, every trinket, every experience — is a bargain. That's why we bought it."** The radical conclusion: "**if you're unwilling to have empathy for the narrative of the person you seek to serve, you're stealing** — because you're withholding a valuable option." And the counterpole: "If they understand what's on offer and choose not to buy it, then it's not for them. Not today, not at this price, not with that structure. **That's okay too.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 5]

**"What do customers want?" is the wrong question.** "If you ask them, you probably won't find what you're looking for. You certainly won't find a breakthrough. **The crowd didn't invent the Model T, the smartphone, or rap.** The crowd didn't invent JetBlue, City Bakery, or charity: water either. Crowdfunding is one thing, but the crowd isn't that good at inventing a breakthrough."
**Three confusions:** (1) Confusing wants and needs — what is needed is air, water, health, a roof; "if we're privileged enough, we decide that those other things we want are actually needs". (2) "people are intimately aware of their wants ... but they are **absolutely terrible at inventing new ways to address those wants**. They often prefer to use a familiar solution to satisfy their wants, even if it's not working very well." (3) Believing that everyone wants the same thing. [Source: TIM, ch. 7]

**"The product is not what is wanted."** The drill-bit riff, starting from Theodore Levitt ("People don't want to buy a quarter-inch drill bit. They want a quarter-inch hole"): "**But that doesn't go nearly far enough. No one wants a hole.**" They want the shelf, then the feeling of being organised, then self-efficacy, then the status gain from the partner's recognition, then peace of mind. Final version: "**People don't want to buy a quarter-inch drill bit. They want to feel safe and respected.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 3]
**The parsimony observation on this: "there aren't that many feelings to choose from. In essence, most marketers deliver the same feelings. We just do it in different ways, with different services, products, and stories. And we do it for different people in different moments."** [Source: TIM, ch. 3]
**The scope limit from KOB:** For some products no emotional bond arises (light bulbs stay functional), for others only the emotional level is appropriate (luxury, fragrance). See `positioning`, laddering limit. [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

**"What is a car for?"** A teenager's first car is not a transport need — at fifteen he had no transport problem, and many get through college without a car. **For the teenager:** the shift from dependent child to independent adult, "a shift in status, in perception, and in power. **It's far bigger than four wheels.**" **For the parents:** the shift from rule to granted freedom and responsibility, with conversations about safety, control and status — "**What will the neighbors say?**" "When the designer, the marketer, and the salesperson see these changes at work, they provide more value, because they can design with these issues in mind." [Source: TIM, ch. 5]

**"We are afraid of playing on status."** Justified: "**When we bring status into our marketing, we are walking on very thin ice.** We don't know if the person we're engaging with **appears** to have high status (and doesn't believe it) or actually believes and wants to increase his standing." Evidence **Frank Sinatra** (after Gay Talese): The outside world saw him at his zenith, as a high-status power broker; "**when Frank looked in the mirror, though, he saw a low-status skinny kid, disrespected, barely holding on to what he had.**" He surrounded himself with yes-men, but had self-sabotaging tantrums and a miserable life. **And the inevitability: "But it's not clear that we have much choice ... because every big decision is made based on our perceptions of status."** [Source: TIM, ch. 11] **The ethical limit lies in `change` (tension versus fear) and in the ethics passage of TIM ch. 23.**

## Concrete examples
- **VisionSpring (India), the central evidence of the book.** Reading glasses, production cost ~2 USD, sold for ~3 USD; the difference finances shipping, local sellers, growth. Starting position in the village: age -> need; no glasses worn -> none owned; rupees visible in shirt pockets -> money available; the eye test worked independently of language and literacy; the glasses work immediately and are not a mistrusted technology. **Even so only a third bought.** The one change: **take all the glasses off the table** and, after the person has put them on, say: "Here are your new glasses. If they work and you like them, please pay us three dollars. **If you don't want them, please give them back.**" **Effect: the share doubled.** Mechanics: rebuilding the story from "opportunity to shop and to own from start to finish" to "shall we take away what you have?" — **desire for gain versus avoidance of loss.** The interpretation: Shopping is a risk that only someone who loses nothing by being wrong can take. "**My narrative is simply my narrative, and if it's not working, it's arrogant to insist on it.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 3]
- **Union Square Cafe (Danny Meyer):** Capacity 600 guests a day. "If you can only delight six hundred people, the best way to begin is by **choosing which six hundred people.**" The magic did not lie in the property (bad neighbourhood at opening) or in the star chef (there wasn't one), but "in the guts it took to **carefully curate the customers**. Choose the people you serve, choose your future." [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
- **"Winner take all" rarely is:** In a congressional primary only **20,000** people voted, so **5,000** decide victory or defeat — among **724,000** district residents, i.e. under 1 percent. "There's a very big difference between five thousand and 'everyone.'" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
- **Kevin Kelly's "1,000 true fans" (2008, founding editor of Wired), verbatim:** "A true fan is defined as a fan that will buy anything you produce. These diehard fans will drive two hundred miles to see you sing; they will buy the hardback and paperback and Audible versions of your book ... If you have roughly a thousand true fans like this (also known as super fans), you can make a living — **if you are content to make a living but not a fortune.**" Godin's sharpening: "**They bend themselves into a pretzel trying to please the anonymous masses before they have fifty or one hundred people who would miss them if they were gone.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 8]
- **The By-the-Way-Bakery wording as a pattern for a change statement:** "We want to make sure no one is left out. By offering people gluten-free, dairy-free, and kosher baked goods that happen to be delicious, we let the entire community be part of special family occasions. **We change hosts from exclusive to inclusive, and guests from outsiders to insiders.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
- **Status analysis of slogans:** "They laughed when I sat down at the piano" = status. "Roll Tide!" = dominance. "Choosy mothers choose Jif" = status and respect. "I pledge allegiance" = belonging. "The Earth needs a good lawyer" = affiliation and justice. [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
- **Pro wrestling as a teaching model:** "What is pro wrestling, really, but a battle for status? It's not just among the wrestlers, but also among the fans. **Because when your hero moves up, so do you.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 11]
