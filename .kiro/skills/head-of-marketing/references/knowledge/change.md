# Change, Tension and Culture

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Tybout/Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018.

## Character of the sources for this subject area
**TIM is almost the sole source here for the mechanics** (tension, pattern swap, "people like us do things like this", tribal leadership, ethics). **KOB supplies two theoretical foundations**: the learning theory of buyer behaviour (ch. 4) and the ritual domains of consumer culture (ch. 3). **The one-sidedness is correct here**, because KOB treats behavioural change as a by-product of positioning and advertising, not as a subject in its own right. **TIM carries a vested interest** (selling seminars); the strongest tools of this area (creating tension, playing on status, shame as a lever, Alinsky, the NRA as a model of method) can be applied manipulatively without the accompanying ethics — the two belong together.

## Core concepts and definitions

### Marketing as change work
"Marketing is the generous act of helping someone solve a problem. **Their** problem." And: "**Most of all, marketing is change.** Change the culture, change your world. Marketers make change happen." [Source: TIM, ch. 1 and framing section]
"Marketing is the act of making change happen. **Making is insufficient. You haven't made an impact until you've changed someone.**" Three examples: changed the boss's opinion, changed the school system, changed the demand for the product. [Source: TIM, framing section]
**The scope: "Marketing isn't just selling soap."** Giving a TED talk, asking the boss for a raise, raising money for the playground — all marketing. "For a long time, during the days when marketing and advertising were the same thing, marketing was reserved for vice presidents with a budget. **And now it's for you.**" [Source: TIM, framing section]
**The diagnostic list ("How to know if you have a marketing problem"):** You are not busy enough; your ideas do not spread; the community is not what it could be; the people who matter to you do not reach everything; the politician needs votes; the work is not fulfilling; the customers are frustrated. **"If you see a way to make things better, you now have a marketing problem."** [Source: TIM, framing section]

### The basic limit: internal narratives
"**Everyone always acts in accordance with their internal narratives. You can't get someone to do something that they don't want to do**, and most of the time, what people want to do is take action (or not take action) that **reinforces** their internal narratives." Hence: "As marketers and agents of change, we almost always **overrate** our ability to make change happen." [Source: TIM, ch. 9]
**The two drivers of behavioural change:** the wish to belong ("people like us do things like this") and the perception of one's own status (affiliation and dominion). **"Since both these forces often push us to stay as we are, it takes tension to change them."** [Source: TIM, ch. 9]

### "People like us do things like this"
The crickets evidence: Crickets are a good source of protein in many places; beef is an easily addressable cause of global warming and an inefficient way to feed the world. It is not genetic and there are no clear rational reasons. "**Because people like us eat things like this.**"
Example sentences of the inner narrative: "People like me don't cheat on their taxes. People like me own a car; we don't take the bus. People like me have a full-time job."
**Even the outsider takes their bearings:** "Even when we adopt the behavior of an outlier ... we're still aligning ourselves with the behavior of outliers. **A sociopath might do things in opposition to the crowd, but he's not unaware of the crowd.**"
**The limit on reach: "We can't change the culture, but each of us has the opportunity to change A culture — our little pocket of the world."**
**The cycle: "Normalization creates culture, and culture drives our choices, which leads to more normalization."** And the task: "Marketers don't make average stuff for average people. Marketers make change. And they do it by **normalizing new behaviors.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 9]

### The end of mass culture
In the past, mass media worked at defining "us" as "all of us", and came close: "We (mostly) all watched Johnny Carson and we (mostly) all wore jeans and we (mostly) all went to school."
**Today: "Mad Men, which was hyped by the New York Times in dozens of articles in just one season, was only regularly seen by 1 percent of the U.S. population."** The Cronut, the deep-fried Oreo at the fairground, the raw Moon Pie in the quirky restaurant reach "basically no one".
**"We've gone from all of us being everyone to all of us being no one."** But: "that's okay, because the long tail of culture and the media and change doesn't need everyone any longer. **It's happy with enough.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 9]
**The first task that follows:** "What the marketer, the leader, and the organizer must do as their first job is simple: **define 'us'.**" And the trap: "The right answer is **not** 'The people who give are people like us.' **That's backwards.**" What is needed is more courage: not just reaching the markets but changing their expectations — "and most of all, changing **what they choose to tell and show each other.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 9]
**The two hard working steps once one wants to change "a culture":** (1) map and understand the worldview of the culture to be changed. (2) focus all energy on this group, **ignore all others**, and build and live a story that lands with this culture. [Source: TIM, ch. 9]

### Elite versus exclusive — the most consequential distinction
**Elite is an EXTERNAL measure:** "Does the world you care about respect this badge?" The Rhodes scholarship is elite (goes to few, is respected by elite persons and institutions), **but not exclusive** — "it's not a tribe, a group of well-connected individuals with their own culture."
**Exclusive is an INTERNAL measure:** "It's us versus them, insiders versus outsiders." The Hell's Angels are not elite but exclusive. Harvard Business School is both, as are the Navy SEALs.
**The instruction for action: "it's EXCLUSIVE institutions that change things. We have no control over our elite status, and it can be taken away in an instant. But exclusive organizations thrive as long as their members wish to belong, and that work is something we CAN control."**
The core: "every member is 'people like us'. **Sign up for that and you gain status. Walk away and you lose it.**" And the conclusion for the beginning: "In order to change a culture, we begin with an exclusive cohort. **That's where we can offer the most tension and create the most useful connections.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 9]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### Pattern match versus pattern interrupt
**Pattern match** is business as usual: If the offer fits the story, the pace, cost and risk, it is an easy decision. Evidence: the family with the endless parade of breakfast cereals (Cocoa Krispies -> Lucky Charms -> Frosted Flakes, depending on offer or promo). A new cereal brand is a match: "**Sure, why not?**"
**Pattern interrupt** needs a jolt; tension arises, energy is redirected. "**Most of the time, for most of those you seek to reach, the answer is no** — because the patterns are established, time is precious, and risk is something to be feared." Examples: getting someone who has never hired a gardener to do so; asking a rich person who habitually gives 100 dollars for 5,000 dollars.
**The strategic consequence with high practical value:** "When life interferes, new patterns are established. **This is why it's so profitable to market to new dads, engaged women, and people who have recently moved. They don't have a pattern to match, so it's all an interrupt.**" Conversely, the purchasing manager has been taught that pattern matching is the way to a quiet job. And: "**The best time to market a new app is when the platform is brand new. When you market to someone who doesn't have a pattern yet, you don't have to persuade them that their old choices were mistakes.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 10]

### Tension — and its distinction from fear
"**Tension is the force on a stretched rubber band.** Pull it at one end and it creates tension at every point."
Illustrative example: Why do some hesitate to ask questions in class but answer gladly when called on? **Voluntariness is the problem, because it demands agency and responsibility.** If the teacher applies focused social tension by calling on someone publicly, the student answers without difficulty — "the tension was sufficient to overcome his or her inertia."
**THE ETHICAL LIMIT: "If you feel like you're coercing people, manipulating them or causing them to be afraid, you're probably doing it wrong."** And the distinction: "**Fear's a dream killer.** It puts people into suspended animation, holding their breath, paralyzed and unable to move forward. Fear alone isn't going to help you make change happen. **Tension might, though.** ... There might be fear, but **tension is the promise that we can get through that fear to the other side.**"
**Tension as a mark of good education:** "All effective education creates tension, because just before you learn something, you're aware you don't know it (yet)." As adults we voluntarily expose ourselves to the tension of a jazz concert, a baseball game or a thriller — "but, mostly because we've been indoctrinated by fear, we hesitate when we have the opportunity to learn something new."
**The entitlement: "If you care enough about the change you seek to make, you will care enough to GENEROUSLY AND RESPECTFULLY create tension on behalf of that change."** [Source: TIM, ch. 10]

### "What are you breaking?"
Every launch breaks something; "the very existence of an alternative causes something else to no longer be true." Evidence: The second hotel at Niagara Falls devalues the first as the only one; the telephone means the telegraph is no longer the fastest way; an exclusive party turns the uninvited into outsiders; a new extreme devalues the outdone one for its fans. "**This is what tension feels like. The tension of being left behind.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 10]
**The Las Vegas evidence:** When glitzy casinos were built, visitors who had been happy in Reno or downtown Las Vegas a year earlier felt like second-class citizens and asked: "**Am I the sort of person who goes to a casino this run-down?**" The mere existence of the fancier alternative demoted their experience at the former favourite place. [Source: TIM, ch. 10]
**The Tesla evidence in pure form:** At the launch of the Model S, the primary function was to tell a story that, for luxury-car neophiliacs, **broke their current car** — no longer fun to own, no longer worth bragging about, no longer proof of status. "This luxury car owner went to sleep the night before, delighted that the car in the garage was shiny, new, and state of the art. **And then he or she woke up to discover that the story was no longer true.**"
**The core observation: "Tesla understood that no one who bought one of the first fifty thousand Teslas actually NEEDED a car. They all had perfectly fine cars."** So Musk created a car "that undid their status as early adopters and as tech geeks and as environmentalists and as those that supported audacity. **All at once.**"
**Why the incumbents could not: "Not because they didn't know how (they did) and not because they didn't have the resources (they did). No, Ford and GM and Toyota didn't launch the Tesla because CAR COMPANIES LIKE US DON'T TAKE RISKS LIKE THIS. And their customers felt the same way."**
And the diagnosis of the times: "This audacity is available to more and more organizations as technology shifts from **'Could it be done?' to 'Do we have the guts?'**" [Source: TIM, ch. 21]

### How the status quo became the status quo
"The dominant narrative, the market share leader, the policies and procedures that rule the day — they all exist for a reason. **They're good at resisting efforts by insurgents like you.**"
**The core statement: "If all it took to upend the status quo was the truth, we would have changed a long time ago. If all we were waiting for was a better idea, a simpler solution, or a more efficient procedure, we would have shifted away a year or a decade or a century ago. THE STATUS QUO DOESN'T SHIFT BECAUSE YOU'RE RIGHT. IT SHIFTS BECAUSE THE CULTURE CHANGES. AND THE ENGINE OF CULTURE IS STATUS."** [Source: TIM, ch. 10]

### The theoretical foundation from KOB: strategies create the learning
"In the traditional view, strategies **respond** to what buyers want. But when buyers learn, **the strategies CREATE the experiences and observations on which buyer learning is based.** ... And by shaping what buyers learn, organizations **shape the rules of the competitive game.** Rather than a race to meet buyer needs, **competition becomes a battle over the rules of the game.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 4]
The mechanism in detail: Before the first purchase, preferences are weakly formed. The buyer tries and **"attributes a successful trial to the unique attribute combination"** of the product. "In doing so, buyers develop a **naive theory** relating brand features to value, which advertising and repeat purchase reinforce." [Source: KOB, ch. 4]
**This is the learning-theory version of TIM's normalisation thesis** — the same statement, once in cultural-sociological and normative terms, once with market-share data.

### Brands as secular ritual (the cultural-anthropological foundation)
"**Brand-based behaviors are the principal forms of secular ritual in contemporary social life.**" Three ritual domains, each with its own management orientation:
- **Fetish:** "As a nation of unchurched seekers for whom denominational religion has become increasingly unsatisfying, and yet for whom the idea of a spiritual quest continues to provide direction to life, Americans have elevated the brand to the status of fetish." The brand becomes "a portal to exalted experience".
- **Totem:** "the brand performs the crucial social function of symbolic classification. **It acts as both a beacon and a badge, a dashboard and a billboard.**" Image: the Kwakiutl totem poles, whose figures embody not only the identity of the clans but **their relationship to one another** — "now imagine those figures replaced by the swoosh, the helios, the mermaid, the bull's-eye or the bull." Evidence of embodiment: Enthusiasts tattoo themselves with the logos of Harley Davidson, Gibson Guitars and Apple.
- **Kinship alliance:** "consumers imagine brands existing on a continuum from intimacy to estrangement, from kinship to enmity; brands may be consanguines, affines, friends, strangers, or adversaries." And the demographic analogy: "**Erosion of brand loyalty in the United States corresponds to the pattern of SERIAL MONOGAMY that is the dominant marital profile of the day.** The demographer-identified trend of starter marriages — **25 percent of first marriages terminating within five years without children** — portends further brand loyalty adjustments." [Source: KOB, ch. 3]
**The human traits as an ordering grid:** **Homo faber** (making things, graduated, from when tool use was discovered in the animal kingdom), **Homo narrans** ("Storytelling is now regarded as our signature talent"), **Homo ludens** ("marketers must **build space into their offerings** within which consumers can create, innovate, and deviate"), **Homo quaerens** ("the quest for meaning is preeminent"). [Source: KOB, ch. 3]

### Marshall Ganz: Story of Self, Us, Now
Ganz (Harvard, worked with **Cesar Chavez and Barack Obama**) and his three-part narrative of action:
- **Story of self** gives standing, a platform to speak from. Explicitly **not** catastrophising or "the faux empathy of online vulnerability". But: "**your chance to explain that you are people like us. That you did things like this. That your actions led to a change, one we can hear and see and understand.**"
- **Story of us** is the core of a tribe: Why are we the same? Why should we care? "**about together, not apart.**"
- **Story of now** is the critical pivot: "**The story of now enlists the tribe on your journey. It's the peer opportunity/peer pressure of the tribe that will provide the tension for all of us to move forward, together.**"
Short version: "I was like you. I was in the desert. Then I learned something and now I'm here. Of course, I am not alone. ... Together, we can make this better. **But if we hesitate, or if we leave the others behind, it won't work.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 20]

### Alinsky reversed — the ethical counter-construction
Starting point: Saul Alinsky's "Rules for Radicals", thirteen principles for zero-sum political settings, including "Power is not only what you have but what the enemy thinks you have", "**Ridicule is man's most potent weapon**", "Keep the pressure on", "**The threat is usually more terrifying than the thing itself**", "**Pick the target, freeze it, personalize it, and polarize it**".
**Godin's verdict: "this approach is now often used by both sides on just about any issue, and it tears away at civil discourse. When you're so sure you're right that you're willing to burn things down, it turns out that EVERYONE IS STANDING IN A BURNING BUILDING sooner or later."**
**The thirteen reversed rules:** "Put people to work. It's even more effective than money." / "Challenge your people to explore, to learn, and to get comfortable with uncertainty." / "Find ways to help others on the path find firm footing." / "Help others write rules that allow them to achieve their goals." / "Treat the others the way you'd want to be treated." / "**Don't criticize for fun. Do it when it helps educate, even if it's not entertaining.**" / "**Stick with your tactics long after everyone else is bored with them. Only stop when they stop working.**" / "It's okay to let the pressure cease now and then." / "**Don't make threats. Do or don't do.**" / "Build a team with the capacity and the patience to do the work that needs doing." / "If you bring your positive ideas to the fore, again and again, you'll raise the bar for everyone else." / "Solve your own problems before you spend a lot of time finding problems for the others." / "**Celebrate your people, free them to do even more, make it about the cohort, and invite everyone along. Disagree with institutions, not with people.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 20]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **Two stumbles in formulating the intended change.** **Stumble 1:** choosing the grandiose, almost impossible change. "That's a heavy burden, as well as **a convenient excuse in moments of despair.** It's no wonder that you're stuck — you're seeking to do the impossible." Better: begin with a hurdle one can jump, then replicate. **Stumble 2:** defending what has already been sold and constructing a "change" backwards to fit it, loaded with buzzwords ("Activation and engagement for TNT's new thriller that makes a meta-statement about viewer identity. **Really?**"). [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
- **"It's not your tribe."** The tribe does not belong to you; you must not tell the members what to do or use them for your own purposes. "**The tribe would probably survive if you went away. The goal is for them to miss you if you did.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 20]
  **KOB's parallel:** "Illicit lurking in chatrooms, flying false flags on bulletin boards, and other unwelcome interaction from the firm can be viewed as **a hostile takeover by the brand of OUR brand, a co-optation of community by corporation that subverts the moral authority the brand desires to tap.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 3] **Two authors from completely different traditions arrive at the same rule — that makes the finding stronger than either source alone.**
- **The half-life rule against letting go.** There is the hope of kicking off a movement and then getting out of the way. "**In fact, that rarely happens.**" The early adopters "are on the prowl, and they'll be the first to leave"; but the status-quo admirers also leave as soon as the tension is gone, because the original status quo persists.
  **"There's a half-life at work. For any tribal behavior that's not energetically maintained, HALF OF THE ACTIVITY WILL DISAPPEAR. Every day, every month, every year — it's not clear what the half-life for a given movement is, but you can expect that it will fade."**
  The alternative: reinvest, "to have the guts to sit with those you have instead of always being distracted to chase the next thing". **Mnemonic: "The best marketers are farmers, not hunters. Plant, tend, plow, fertilize, weed, repeat. Let someone else race around after shiny objects."** [Source: TIM, ch. 20]
- **Creating cultural artefacts on purpose — and holding back while doing so.** Use status roles to raise a costume, code words or the secret handshake; "you can be Betsy Ross and sew the flag". But: "**Don't say it all, and don't make it obvious. It's fine that there are secret handshakes, Easter eggs, and unknown features. It's fine that commitment and longevity earn an extra edge.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 20]
- **Commitment economics:** "**Most of all, the tribe is waiting for you to commit.** They know that most marketers are fly-by-night operators, knocking on doors and moving on. But some hunker down and commit. And in return, the tribe commits to them. **Because once you're part of a tribe, your success is their success.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 20]
- **"Just enough art" — the jump-distance rule.** When JetBlue launched, it simply had to be hipper than American and Delta; when Virgin America launched six years later, it had to be hipper than JetBlue — "**that's a different hurdle. The bar had been raised.**" Analogy in photography: It is technically easy to make the pictures of **yesterday**; establishing the next phase takes a leap, "just a bit better and a bit unexpected. **Leap too far, though, and the tribe won't follow.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 9]
- **The way out of perfectionism: "good enough".** "**Perfect closes the door.** It asserts that we're done. Worse, **perfect forbids us to try. To seek perfection and not reach it is a failure.**" The causal chain: "Good enough leads to engagement. Engagement leads to trust. Trust gives us a chance to see. And seeing allows us to learn. Learning allows us to make a promise. And a promise might earn enrollment. **And enrollment is precisely what we need to achieve better.** Ship your work. It's good enough. Then make it better." [Source: TIM, ch. 22]
- **Permission to abandon — as a counterweight to the persistence rule:** "It's entirely possible that the thing you are marketing satisfies no real demand, there is no good strategy behind it, and that you are being selfish in thinking that just because you built it you should stick with it. **Blow it up. Start over.**" And: in case of failure "all it means is that you've ruled out one more route in your quest to matter." [Source: TIM, ch. 23 and ch. 12]
  **OPEN POINT:** Elsewhere TIM demands extreme persistence (ch. 15: "change them when your accountant is tired of them"; ch. 20: "stick with your tactics long after everyone else is bored"; Ziglar, who stays in a town for weeks). **The book supplies no criterion for when persistence turns into stubbornness.** See contradiction register, internal contradiction within TIM.

## Typical problem statements and approaches

**"Rational arguments don't work."** The model case: **Lion Guardians (Leela Hazzah, Kenya/Tanzania).** Problem: encroachment on habitat **and** a rite of passage in which adolescent Maasai men **kill a lion alone.** Population estimated at **only 30,000 lions left, down from 200,000** a generation or two ago.
"**All the rational arguments in the world aren't powerful enough to change deeply held cultural beliefs.**" Instead, new cultural beliefs were built **on fundamental human wants.** The analysis: The cultural **goals** are to bind the community, to create empowerment and opportunity, to instil courage and patience, to have a meaningful rite of passage, **to raise the boy's status. "None of these goals are directly related to killing a lion. That was simply a historical artifact.**"
The solution: a **new** rite of passage within the Maasai cultural systems. Courage and patience are now proven by **saving** a lion; the Maasai find and name lions, track them and carry out a census by radio telemetry. Self-description: "**Wildlife conservation has traditionally focused on wildlife, not people. At Lion Guardians, we take the opposite approach.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 11]

**"We argue instead of normalising."** The model case: **Blue Ribbons.** Godin's home town had excellent schools (blue-ribbon award) but a rift over the budget vote; above all long-time residents and second- or third-generation families were angry about rising school taxes, organised, and **for the first time in memory the vote failed.** A second defeat would have meant draconian cuts. **Eight days remained.**
The approach: no arguing, no flyers, no rally, but **a hundred blue ribbons on a big tree in front of the middle school.** Within days, **thousands of ribbons**, hung by dozens of families, hung on dozens of trees. Message: People like us, in this town, in this blue-ribbon district, support our schools. **Result: The budget passed two to one.** [Source: TIM, ch. 9]
Second case: **same-sex marriage referendum in Ireland.** The rational route (fairness, respect, civil rights) does not get far. Instead, **Brighid White and her husband Paddy, both almost eighty**, made a video about their son. "It's easy for some to watch that video and see themselves. **As parents. As traditionalists. As Irishmen.**" **The generalisation: "The essence of political change is almost always cultural change, and the culture changes HORIZONTALLY. Person to person. Us to us."** [Source: TIM, ch. 9]

**"The tactic was the secret" — the fallacy.** **Robin Hood Foundation, 2015: 101,000,000 USD in ONE night**, the most effective fundraiser of its kind in history. **"Some people look at this result and conclude that the tactic (a gala) is the secret. IT'S NOT."** It is the peer pressure of "people like us do things like this". Mechanics: The foundation had spent **a generation** building expectations, telling the story of the early adopters' generosity and playing on the hypercompetitive egomania of Wall Street. Almost all the money revolved around one exchange: **cash for status.** The tension arises from the event: "**You're there, your peers are there, your spouse is there. An auction is taking place. The cause is a good one. With a simple act, you can raise your profile, earn respect, and dominate the competition.**" Over years the narrative is normalised: "not extreme, not for this 'us'. Instead, **it's what we do**." And: "**The intentional nature of this process is easily overlooked. It rarely happens as an unintentional side effect.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 9]

**"How much trust is in the room?" — the standing ovation as a measuring instrument.** At TED, **three** people are enough; at a Broadway show perhaps **fifteen strangers**; at the Mezzrow jazz club it is **probably impossible.** Explanation: In some audiences there are few strangers, and trust plus the need to belong activate the ovation. In a room full of strangers one wears the tourist hat. With hard-core jazz fans, the rule is: jazz fans do not give ovations in the club. "**The venue has a bias.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 9]

**"Why does nothing spread?"** Probably the tension is missing that can only be released by passing it on. The mechanism, stated precisely: "**The best work will create an IMBALANCE in the viewer, one that can only be remedied by spreading the word, by experiencing this with someone else.** The tension this imbalance creates forces the word to spread. It means that asking, 'Have you seen ...?' **raises the status of the asker**, and the champions multiply." And: "**Telling others about how we've changed is the only way to relieve our tension.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 21]

**"We don't want to exclude anyone."** The counter-position with a deliberately uncomfortable piece of evidence: **the NRA as a model of method.** "There are few groups more controversial. **But as focused nonprofit/political marketers, they have no peer.**" Figures: **only five million members, less than 2 percent of the population** — and with that they changed the attitude and focus of thousands of legislators. "**By focusing on the minimal viable audience (just five million people), the NRA is very comfortable saying, 'It's not for you.'**" Evidence of activation: "**A Pew study shows that gun owners are more than twice as likely to contact government officials about their issues than nonowners are.**" Cultural mechanics: "The NRA intentionally creates 'people like us.' They're comfortable with insiders and outsiders ... and they've done it **not by changing worldviews but by embracing them.**"
**Godin's explicit distancing, which MUST travel with the quotation: "The NRA isn't my version of 'better,' but it clearly resonates with those that they seek to serve."** And the generalisation: "This persistent, disciplined approach to an issue is precisely how much of the change has been made in our culture." [Source: TIM, ch. 21]

**"Is marketing evil?"** The precondition of the argument: "**If marketing doesn't work, then a lot of us are wasting a great deal of effort (and cash). But it does.**" Godin's answer: not "is marketing evil", but "**Are marketers evil? ... Some of them.**"
**The list of the evil:** persuading children to start smoking; cynically manipulating the electoral process; lying in such a way that catastrophic side effects arise; **selling an ineffective remedy when an effective medicine is available**; inventing new ways to make smoking acceptable.
**The list of the beautiful:** persuading people to get the polio vaccine **or to wash their hands before an operation**; selling a product to someone who thereby discovers more joy or productivity; electing someone who improves the community.
**The rejection of the excuse of the job: "if marketing works, if it's worth the time and money we spend on it, then I don't think it matters a bit if you're 'just doing your job.' IT'S STILL WRONG."**
**The tool argument: "Just like every powerful tool, the impact comes from the CRAFTSMAN, not the tool. ... The question, one I hope you'll ask yourself, is WHAT ARE YOU GOING TO DO WITH THAT IMPACT?"**
**The test criterion: "marketing works for society when the marketer and consumer are BOTH AWARE of what's happening and are BOTH SATISFIED with the ultimate outcome."** Example: selling cosmetics is not evil, "**because beauty isn't the goal — it's the process that brings joy**". Counter-example: cheating someone out of their house to earn a commission.
**The assignment of responsibility: "Just because you CAN market something doesn't mean you SHOULD. You've got the power, so you're responsible, regardless of what your boss tells you to do."** And the jurisdiction: "I'm not in charge of what's evil and what's not. **You, your customers, and their neighbors are.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 23]

**"We take every failure personally."** "If you bring your best self to the world, your best work, and the world doesn't receive it, it's entirely possible that **your marketing sucked** ... that you chose the wrong axes, and that you failed to go to the edges ... **Fine, but that's not about you. That's about your work as a marketer. And you can get better at that craft.**"
The pottery analogy: "**Just because the pot you made on the wheel broke in the kiln doesn't mean you're not a good person.** It simply means your pot broke and that maybe some lessons in pottery will help you go forward."
The danger: "If we're going to take it personally every time someone doesn't click on a link ... **we get stuck in search of perfect. Stuck without empathy. Stuck in a corner, bleeding and in pain.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 23]

## Concrete examples
- **Marketing in five steps** (TIM's basic operational grid): (1) invent something worth making, with a story worth telling. (2) build it so that **a few** benefit especially from it. (3) tell a story that fits the built-in narrative and the dreams of this small group. (4) spread it — "the one everyone gets excited about". (5) **the overlooked step: show up, regularly, consistently, generously, over years**, organise, lead, build confidence, earn permission and enrollment. [Source: TIM, ch. 2]
- **Six "Things marketers know":** (1) Engaged, creative people change the world; only they do. (2) You cannot change everyone -> "Who's it for?" focuses and helps in dealing with the non-believers. (3) Change comes about best with intent -> "What's it for?" (4) **People tell themselves stories; for them these are completely true, and wanting to talk them out of them is foolish.** (5) People can be grouped into stereotyped groups that tell themselves similar stories. (6) **"What you say isn't nearly as important as what others say about you."** [Source: TIM, ch. 2]
- **Roots and shoots** (the summarising image): The work is a tree whose roots lie in the soil of dreams and wants — not of everyone, only of those one wants to serve. If the work is merely a commodity, the roots do not go deep, "it will be crowded out by all the similar trees". As the tree grows, it becomes a beacon: early adopters climb it, use it as shade, eat the fruit — and attract others. Its height then blocks "the futile efforts of other, similar trees. **The market likes a winner.**" And: "**It's a mistake to show up with an acorn and expect a crowd.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 9]
- **The sunflower metaphor:** "**How tall is your sunflower?** That's what most people seem to care about. How big a brand, how much market share, how many online followers. ... The thing is, **tall sunflowers have deep and complex root systems. Without them, they'd never get very high.**" [Source: TIM, framing section]
- **"It's not going to market itself":** The best ideas are not accepted immediately — "**even the ice cream sundae and the stoplight took years to catch on.**" Reason: they demand considerable change, contradict the status quo, "**and inertia is a powerful force** ... And because we often want others to go first." [Source: TIM, framing section]
- **The capitalism-purpose sentence:** "**The purpose of our culture isn't to enable capitalism, even capitalism that pays your bills. The purpose of capitalism is to build our culture.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 12]
- **Getting the boss to say yes** — change aimed at a single person: "It's one thing to market to the world, but it feels quite different to market to one person ... **Except it's not. Not really.**" The boss wants what she has always wanted, sees through **her** lens of experience, knows who the "people like us" are, and wants status, security and respect. Two wrong paths: coming with one's own wish (price, features, false urgency); or **asking for authority without offering responsibility.** The way: see status roles, decode dominion versus affiliation, earn enrollment through trust. [Source: TIM, ch. 21]

## Open points (contradiction register)
The following points are **documented, not resolved.** Full evidence in the register in `../research/system-overview.md`. When requests touch them, **both positions** are to be presented.

- **C2 Persistence versus a fresh start, within TIM:** Ch. 15 and 20 demand extreme persistence (change campaigns when the accountant is tired of them; the Ziglar case), ch. 23 demands blowing it up and starting over. **There is no criterion in the source for when persistence turns into stubbornness.** This is carried as an observation task.
- **One-sidedness as an open point:** Eight TIM chapters against two KOB chapters. This area predominantly represents one position, and it comes from an author with a vested interest. **Anyone who adopts only the mnemonics distorts the source** — Godin is explicitly less dogmatic than his aphorisms in several places.
- **Type of evidence as an open point:** The evidence in this area consists of case stories, partly without a control group. Reliable impact research on culture and community marketing is missing in both sources.
