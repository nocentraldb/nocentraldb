# Channels, Advertising and Permission

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Tybout/Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018.

## Character of the sources for this subject area
**This is where the two books stand furthest apart, and the gap is partly a question of dates.** KOB (2005) knows neither social media as a channel nor the Google/Facebook duopoly; TIM (2018) treats them as a basic fact. **KOB is the denser source for the mechanics of advertising effect** (information-processing model, brand linkage, media strategy, wear-out). **TIM is the denser source for permission as an asset, for remarkability, and for the worked-through funnel economics.**
**Important: both books share the diagnosis (fragmentation, advertising alone is not enough) and differ in the conclusion** — KOB professionalises advertising, TIM shifts the weight. **TIM carries a conflict of interest** (seminar sales, and the author is the inventor of the term "permission marketing", which he promotes here).

## Core concepts and definitions

### The shared diagnosis: fragmentation and clutter
**KOB's figures (2005): "Network television that once reached over 90 percent of Americans now reaches less than half that number. IN 1965, 80 PERCENT OF AMERICANS COULD BE REACHED BY THREE TV SPOTS, WHEREAS TODAY IT WOULD REQUIRE 97 SPOTS."** In addition: over 200 TV channels by satellite or cable, XM Satellite Radio alone over 120 channels, millions of websites, **and an exceptionally popular prime-time show reaches 15 million people, which is only 5 percent of the US population.** And: "**TiVo and other digital video recording devices make it easy to skip the normal viewing of commercials.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 7 and introduction]
**KOB's counter-finding to the "death of advertising": "Despite these concerns, advertising expenditures have been GROWING at a steady rate. In 2004, ad spending growth was about 8 percent, while Internet and cable television advertising spending saw DOUBLE-DIGIT growth. Thus, IT APPEARS THAT THE FORECASTED DEMISE OF ADVERTISING RUNS AFOUL OF FACT. However, the advertising landscape is definitely changing."** The observed shift: "Growing numbers of advertisers with large budgets are focusing their spending on **building RELATIONSHIPS WITH CURRENT HEAVY USERS** of their brands." [Source: KOB, ch. 7]
**KOB's limitation from the inside: "Great advertising is important, but ADVERTISING ALONE IS NO LONGER ENOUGH, due to the high levels of media fragmentation."** [Source: KOB, introduction]
**TIM's version of the same diagnosis:** The internet is "the first mass medium that wasn't invented to make marketers happy. Television was invented to hold TV ads, and radio was invented to give radio ads a place to live. **But the internet isn't built around interruption and mass.** It's the largest medium, but it's also the smallest one. **There's no mass** ... In fact, it's **a billion tiny whispers**, an endless series of selfish conversations that rarely include you or the work you do." [Source: TIM, ch. 1]
**And the decisive reversal of perspective: "What looks like CLUTTER to the marketer feels like CHOICE."** A million books a year, over 500 battery chargers on Amazon, more coaches, courses and clubs than anyone can consider. The obvious customer reaction to this tsunami: **walking away.** [Source: TIM, ch. 5]

### Advertising as an optional engine
"**Advertising is a special case, an OPTIONAL ENGINE for growth.**" Media companies, telecommunications firms and delivery services all earn the same way: "**they sell the attention of those they OSTENSIBLY SERVE.**" You do not have to earn this attention, you can buy it: "You're no longer the outsider; now you're the customer."
**The good news:** If you find an ad approach that works, "**you can scale it. You can scale it quickly and precisely.**" **The bad news:** "it's not easy to find an ad approach that works. That doesn't mean you shouldn't try, but you need to be clear about what you're doing and why."
"**An ad, unnoticed, doesn't exist.**" A noticed ad is noticed by **some**, and if by the right ones, it creates tension — "the tension of not knowing and needing to know more. The tension of being left behind."
**The sharpest verdict: "Almost all TV advertising is simply SEMIOTIC NOISE. Reassuring the viewer ('As seen on TV') that this is a safe brand, a brand you and your peers know, a brand that can afford to be on this box. THAT'S A TAX that major companies in competitive markets have to pay. But it's not the sort of marketing that's realistic to consider for anyone else."**
**And the author's own qualification, which has to travel with the quote: "It's NOT that advertising CAN'T work. It's simply that it's not the right answer for everyone, at least in this moment."** [Source: TIM, ch. 15]

### The three elements of online advertising magic — and the counter-finding
**The magic:** (1) "you can reach people **more precisely** online than in any other medium. Not just the demographics of what they look like, but the **psychographics** of what they believe and what they're looking for." (2) "you can reach people **instantly.** You can decide to run an ad at 10 a.m. and have it reach people beginning at 10:01 a.m." (3) "**you can measure everything.**"
**The counter-finding: "Because online advertising is also THE MOST IGNORED ADVERTISING EVER CREATED. It's not unusual to run an ad in front of A HUNDRED THOUSAND PEOPLE AND GET NOT A SINGLE CLICK. It's not unusual for an entire ad campaign to start, run, and finish without making ANY impact on the culture."**
**The reason: "Advertising is UNEARNED MEDIA. It's bought and paid for. AND THE PEOPLE YOU SEEK TO REACH KNOW IT. They're suspicious. They're inundated. They're exhausted. You didn't pay the recipient to run that ad, but you want the recipient to pay you with their attention. So you're ignored."** [Source: TIM, ch. 15]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### Brand marketing versus direct marketing — the section TIM itself calls the most valuable
Source: **Lester Wunderman**, the father of direct marketing, who named it and used it to build American Express, the Columbia Record Club and a hundred other projects; Godin brought him onto the Yoyodyne advisory board in 1995.
**The distinction lies in what happens AFTER the ad:**
- **Direct marketing is action-oriented. AND IT IS MEASURED.**
- **Brand marketing is culturally oriented. AND IT CANNOT BE MEASURED.**
Operational test: Someone who runs a Facebook ad, counts clicks and measures conversions is doing **direct marketing.** Someone who puts up a highway billboard in the hope that at the next death in the family people will remember the funeral home is doing **brand marketing.** Side effects in both directions are possible and welcome, but "**the danger is in being CONFUSED.**"
**The double rule:**
- For **direct marketing**: "**measure everything.** Compute how much it costs you to earn attention, to get a click, to turn that attention into an order. **Direct marketing is action marketing, and IF YOU'RE NOT ABLE TO MEASURE IT, IT DOESN'T COUNT.**"
- For **brand marketing**: "**be patient. REFUSE TO MEASURE.** Engage with the culture. Focus, by all means, but mostly, **be consistent and patient. If you can't afford to be consistent and patient, DON'T PAY FOR BRAND MARKETING ADS.**"
**Godin himself: "The two paragraphs above ought to have paid for the time and money you've spent on this book."**
**The explanation for Google's and Facebook's revenue growth: "many of the ads that are run on these services PAY FOR THEMSELVES. A hundred dollars' worth of online advertising generates $125 in profit for the advertiser. And she knows this, so she buys more. In fact, SHE KEEPS BUYING ADS UNTIL THEY STOP PAYING FOR THEMSELVES."**
**The break point for brand advertisers:** Brand advertising (Ford, Absolut Vodka, Palmolive) shaped the culture for generations, "but these brands **cannot possibly build direct marketing campaigns that work.** And so the shift to a measured direct marketing environment online has been **stressful and riddled with failure.**" Evidence: **Procter & Gamble** spends billions on TV brand advertising for Tide and Crest — "**when TV brand ads are replaced with digital direct ads, their business model falls apart.**" And the local pizzeria had stuck with Yellow Pages advertising, where a big ad paid for itself and the special phone number proved it; "**moving to Yelp is time consuming and feels risky. No control, no proven history.**"
**The balance for smaller players:** "For many smaller businesses, the move from expensive, slow, and difficult-to-measure brand ads to quick, agile, and measurable direct ads is a **positive** shift. **But it's not easy to act like a direct marketer when you're trying to reach people who generally don't click on ads.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 15]
**The scope of brand marketing, understood broadly:** "**Everything** you do, from the way you answer the phone to the design of your packaging, from your location to the downstream effects of your work, from the hold music to the behavior of your executives, **and even the kind of packing peanuts you use** — all of it is a form of marketing your brand. You can't measure it. You might not even notice it. **But it still matters.**"
**The most important rule on this: "You definitely, certainly, and surely DON'T HAVE ENOUGH TIME AND MONEY TO BUILD A BRAND FOR EVERYONE. You can't. Don't try. Be specific. Be very specific. And then, with this knowledge, OVERDO your brand marketing. Every slice of every interaction ought to reflect the whole. EVERY TIME WE SEE ANY OF YOU, WE OUGHT TO BE ABLE TO MAKE A SMART GUESS ABOUT ALL OF YOU."** [Source: TIM, ch. 15]

### The information-processing model of advertising exposure
Two stages: Advertising information is encoded in **short-term memory** (limited capacity, short duration) — "**if there is no further processing, the information will be LOST**". With enough elaboration or repetition it moves into **long-term memory**, whose hallmark is **organisation**: information is stored in **networks of associations**, the individual pieces are called **nodes**, ordered hierarchically (Coca-Cola -> soft drink -> beverage). The association types from which benefits are derived: **attributes** (caramel colour), **users** (young adults), **usage occasions** (sporting events).
**Three factors of activation: (1) FREQUENCY** — "the more often a node is activated, the more readily it comes to mind. **We all recognize that leading brands are easier to recall than minor brands.**" **(2) RECENCY** — "memory operates on a **last-in, first-out** basis." **(3) ELABORATION** — the associations a person forms. The model example: An airline's convenience can be linked to many destinations, e-tickets and curbside check-in; "**by elaborating on these conveniences in the ad, an airline can prompt the activation of the CONVENIENCE node. Through these associations, customers may conclude that flying with the airline is convenient, ALTHOUGH CONVENIENCE IS NOT EXPLICITLY MENTIONED IN THE AD.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 7]

### Four elaboration strategies (with origins)
- **HARD SELL** (popularised by the agency **Ted Bates** in the 1950s): explicit link between name and benefit, "Buy this brand, get this benefit." Evidence: **Visa "It's everywhere you want to be"; Bounty "The quicker picker-upper".** Effectiveness depends on whether the benefit is important **and** distinguishes the brand.
- **BIG IDEA** (developed by **Leo Burnett**): a multitude of reasons to believe that the brand owns a particular benefit. Example Delta with the big idea **convenience**: one ad on the online boarding pass, further ones on curbside check-in and more hubs. "**The big idea approach links different SUB-benefits as multiple reasons to believe in a PRIMARY benefit.**"
- **STORY GRAMMAR**: an episode sequence with problem/goal, progress, outcome. Evidence **Dynaco** (industrial doors), a print ad with three photos: a high-speed door hits a person; the second shows that the person is unhurt; the third, how easily the door is reset. **The warning: "The irony is that while the drama captures people's attention, sometimes the audience may become SO ABSORBED IN THE STORY that they fail to link the benefit to the brand."**
- **CELEBRITY SPOKESPERSON**: "most effective when the spokesperson's CHARACTER can be used to PERSONIFY the brand's benefit." Evidence: **Lance Armstrong's endurance and speed for the U.S. Postal Service.**
**Humour as an elaboration engine, with a warning:** "**The caveat is that when humor serves as the motivational device, the ad may WEAR OUT after relatively few exposures. Because humor can be DISTRACTING, the humor must be closely tied to the key benefit.**" Three forms of use: showing the consequences of the benefit; showing the consequences of non-use (**Dr. Scholl's foot deodorant: dead fish float to the surface of a lake when someone dangles unpowdered feet in it**); showing the competition's weaknesses (**Lee Jeans showed the effort of fitting into other jeans brands — "by drawing attention to the fact that Lee UNDERSTOOD THE CONSUMER'S PROBLEM, the advertising implied that Lee was likely to be the solution"**). [Source: KOB, ch. 7]

### Brand linkage — the most important practical section of KOB ch. 7
"**A key to effective advertising is the LINKAGE between the benefit and the brand name.** As critical as brand linkage is, for many advertising campaigns, the audience can play back the message, but **they either do not relate it to the advertised brand, OR THEY RELATE IT TO A COMPETING BRAND. For example, ads for EVEREADY batteries were remembered as ads for DURACELL.**"
**Two threats:**
- **LATE IDENTIFICATION.** "By withholding the brand name until the end, **the audience is INVITED TO MAKE ITS OWN ASSOCIATIONS** throughout the ad's duration. The result is that consumers may associate the commercial with another brand or even another category. **In most cases, presenting the brand name at the end DOES NOT CORRECT the erroneous association**" — because correcting a judgement demands cognitive resources, "and people typically do not find it worthwhile to exert the effort."
  **The exception in which late ID is right: "Late IDs are useful when an individual's disposition toward the brand is UNFAVORABLE, because it encourages people to process the CURRENT message rather than focus on their own prior (negative) thoughts."** Evidence **Gallo**: The winery had the reputation of a mass-market brand. For the first high-end wine, "Gallo of Sonoma", a late ID was used — "consumers heard about the virtues of the brand **before** knowing the brand name. **Without a late ID, people might have DISMISSED Gallo of Sonoma because of their prior opinion of the Gallo brand.**"
- **ME-TOO EXECUTIONS.** Executions that are too similar to those of competitors undermine the link — "**particularly problematic when the advertisers are from the same industry and the ads appear in the same medium.**" Evidence: **Consulting firms that all advertise in Fortune and Business Week and all use metaphors for their point of difference "renders their advertising very similar."** [Source: KOB, ch. 7]

### The decision process itself as a basis for judgement — the BMW evidence
Two ads identical except for the headline: "There are many reasons to drive a BMW. **Can you think of ONE reason?**" versus "**Can you think of 10 reasons?**"
**Result: "Individuals exposed to the ONE-reason ad exhibited a MORE FAVORABLE disposition toward the car. The 10-reasons ad prompted the inference that IF IT IS HARD TO THINK OF REASONS TO DRIVE A BMW, IT MUST NOT BE AS GOOD AS ONE THOUGHT."** (Wanke/Bohner/Jurkowitsch 1997, *JCR*.)
**The moderation by expertise:** People who know a great deal about the brand recognise that even many reasons are easy to find; people who know very little recognise that even few are hard. "**It is those who fall BETWEEN these knowledge extremes who may rely on their experience as the basis of brand evaluation.**"
**The repair: "When the respondents were asked to IMAGINE 10 reasons (thus making the process more enjoyable), their evaluation became MORE FAVORABLE. Framing the task in terms of a pleasant experience made the task SEEM easy."**
**The generalisation: "Because consumers monitor their processing experience and use it as the basis for judgment, ADVERTISERS MUST STRIVE TO MAKE THE DECISION PROCESS A POSITIVE EXPERIENCE."** [Source: KOB, ch. 7]

### Two choice situations — and the counter-intuitive copy consequence
- **MEMORY-BASED CHOICE**: The brand has to be retrieved from long-term memory (choice of restaurant and shop, ordering a drink, a shopping list written in advance). "**A brand that comes to mind more readily has a greater chance of being chosen.** Frequent as well as recent exposures will make a brand more accessible, as will ad copy that encourages **elaboration**."
- **STIMULUS-BASED CHOICE**: Decision-relevant information is physically present (supermarket shelf, cinema page, catalogue, web surfing). **"Contrary to conventional wisdom, ad copy that promotes attention and elaboration may NOT necessarily help with visual identification. Rather, it is THE VISUAL MATCH between the product in the ad and the product on display at the time of brand choice that ensures brand choice. Thus, an effective ad copy strategy for the stimulus-based customer may be one that features JUST THE PRODUCT in its original package or the brand name, UNENCUMBERED BY OTHER INFORMATION."** [Source: KOB, ch. 7]

### Media strategy: intimacy, timing, scheduling
**MEDIA SELECTION by INTIMACY**, from impersonal (focus on the brand) to intimate (focus on the customer):
- Low intimacy, mass media: when brand information is the point.
- Selective mass media: **Saks Fifth Avenue publishes its own magazine "5" for its best customers; Sears placed tools, appliances and furniture in the reality show "Extreme Makeover: Home Edition".**
- Shared interests beyond the category: **NetZero sponsored Ward Burton's NASCAR car #0 and showed racing fans a shared interest — speed. TiVo advertised in Architectural Digest, Golf Digest and The New Yorker, "to underscore the values the company held in common with upscale consumers."**
- Highest intimacy, individual communication: **Lucky Strike sent teams to bring smokers drinks and folding chairs outside office buildings; W.W. Grainger provided every customer with an individualised price list reflecting that customer's contract agreements.**
- **The multi-stage evidence U.S. Army:** TV advertising informed and directed viewers to the website; goarmy.com allowed a more intimate understanding through profiles of current soldiers (of all genders, races and ethnicities) with typical daily routine, skills and challenges; **and at the most intimate level, recruiters spoke one to one.**
**MEDIA SELECTION by TIMING — with a hard limit.** Products like **No Doz** could be advertised at night on radio or on billboards. **But: "Caution is warranted, as some audiences might perceive such deliberate customized advertising to be INTRUSIVE."** Three documented failures: **Health Club TV** (fitness and sports programming during workouts with interspersed health advertising); **Whittle Communications** (health product advertising in magazines developed specifically for doctors' offices; later TVs in practices). "**ALL THESE VENTURES FAILED, suggesting that people might not appreciate being the targets of advertising messages AT TIMES WHEN THEY ARE MOST VULNERABLE.**"
**MEDIA SCHEDULING:** Trade-off between **reach** (share of the target group reached at least once per month), **frequency** (average number of contacts per person per month) and **continuity versus concentration** over the year.
**Evidence for concentration: "In introducing a new cereal, brands often spend AS MUCH AS 40 PERCENT OF THEIR BUDGET IN THE FIRST THREE MONTHS after launch. This delivers the reach and frequency needed to prompt the desired level of trial and repeat purchase. This introductory effort is often followed by SEVERAL MONTHS WITH NO AD SUPPORT."** And: small brands often compete with larger ones by alternating advertising and pause months.
**The contact-number question — and the reasoned refusal to answer it: "For many years, the answer offered by the trade association and other industry experts was THREE OR MORE. However, this conventional wisdom has more recently been challenged by the belief that perhaps ONE exposure is optimal. The rationale is that reach may be more important than frequency ... It is important to recognize that NEITHER OF THESE RECOMMENDATIONS OFFERS A USEFUL GUIDELINE. The optimal number depends on the longevity of the brand's position, the creative strategy, the extent to which the advertising presents news, the competitors' spending level and creative strategy, and many other factors. IN SHORT, THE OPTIMAL FREQUENCY LEVEL IS AN EMPIRICAL QUESTION."**
**WEAR-OUT:** "Wear-out occurs when people start thinking about **their OWN experiences** with the brand, which might often be **LESS favorable** than information presented in the advertising message." Most likely **when media are cheap and budgets are concentrated on few vehicles** — typical in **B2B with few trade titles per industry (vertical books) and low ad prices**, hence multiple placements per issue; and in consumer marketing with few suitable radio stations.
**Countermeasure with a fine rule: "When successive ads appear closely together and the message is relatively simple, people quickly begin to ASSUME THEY KNOW THE AD without paying much attention. Increasing the spacing reduces consumers' sense of familiarity and makes them MORE likely to pay attention. When the content is COMPLEX, ad exposures should be CONCENTRATED AT FIRST (to allow the audience to build on their knowledge through successive exposures), and SPACED FURTHER APART after the message content is fully realized."** Plus: offer news, for example via the big idea. [Source: KOB, ch. 7]

### Permission as an asset
**The definition: the privilege of delivering "anticipated, personal, and relevant" messages to people who WANT to get them.** "That hardly seems controversial, but it was. **It got me thrown out of the Direct Marketing Association.**"
**The Yoyodyne figures as evidence:** At one point the company sent, received and processed more email than anyone else on the planet — **with the active permission of every single person. Open rates above 70 percent, average response rate 33 percent. "That's about A THOUSAND TIMES the rate of a typical commercial email sent in 2018."**
**What permission is NOT: "Just because you somehow get my email address doesn't mean you have permission to use it. Just because I don't complain doesn't mean you have permission. Just because it's in the fine print of your privacy policy doesn't mean it's permission either."**
**The operational test: "Real permission works like this: IF YOU STOP SHOWING UP, PEOPLE ARE CONCERNED. THEY ASK WHERE YOU WENT."** And the simplest definition: "**the people who would MISS you if you didn't reach out.**" As a metric: "**How many people would reach out and wonder (or complain) if you didn't send out that next email blast? That's a metric worth measuring and increasing.**"
**The discipline of the promise:** "In order to get permission, you make a promise: 'I will do x, y, and z; I hope you will give me permission by listening.' **And then — this is the hard part — that's ALL you do.** You don't assume you can do more. You don't sell the list or rent the list or demand more attention." You can promise a newsletter and talk for years, a daily RSS feed and talk every three minutes, **a sales pitch every day (like the internet retailer Woot)** — "**but the promise is the promise until BOTH SIDES agree to change it.** You don't assume that just because you're running for President or coming to the end of the quarter or launching a new product that you have the right to break the deal. **You don't.**"
**The sharecropper argument against platforms: "Facebook and other social platforms seem like a shortcut, because they make it apparently easy to reach new people. But the tradeoff is that YOU'RE A SHARECROPPER. It's not your land. YOU don't have permission to contact people; THEY do. YOU don't own an asset; THEY do."** In addition: "Over time, the platform makes money by **charging you**, not by giving away their work. And so you'll need to boost a post. Or worry about what happens when the platform tries to increase its stock price." **"You should OWN that, not RENT it."**
And the formality rule: "Permission doesn't have to be formal, but **it must be obvious.** My friend has permission to call me if he needs to borrow five dollars, **but the person you meet at a trade show has no such ability to pitch you his entire resume, even though he paid to get in.**"
**Subscriptions as an open act of permission:** "That's why home delivery newspaper readers are so valuable, and why **magazine subscribers are worth more than newsstand readers.**"
**The protective instruction: "Protect it. It's MORE VALUABLE THAN THE LAPTOPS OR CHAIRS IN YOUR OFFICE. If someone walked out the door with those, you'd fire them. ACT THE SAME WAY IF SOMEONE ON YOUR TEAM SPAMS THE LIST just to make a metric go up."** [Source: TIM, ch. 17]

### Remarkability (Purple Cow) and evangelism design
"It's almost impossible to spread your word directly. Too expensive, too slow. **The alternative is to INTENTIONALLY CREATE a product or service that people decide is worth talking about.**"
**The responsibility rule: "Whether something is remarkable ISN'T UP TO YOU, the creator. You can do your best, but the final decision is up to your USER, not you. IF THEY REMARK ON IT, THEN IT'S REMARKABLE."** And it has to happen on purpose, built in deeply — "**that means that effective marketers are also in charge of the EXPERIENCE that the customers have.**"
**Distinction from stunts:** "Offensive/juvenile/urgent/selfish is not the same thing as purple. ... **Stunts come from a place of selfishness.**"
**The reason people talk is always self-related: "The best reason someone talks about you is because THEY'RE ACTUALLY TALKING ABOUT THEMSELVES: 'Look at how good my taste is.' Or perhaps, 'Look at how good I am at spotting important ideas.'"** Conversely: We criticise and talk about crossed lines "to send a signal to our friends and neighbors". **"We're not impressed by how much money you spent, what lines you crossed, or how important the work is to you. No, WE SPREAD THE WORD WHEN IT BENEFITS US, our taste, our standing, our desire for novelty and change."**
**The construction rule: "The hard work of creating the change you seek begins with DESIGNING EVANGELISM INTO THE VERY FABRIC of what you're creating. People aren't going to spread the word because it's important to YOU. They'll only do it because it's important to THEM."**
**And the consequence for the choice of topic: "If the change you seek to make CAN'T BE TALKED ABOUT, perhaps you should find a different change worth making."**
Evidence for built-in spread: **Fight Club** — "as soon as the right sort of character (worldview!) heard about Fight Club, that rule was **an invitation** to talk about Fight Club." And **Alcoholics Anonymous**, "a huge organization. And it's hardly anonymous. Built into the practice of an active member is the posture that, when in doubt, **we talk about AA, because talking about it is a GENEROUS ACT. It's a shame killer. It's a life raft.**"
**"Ideas travel horizontally now: from person to person, NOT from organization to customer."** [Source: TIM, ch. 17]

### The funnel
Image: a funnel **with holes and leaks.** Attention at the top, loyal customers at the bottom; in between most of them seep out — "they walk away, trust diminished, or leave due to a **mismatch** between what you offer and what they believe, **a disconnect between what you SAY and what they HEAR.** Or maybe it's just not a good fit, or they're distracted, or life got in the way."
**Trust is not static:** On the way from stranger to friend to customer to loyal customer, **the state of trust changes** — either it grows or, more likely, "they become more distracted, more fearful, more eager to run away, **because saying yes is more stressful than simply walking away.**"
**The six repair levers: (1)** make sure the **right** people are attracted. **(2)** make sure the promise that brought them in matches the destination. **(3)** remove steps so fewer decisions are needed. **(4)** support those involved, amplify dreams, soothe fears. **(5)** use tension to create forward movement. **(6) above all: "hand those who have successfully engaged in the funnel A MEGAPHONE, a tool they can use to tell the others. People like us do things like this."**
**The four-step chain for online direct marketing:** "The ad exists to get a click. The click exists to either make a sale or **earn permission.** The sale exists to lead to another sale, or to word of mouth. Permission exists to lead to education and to a sale. **That's it.**" Every step has costs. **"Assign values to each step. If you can't, DON'T RUN ANY DIRECT-RESPONSE ADS until you can."**
**The warning against wrong optimisation: "By all means, work to lower the cost of that first click. But if you do it by making A RIDICULOUS PROMISE in the ad you run, IT'LL BACKFIRE, because once in the funnel, people will stop trusting you, the tension will evaporate, and your yield will plummet."** Instead: consider which steps to move or eliminate, explore "what happens if people engage in your ideas or your community **BEFORE you ask them to send you money**", and invest in lifetime value — "**building new things for your customers instead of racing around trying to find new customers for your things.**"
**The two stop rules: "If you can't SEE the funnel, don't buy the ads. If you can MEASURE the funnel and it costs too much for you to afford ads, don't buy the ads. FIX THE FUNNEL FIRST."**
**The book's most honest qualification: "It's not going to be a magical fountain of results. ... While there are plenty of people happy to sell you a miracle — a self-running, passive funnel of income — THESE MAGICAL FUNNELS ARE RARE. That's because THE LIFETIME VALUE OF A NEW CUSTOMER RARELY EXCEEDS THE COST OF RUNNING THE ADS NECESSARY TO GET A NEW CUSTOMER."** Reason: "People are so distrustful, and the web is so cluttered, that the ads rarely have enough power to pay for themselves. ... **the cost of engagement has gone through the roof.**"
**THE CORE STATEMENT OF THE AREA: "Most brands that matter, and most organizations that thrive, are PRIMED BY ADVERTISING BUT BUILT BY GOOD MARKETING. They grow because users evangelize to their friends. They grow because they are living entities, offering ever more value to the communities they serve. They grow because they find TRIBES that coalesce around the cultural change they're able to produce."**
**The sequence that follows: "The goal is to PRIME THE PUMP with ads that are aimed at neophiliacs, people looking to find you. THEN build trust with frequency. To gain trial. To generate word of mouth. And to make it pay by building a cohort of people, a network that needs your work to be part of who they are and what they do."** And the warning: "**It's easy to skip the last part, the stuff that happens AFTER the first click. And if you only do the easy, expensive part, you'll almost certainly be unhappy with the outcome.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **Frequency and trust.** "People don't remember what they read, what they hear, or even what they see. If they're lucky, people remember what they DO, but they're not very good at that either. **WE REMEMBER WHAT WE REHEARSE.**" Evidence: We remember Uncle Fred, who came for Christmas twenty years in a row, but not his date Ethyl, who was there only once. We remember events with photos in the album — **not because of the photographing, but because we rehearse the story every time we see the picture.**
  **The trust mechanism: "The familiar is normal and the normal is trusted."** And why marketers forget this daily: "**because WE get bored with our stuff.** Our story, our change. We've heard it before. ... And so we change it."
  **Jay Levinson, quoted: "Don't change your ads when YOU'RE tired of them. Don't change them when your EMPLOYEES are tired of them. Don't even change them when your FRIENDS are tired of them. CHANGE THEM WHEN YOUR ACCOUNTANT IS TIRED OF THEM."**
  **The dip: "frequency teaches us that there's a very real DIP — a gap between when WE get bored and when PEOPLE GET THE MESSAGE."** Typical quitting points in this gap: the talk given a few times, perhaps on the TED stage, then on to the next thing; the freelance business started with a few clients, then it sputters; the company founded, money spent quickly, **driven into the wall "just before the good stuff happens".** "**If you quit right in the middle of building that frequency, it's no wonder you never got a chance to earn the trust.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 15]
- **Goal, strategy, tactic — the theft test.** "**If you tell your competition your TACTICS, they'll steal them and it will cost you. But if you tell them your STRATEGY, it won't matter. Because they don't have the guts or the persistence to turn your strategy into their strategy.**" Rules for change: The strategy may be changed if it fails, **"but you don't want to do it often"**; tactics are dozens or hundreds of steps, and "**you can change tactics the moment you decide that they're not helping you achieve your strategy any longer.**"
  **The case comparison:** **Coca-Cola** — goal: more people drink more Coke. Strategy: an enormous number of ads to convince the mass market that Coke is part of the happy-making culture and everyone else drinks it too. Tactic: the ad content, which changed again and again.
  **Patagonia** — goal: get a tiny group of outdoor enthusiasts to care about the environment and express that through Patagonia clothing ("These are all **silent sports**. None require a motor; none deliver the cheers of a crowd"). Strategy: redefine the way some people think about environmental impact and clothing quality; give this small group a label and an evangelism tool; **create insiders and outsiders.** Tactics: new recycling routes, stores in brick buildings, decisions on materials, selection and price. **"When a tactic fails, they don't abandon their strategy, THE SAME ONE THEY'VE HAD FOR MORE THAN THIRTY YEARS."** [Source: TIM, ch. 15]
- **CPM comparison and the question of value.** "An ad in a fancy magazine will cost you **$80 CPM**, which means you get in front of 1,000 readers (using the term loosely) for eighty dollars. Or a bit under a dime per person. An ad on a second-rate website might cost you **$80 to be in front of A MILLION PEOPLE.** But of course, these people are **scanning, clicking, ignoring**, and will neither remember your ad nor take action. **Anyone about to buy an ad needs to ask, 'What's it worth?'**" And: "People seeking to make change happen are often in a hurry, and **advertising FEELS like a shortcut. But without persistence and focus, the investment is wasted.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 15]
- **The duopoly's share and who keeps the profit.** In 2017 Google and Facebook took in **over a hundred billion dollars**, "**about half of all the money spent on online advertising worldwide.**" The calculation: 1,000 USD for ads shown to a million people -> **twenty clicks** -> **50 USD per click** -> one in ten becomes an order -> **500 USD per order.**
  **The critical addition: "The vast majority of your profit is going straight from you to the place you're buying the ads, which is why those two companies are so extraordinarily successful. They're SKIMMING THE PROFIT OFF THE TOP of just about all of their advertisers. GOOGLE MIGHT MAKE A HUNDRED DOLLARS PER SALE IN PROFIT, WHERE YOU, THE ADVERTISER DOING ALL THE WORK, ARE MAKING JUST TEN DOLLARS."** You live with it because the margin on the next sale stays positive.
  **And the reality check: "It's worth noting that VERY FEW ORGANIZATIONS DO THIS MATH CAREFULLY. They're SPENDING AND PRAYING, hoping it all comes out in the wash."** [Source: TIM, ch. 19]
- **SEO critique and the alternative.** "**The Google ecosystem is based on a MYTH** — that millions and millions of businesses, all grooming themselves for the search engine, will be found by people who seek them. Dating sites offer the same promise. As do the social networks. **THE MATH CAN'T SUPPORT THIS.** There are a thousand pages of results. What delusion we must be under to imagine we will be the first match."
  **The way out: not wanting to be found for a generic term, but "to have someone care enough about you and what you create that they'll type in YOUR NAME."** Godin's own example: You can find his blog by googling "blog" — "**But I'd rather have you search for 'Seth' instead.**"
  **The qualification he names himself: "A locksmith or a hotel or a doctor who WINS their generic search will earn a huge profit. But everyone else is left to spend money on consultants and trickery. The math can't support this pyramid scheme."**
  The two steps: (1) build a product that people want to search for **specifically** — "**you cannot win in a generic search, but you'll ALWAYS win if the search is specific enough.**" (2) be the one they want to find. "**When you do that, Google's ON YOUR SIDE. They actually WANT you to be found when someone searches for you.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 15]
- **How to obtain permission at all: generosity where permission already exists.** The model case: **Marvel and the San Diego Comic-Con.** When Marvel wants to launch a new superhero franchise, it does not start **with nationwide TV ads. "The Comic-Con HAS permission. Permission from raving fans, neophiliac fans, to break new ideas, to help them find the next big thing."** That is the place for Deadpool — **not with a pitch, but with generosity:** a special preview, an interview with the director, real news. **"The movie's not coming out for a year. They're not there to sell tickets. They're there to EARN PERMISSION."** And above all it is a signal to the core of the tribe "that this is the sort of thing people like us will be talking about next year."
  **"It doesn't matter that there's only a tiny percentage of the movie market at Comic-Con. What matters is the QUALITY OF THEIR STORY and the DEPTH OF THEIR EMPATHY AND GENEROSITY."** [Source: TIM, ch. 17]

## Typical problems and approaches

**"Should we run ads?"** The decision chain from TIM: (1) Is it direct or brand marketing? (2) For direct marketing: Can I see the funnel and value each step? If not — don't run them. If yes and too expensive — fix the funnel first. (3) For brand marketing: Can I afford consistency and patience? If not — don't run them. [Source: TIM, ch. 15 and 19]
The addition from KOB: Even when you do run them, the number of contacts is **an empirical question** and not a rule, and the message has to hit the regulatory focus, the aspirations and the prior beliefs of the target group. [Source: KOB, ch. 7]

**"The campaign is remembered, but attributed to the wrong brand."** Diagnosis via brand linkage. Checkpoints: Is the brand identified early? Is the execution distinguishable from the competition? Is the benefit elaborated **and** tied to the name? The Eveready/Duracell case is the standard evidence. [Source: KOB, ch. 7]

**"We have a large following, but it brings nothing."** The sharecropper argument: The platform owns the relationship, not you. The counter-design is your own permission assets. Evidence of their value: **Dany Levy started the email newsletter DailyCandy** (city-specific alerts for young women with local sales, parties, connections) — "**the asset was so valuable that she ended up selling it for MORE THAN A HUNDRED MILLION DOLLARS.**" And **Tuma Basa at Spotify**: hired in 2015 **in a defensive move** against Apple's DJ-curated playlists, he took over RapCaviar; **within months over three million subscribers, within three years nine million.** "**He built the most important asset in the music business. Bigger than any radio station. More important than any magazine.**" When Basa profiles a new artist, she becomes a superstar; **every Friday morning the playlist is updated, and by the end of the day the landscape of hit music has changed.** Conclusion: "**Spotify doesn't need to own radio spectrum, or a magazine. They own a PERMISSION ASSET instead. Permission, attention, and enrollment drive commerce.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 17]

**"Our funnel converts poorly."** The funnel mathematics on the real example **Casey Neistat**: A video with about **1,000,000** views led to a Twitch livestream that was watched **18,000** times (**about one in fifty**); there were about **1,000** comments (**one in eighteen**); and of the commenters **perhaps five** will go further. **The chain: 1,000,000 to 18,000 to 1,000 to 5. "That's what a funnel looks like. Your mileage may vary."**
**The decisive reading that turns the optimisation question around: "The reason that he's Casey and we're not ISN'T BECAUSE HE HAS OPTIMIZED HIS FUNNEL. It's because the TOP of the funnel is regularly and effortlessly filled with people who are ENROLLED IN HIS JOURNEY. Everything gets better once you earn that trust."** [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

**"Can brand advertising effect be measured?" — THE MAIN CONTRADICTION OF THE SYSTEM, to be kept open.**
- **TIM position (instruction to act):** "Brand marketing is culturally oriented. **And it CANNOT be measured.**" Hence: "**refuse to measure**", be patient and consistent, and if you cannot, don't run it at all. [Source: TIM, ch. 15]
- **KOB position (method canon):** four families of measurement, each with named validity limits (learning, attitudes, implicit, choice), plus the explicit recommendation to rely more on **implicit measures**, because they capture "what people have learned from the advertising **even though they may have forgotten where they got the information.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 7 — details in `measurement`]
- **The point that sharpens the contradiction: KOB itself supplies the explanation for why Godin's refusal to measure is practically plausible** — the recall-underestimation finding ("people often have difficulty tracing the origin of their knowledge") and the established-brand finding ("when brands are well-established, it is unlikely that one or even several exposures will affect people's judgments"). **But KOB draws the opposite conclusion: not to stop measuring, but to measure BETTER.**
- **Both positions are tenable and answer different questions:** Godin writes for actors without a research budget, for whom measurement would cost more than the insight gained; KOB for brand managers with access to copy-testing services and reaction-time methods. **Can be named as a synthesis, not as a decision.** See contradiction register.

**"And what if nothing may be free?"** Two reasons against fully free: (1) "**Engaging in a transaction is fundamentally different than encountering an apparently worthless (or at least priceless) object that's been freely shared. Scarcity, tension, and enrollment ALL exist when we must decide to make a purchase, and the marketer SACRIFICES ALL OF THESE when a purchase is truly free.**" (2) Without cash flow, no investment in product, team or marketing.
**But free is a category of its own: "Free is not simply a penny less than a penny. It's an ENTIRELY DIFFERENT CATEGORY of transaction, because LIKE DIVIDING BY ZERO, IT SCALES TO INFINITY."** Evidence: "**If Facebook cost three dollars a month to use, it would have attracted fewer than a million users. If it cost money to listen to the hits on the radio, the Top 40 would disappear.**"
**The resolution — two offers married to each other: "(1) FREE IDEAS THAT SPREAD. (2) EXPENSIVE EXPRESSIONS of those ideas that are worth paying for."** Evidence: The cook gives away recipes, appears on podcasts, leads online seminars — "**but if you want to eat that pasta served on china on a white tablecloth at her restaurant, it's going to cost you twenty-four dollars.**" If the song on the radio is free but the concert ticket costs eighty-four dollars, the artist gets paid. **"The china and the ticket are SOUVENIRS OF IDEAS, and souvenirs are supposed to be expensive."** [Source: TIM, ch. 16 — more in `pricing`]

## Concrete examples
- **Penguin Magic** as a model for spread built into the product: Every trick is demonstrated by video, **without revealing the trick — that creates tension**; anyone who wants the secret has to buy. **Over one billion video views at zero distribution cost.** Audience insight: Professionals hardly buy (ten to twenty tricks are enough, the audience changes), **amateurs always have the same audience and constantly need new material.** Peer reviews by other magicians, **over 82,000 product reviews**, which shrank the product cycle from years to about one month (**over 16,000 items**). Plus **300 lectures ("the TED Talks of magic") and about 100 live conventions.** "**The more magicians learn from each other, the more likely that Penguin will do well.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 1]
- **Slack as a built pattern interrupt:** Hardly anyone has the pattern of changing the way the whole day is worked. "And yet Slack is the fastest-growing product of its kind. **How? Because after capturing the energy and affection of some neophiliacs, THE RATCHET KICKED IN. Using Slack is better when your coworkers use it.** So, existing users have a powerful **SELFISH** reason to tell the others, and in fact, every day they don't is painful for them." The interrupt for the newcomer is a sentence about colleagues: "**You're missing out.**" — "Every day you're not on Slack, people at work are talking about you behind your back, working on projects without you, having conversations you're excluded from. **You can release that tension, right now, simply by signing in.**" **"That single horizontal transmission built a multi-billion-dollar software company. It's not accidental. IT'S BUILT INTO THE SOFTWARE ITSELF."** [Source: TIM, ch. 10]
- **"How do I get an agent?"** — the reversal of the channel question. **Brian Koppelman** is quoted: It doesn't work that directly; the agent takes calls, "but he's not going to become your full-time sales rep". **"The method isn't to go out and find an agent. THE METHOD IS TO DO WORK SO IMPOSSIBLY MAGICAL THAT AGENTS AND PRODUCERS COME LOOKING FOR YOU."** And: "In fact, the approach works best if it's **not** a fully polished and complete creation." [Source: TIM, ch. 21]
- **NetZero as a practitioner case for channel work under resource scarcity:** Starting budget **under 5 million USD**; "**due to our limited cash, we could not afford to spend the requisite $750,000 to $1 million for producing high-impact television spots.**" AOL held over 50 percent of the dial-up market at **21.95 USD per month**, competitors were between 19.95 and 21.95 USD for the same basic service, **which NetZero offered for free.** The IPO raised **184 million USD.** Later the switch to a paid model: **NetZero Platinum at 9.95 USD** for unlimited access without banner advertising, rolled out via the **NBA playoffs and finals on NBC**, with complete changeover of all TV spots — "**from a brand that had previously been 100 PERCENT FREE**". Then **NetZero HiSpeed at 14.95 USD**, "almost 40 percent cheaper than AOL ... and faster as well". **Result at the end of 2004: aided brand awareness 87 percent**; United Online with revenues of almost 500 million USD and profits above 100 million USD. [Source: KOB, ch. 14]
- **Creative attention beyond classic media:** **Red Bull enlisted influential students to promote it; BMW's Mini mounted one of its cars on the roof of a large SUV and drove through big cities.** [Source: KOB, introduction]

## Open points (contradiction register)
The following points are **documented, not resolved.** Full evidence in the register in `../research/system-overview.md`. For requests that touch them, **both positions** are to be presented.

- **A2 Can brand advertising effect be measured?** TIM's refusal to measure versus KOB's four families of measurement. Main methodological treatment in `measurement`.
- **B4 Internal work versus advertising effect:** KOB ch. 12 puts internal anchoring above advertising and warns of the seduction of new campaigns — in a book with a complete chapter on advertising effect.
- **C3 TIM calls almost all TV advertising semiotic noise and celebrates two TV and mass-advertising cases in ch. 13.**
- **Data currency as an open point:** KOB's channel figures are from 2005, TIM's from 2018. For programmatic, retail media and attribution after the end of cookies there is no material.
