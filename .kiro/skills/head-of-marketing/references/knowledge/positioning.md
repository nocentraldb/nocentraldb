# Positioning and Differentiation

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Tybout/Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018.

## Character of the sources for this topic area
**KOB is clearly the denser and more precise source here**: four named positioning components, a model statement, the distinction between feature frames and goal frames, points of parity, laddering up and down, reframing, and **systematically documented failures with figures** (TiVo, Envoy, Dash, Reebok, Miller Lite, FreshPrep). **TIM is the denser source for the courage to have an edge and for the critique of the positioning tradition itself** — and in doing so attacks the school in whose line KOB ch. 1 stands. This tension is the core of this topic area and is deliberately not smoothed over.
**KOB is from 2005**, TIM from 2018. **TIM carries a conflict of interest** (seminar sales).

## Core concepts and definitions

### The definition
"**Brand positioning refers to the specific, intended meaning for a brand in consumers' minds.** More precisely, a brand's positioning articulates **the goal that a consumer will achieve** by using the brand and explains **why it is superior** to other means of accomplishing this goal." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
Addressee: The statement is shared inside the firm and with partners to create a common vision and a guideline for tactical thinking. "**Consumers are not expected to read the positioning statement.** Rather, the consumer will see the end results — the brand design, pricing, communications, and channels of distribution." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

### The four components
1. **Target** — a short description of the target customers by identifying characteristics, **demographics and psychographics** (activities, interests, opinions), chosen on the basis of category and brand usage.
2. **Frame of reference** — the goal of the target group that brand consumption serves. It guides the choice of target group, identifies usage situations and **defines the relevant competitors** (brands that claim to serve the same goal).
3. **Point of difference** — why the brand is superior to alternatives **within this frame**.
4. **Reasons to believe** — evidence. "This final element is more important when the claims are relatively **abstract** (credence claims) versus **concrete** (verifiable), because concrete claims often are their own reason to believe." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

**The model statement (DeWalt by Black & Decker):**
> "To the tradesman who uses his power tools to make a living and cannot afford downtime on the job **(target)**, DeWalt professional power tools **(frame of reference)** are more dependable than other brands of professional power tools **(point of difference)** because they are engineered to the brand's historic high-quality standards and are backed by Black & Decker's extensive service network and guarantee to repair or replace any tool within 48 hours **(reasons to believe)**."
Communicative condensation: "No downtime with DeWalt." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

**Target-group dependence demonstrated:** For the do-it-yourselfer, frame and differentiation would have to change — "To the Do-It-Yourselfer who takes pride in achieving a professional result ... DeWalt power tools are superior ... because they are engineered for and chosen by tradesmen, who depend on their tools to make a living." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

### The three positioning questions (compact version)
**(1) Who is to be addressed? (2) Which goal does the brand allow the target to achieve? (3) Why should the brand be chosen over others that achieve the same goal?** [Source: KOB, ch. 1; origin: Keller/Sternthal/Tybout, HBR September 2002]

### Points of parity — the term that is entirely missing in TIM
"The features that are shared by members within a category are referred to as **points of parity**." They can represent the frame of reference itself if the customer knows the relationship between a bundle of features and the category but not the brand: Subway's fast service, many locations and low prices suggest fast food. [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
**The rule of caution:** "If a brand does not possess attributes associated with a category, obviously it cannot use parity to align itself with that category." Evidence **Motorola Envoy**: It served the goal of wireless communication but was **as large as a VHS cassette and initially cost USD 1,500** — "it did not belong in the pager category, **and consumers knew it**." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### Frame of reference: features or abstract goals
**A) Via product features.** Three routes: claim category membership (DeWalt = professional power tools; Coca-Cola = soft drinks; Subway = fast food); choose a **specific competitor as the gold standard** (DeWalt against Makita, which at launch was widely regarded as the tradesman's brand; Subway against McDonald's); or present points of parity.
**The exceptional case without feature overlap:** **Invisalign** used the **traditional dental brace** as its frame. Hardly any feature similarity, but consumers associate braces with the goal of a perfect smile; Invisalign promised it more effectively via clear, invisible plastic aligners. **The named risk: "The lack of feature similarity allowed orthodontists who were skeptical about Invisalign to question whether the system would in fact achieve the same goal."** [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

**B) Via abstract consumer goals.** Sensible **when the brand is established in its category.** After establishment, DeWalt could have used an emotional frame: "Tradesmen (like most people) seek the acceptance and regard of their peers" — implemented as a construction-site scene in which tradesmen ask the alpha male, who uses DeWalt, for advice. "**Having the 'right' tools would help to achieve this goal, just as would driving the right truck, or hanging out at the right bar.**"
**Additional strategic benefit:** A goal-based frame finds competitors **beyond the category**. Coca-Cola's goals (refreshed, sociable) are also served by bottled water and sports drinks. [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

### The central warning: frame NEW products concretely
"**When introducing a new product, a frame of reference based on abstract consumer goals is likely to be inappropriate.** Framing the brand concretely using other products and product features is necessary because **consumers learn about new brands by relating them to familiar ones.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
Chain of evidence:
- **TiVo (negative):** In 1999 Forrester predicted "These hard-drive machines will take off faster than any other consumer electronics product has before" with over 50 percent household penetration by 2005. **In fact, in January 2005 only 2.3 million households, just under 2 percent, had TiVo.** Positioned on the abstract goal "viewer freedom", with ads that remove parking meters and drive the wrong way down one-way streets. The slogan "TiVo, TV your way" showed that it was about television, "**but it was a mystery to consumers just how TiVo accomplished this goal.**" The authors' recommendation: comparison with the **home video category**, which represents the freedom to choose one's film whenever one wants.
- **Envoy (negative):** abstract frame "staying in touch while on the go". Failed "because consumers did not understand Envoy's role in relation to the many other products they might use to stay in touch" (pagers, mobile phones, e-mail).
- **Palm Pilot (positive):** the same product type, but launched with **electronic organizers as the concrete frame**, and credible because it served only their core functions (address book, calendar, to-do list). **Result: over one million units in 18 months.** [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
**The abstraction rule by decision type:** For the strategic plan, abstract and visionary wording is allowed; **for execution you need target group, product category and point of difference** — because the retail trade has to decide where the brand goes on the shelf, and the consumer has to find it. Evidence **Kraft FreshPrep (2003)**: "Both grocers and consumers were uncertain about whether the product belonged at the meat counter, in the deli case, or in the dairy section, and this confusion was a contributing factor in the product's failure." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

### Differentiation: functional and emotional benefits
**Functional benefits:** Gillette differentiated via a closer, more comfortable shave; BIC via economy ("a good (enough) shave more conveniently and less expensively").
**Four forms of reasons to believe:**
1. **Tangible product features.** In 2005 Gillette's M3 Power was the only wet razor with battery-powered vibration; DeWalt's service network and the 48-hour promise; Subway's nutrition information in stores and on napkins; Wal-Mart's remote locations and plain atmosphere.
2. **Technically irrelevant features.** "The product attributes presented as reasons to believe a functional benefit are **not always technically relevant**." **Alberto-Culver added real silk to Silkience shampoo** — "although adding silk was irrelevant to how silky it left hair, it reinforced the association between silkiness and the shampoo in consumers' minds." **Folgers** supported taste superiority with "mountain grown" — "the claim was accurate but **largely irrelevant**, because most coffee is mountain grown." Scientific basis: Carpenter/Glazer/Nakamoto (1994), "Meaningful Brands from Meaningless Differentiation: The Dependence on Irrelevant Attributes", *Journal of Marketing Research* 31, 339-350. [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
   **TIM fundamentally contradicts this practice** (see below and the contradiction register).
3. **Brand image via users.** Tiger Woods for Nike as a golf expert; a spokesperson known for being stingy (Jack Benny, Minnie Pearl) for economy.
4. **Usage occasions.** Wine at a wedding (Paul Masson) supports performance; feeding a horde of teenagers (Wal-Mart) supports economy. [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
**Emotional benefits:** "shift the emphasis from the brand and its functions to the user and the feelings to be gained". Linked to a functional benefit (McDonald's advertised cleanliness and taste to imply fun) or independent of it (Himalaya perfume: refreshing and tempting). **Badge brands** via self-presentation: Harley-Davidson (strong, rebellious, independent, plus membership in the Harley Owners Group), Abercrombie (peer acceptance), the Tiffany-blue box on Valentine's Day. **Inward-directed:** Starbucks regulars order highly personalised ("one Venti, decaf, skim cappuccino, dry") in comfortable armchairs with soft jazz — "a trip to Starbucks promises self-expression and self-indulgence in an otherwise unfulfilling day." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

### Laddering — and its limit
**Laddering down:** several reasons to believe for the same functional benefit. **Pantene** (taken over by P&G in the early 1990s) supported "healthiest hair" not only with **ProV** but also with different shampoos for softer or thicker hair. **Within a few years Pantene became the market share leader of the category.**
**Laddering up:** from tangible feature via functional to emotional benefit. "**A ladder is established with tangible features at the bottom** that offer a reason to believe the functional benefit, which indicates **what the brand does** for the consumer. In turn, the functional benefit provides a basis for inferring emotional benefits, which describe **how the functional benefit makes the consumer feel.**" The strategic point: "a brand may distinguish itself from competitors **even if other brands eventually achieve attribute parity.**"
**THE LIMIT that matters in the system:** "**Laddering does not imply that the goal is necessarily to move to the top of the ladder.** For some products, most consumers are unlikely to develop a deep emotional attachment." Evidence **light bulbs**: perceived functionally, hence a functional benefit (convenience through long life), supported by several reasons to believe (**Philips Halogena with a two-year guarantee**) or a modern presentation (**Philips showed light bulbs that outlast a young man's four years of study**). And conversely: "In other instances, **only** the emotional benefit might be appropriate — image products such as luxury goods and fragrances are not marketed on the basis of tangible features or functional benefits." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
**This is a substantial restriction of scope relative to TIM's generalisation "We sell feelings, status, and connection, not tasks or stuff" (TIM, ch. 7).** The generalisation does not hold everywhere.

### The XY grid of extremes — TIM's only positioning tool of its own
Procedure: draw a simple XY grid, plot **every available alternative** (the author expressly does not yet call them "competitors"), and for each axis choose something that matters to people.
**The model case:** Getting diamonds across town, axes speed and security. Registered mail and an armoured truck both insure a small envelope, but one takes a long time, the other an afternoon; FedEx and UPS lie in between; a bicycle courier is faster without security; a postage stamp is neither. "**The magic of the XY positioning of extremes is that it clarifies that each option might be appropriate, depending on what you seek.**" The same map would look completely different with the axes convenience, cost, environmental impact or scalability.
**The list of axes to choose from:** Speed, Price, Performance, Ingredients, Purity, Sustainability, Obviousness, Maintenance costs, Safety, Edginess, Distribution, Network effect, Imminence, Visibility, Trendiness, Privacy, Professionalism, Difficulty, Elitism, Danger, Experimental, Limited, Incomplete. [Source: TIM, ch. 5]
**The axis-choice rule: "Choose your axes, choose your future."** The temptation is to take the axes that matter to most people — then you land in a crowded quadrant, and **"without the magic of advertising, it's very difficult to grow in a quadrant that's crowded. Your customer doesn't know what to do, so he does nothing."** Alternative: **find two overlooked axes and build your own quadrant.** The others clump together as "Oldsmobile and Plymouth and Chevrolet and the rest of the lumpenproletariat". [Source: TIM, ch. 5]
**The music-teacher case, which shows the price of the edge:** "I'm local" is not enough; "I'm pretty good" and "I don't shout at your child" are not attributes worth mentioning. If he instead chooses axis 1 "I'm serious, my students are serious, it's about rigour" and axis 2 "My students win competitions", the result is a teacher you drive to and pay more for. **The price: "he does, in fact, have to be more rigorous and professional than other teachers. He does have to make the difficult decision of expelling students who aren't serious enough. And he has to persevere enough ... that they actually do win competitions."** A few blocks away another teacher can occupy the whole student, experience instead of grades and connection instead of competition. "**Both teachers treat different people differently. They don't compete; they're simply on the same board.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 5]

### "It's not for you" as a positioning discipline
"'It's not for you' shows the ability to respect someone enough that you're not going to waste their time, pander to them, or insist that they change their beliefs." And the reality check: **Every Amazon bestseller has some one-star reviews** — "it's impossible to create work that both matters and pleases everyone."
**The comedian's dilemma** as a diagnostic tool: A great comedian bombs completely until he discovers that the audience was an Italian tour group with no English. "It's entirely possible that your work isn't as good as it needs to be. **But it's also possible that you failed to be clear about who it was for in the first place.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
**The three-sentence promise as a positioning template:** "My product is for people who believe ____ / I will focus on people who want ____ / I promise that engaging with what I make will help you get ____" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **Repositioning is the exception, not the rule:** "It is difficult to change the position of a well-established brand. **Most efforts to reposition a long-lived brand fail.**" Evidence: **Dash** (positioned on low-suds front-loader performance; when it lost popularity as a front-loader, the repositioning on deodorising failed — "years of positioning Dash as a low-suds detergent could not be overcome"); **Reebok** (positioned as a women's sports shoe with comfort — "despite extensive marketing efforts over the past 20 years to change that image by using male celebrities and depicting athletic male pursuits, Reebok's persona has remained feminine"). [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
- **Hold the position rather than change it: modern instantiation.** Evidence **Special K**: historically "the healthy way to stay fit", with fitness long defined as slim. In the face of growing unease about the objectification of women, **the benefit was kept but redefined: athletic and active instead of thin**, personified by Cindy Crawford. "In effect, Special K's benefit was **not changed**. But it received a contemporary depiction." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
- **Communication alone is not enough when the cause lies in the product.** Evidence **milk**: Per-capita consumption fell; in the mid-1990s a celebrity campaign with the milk moustache ran, and in California the "Got milk?" campaign, which became so popular that it was exported to other countries. "**As creative as these campaigns were, they weren't ultimately successful in reversing the per capita decline.** People already knew that milk was nutritious. They were avoiding milk because it contained fat." The turn came via the marketing mix: contemporary packaging and then **low-fat, vitamin-enriched milk that tasted good** — "the decline in consumption began to slow by 2003." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
- **Reframing on second entry:** When Miller introduced the first light beer, the frame was regular beer and the point of difference "less filling". With Bud Light's entry a decade later, **"less filling" became a point of parity and thereby the frame of the new light-beer category.** "Bud Light's entry turned Miller Lite's point of difference into a point of parity." **Miller's failure to reframe is part of the explanation for the sales decline** — "focusing on this parity claim provided Budweiser with the opportunity to distinguish itself by claiming that Bud Light was the superior-**tasting** light beer, using the Budweiser heritage as the reason to believe." **Rule: "Reframing is necessary when a pioneer brand is faced with a viable second entrant into a category."** [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
- **Multiple target groups with caution:** "A common growth strategy is to seek additional targets when demand within the initial target becomes saturated." But: "**Although DIYs may wish to identify with professional tradesmen, the reverse is unlikely to be true.**" The documented case: Tradesmen were the target group of the Black & Decker professional tools, consumers of the less robust line **and of the small appliances (popcorn makers, waffle irons, Dustbuster)**. "As consumers' acceptance grew, **tradesmen's acceptance declined.** Apparently, tradesmen preferred power tool brands (such as Makita and Milwaukee) **that were not in their customers' tool boxes or their wives' kitchens.** Black & Decker only regained its dominant position with tradesmen when it launched a separate (DeWalt) brand targeted solely at tradesmen." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
- **Positioning as an execution guideline, not a slogan.** Evidence **Hallmark**: The point of difference "superior quality" guides the choice of materials (quality paper and verses), price (high), distribution (Gold Crown Stores), advertising (carefully made two-minute emotional spots) and media (quality family programming). The frame "communicating sentiments" gives a direction of growth: **alongside greeting cards also flowers, sweets and stuffed animals.** "Effective positioning not only charts the strategy a brand pursues, but **directs the choices among alternative ways to execute** the strategy." [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
- **Frame of reference as a lever (practitioner's view):** Three techniques — **broad category definition** (American Express reached broader acceptance via a broader frame), **gold standard comparisons**, and **"I am what I'm not"**. And the appropriation technique: **Tropicana "effectively co-opted this benefit as its own"** — claiming a category benefit as its own point of difference. [Source: KOB, ch. 15]
- **Problem first, not solution first:** "Effective marketers don't begin with a solution, with the thing that makes them more clever than everyone else. Instead, we begin with a group we seek to serve, a problem they seek to solve, and a change they seek to make." [Source: TIM, ch. 6]
- **Quality is the entry condition, not differentiation:** "**Quality, the quality of meeting specifications, is required but no longer sufficient.**" Historical reasoning: In 1906 the forerunner of the FDA arose against products such as Berry's Freckle Ointment and LashLure (which caused more than a dozen forms of blindness); until fifty years later product quality was a gamble. Today FedEx delivers over 99 percent on time, cars do not break down spontaneously, make-up rarely blinds. And the author's express demarcation: "**If you can't deliver quality yet, this book isn't much help to you.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 6]

## Typical problems and approaches

**"Our new product is not understood."** First check: Is the frame of reference concrete? For new products an abstract goal frame is, according to KOB, the most likely error (TiVo, Envoy versus Palm Pilot). Second check: Does the product possess the points of parity that make the claimed category membership credible (Envoy did not)? Third check: Does the retail trade know which shelf it belongs on (FreshPrep)? [Source: KOB, ch. 1]

**"We have become interchangeable."** TIM: You look for a point on the map **with edges**, expressly **not** a self-referential USP for maximising market share, but "**a generous beacon, a signal flare sent up so that people who are looking for you can easily find you**". [Source: TIM, ch. 5] KOB: A me-too with a lower price aggravates the problem, because "the me-too brand derives its identity from the pioneer, and greater similarity reduces the distinctiveness of the me-too brand" (see `diffusion`).

**"Better" cannot be defined.** TIM: "**Better isn't transitive.** Hermès is more expensive than Louis Vuitton, which is more expensive than Coach. But that doesn't mean that the Hermès bag is 'better'." And: "Better isn't up to you. ... We remember the best one. Best **for what**? And that's the key question. **Best for us.**" Evidence: In Cleveland there are over 250 motorcycle models for sale, "**can you name them? No one can, not even a motorcycle collector.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 5]
And the user side: "**Better is up to the users, not up to you.** Google was better than Bing and Yahoo! ... The search results weren't obviously better. The search itself wasn't dramatically faster. **What was better was that the search box didn't make you feel stupid. Yahoo! had 183 links on their home page. Google had two.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 6]

**"We are afraid to commit."** KOB names the organisational-political reason: "not everyone in the firm may want the greater direction and focus that positioning statements provide. Some may want to avoid positioning statements, or to write only **vague** ones, because they fear that customers might find out the supplier does not consider them the targets. **They would rather have the customers, and their own sales force, believe that all customers are equally good prospects!** This not only dilutes sales force efforts but can also have negative consequences when customers purchase an offering that was not designed to meet their requirements." [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
TIM names the psychological reason: With mass appeal the "no" becomes deafening, and the temptation is "to sand off the edges and fit in. Fit in all the way. Fit in more than anyone else. **Resist. It's not for them.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]

**"May our reasons to believe be technically irrelevant?"** **Here the sources contradict each other directly, and the contradiction stays open.**
- **KOB position:** The practice demonstrably works and is documented peer-reviewed (Silkience with real silk, Folgers "mountain grown"; Carpenter/Glazer/Nakamoto 1994). It is described, not criticised. And ch. 2 additionally concedes that **strong brand design can carry a weak product** — "it might be possible to cost-reduce the product quality of Philadelphia and maintain the brand concept". [Source: KOB, ch. 1 and ch. 2]
- **TIM position:** Ogilvy, Reeves, Trout and Ries "figured out a hole in the market and then simply **invented** claims and features that would fill that hole. So, one soap is for people who want purity, while another is for people who care about not having dry skin. **It didn't matter if the soaps were the same**, since they were 'positioning' themselves." Verdict: "**This is all fine, but it doesn't hold up over time, not in a hyper-competitive world.**" Replacement criteria: "Claims that are **true**, that we continually double down on in all our actions" and "Claims that are **generous**, that exist as a service to the customer." [Source: TIM, ch. 5]
- **To note in every application:** KOB's position is empirically supported, TIM's is normative and unproven. Conversely, in its reading list TIM recommends precisely Trout/Ries' "Positioning" and Ogilvy and Hopkins and thanks Trout and Ries by name — **an internal contradiction in TIM, see register.**

## Concrete examples
- **Dunkin' Donuts versus Starbucks:** Both sell coffee, but for **two decades Starbucks did not try** to sell to Dunkin' customers and vice versa. The distinction was not external (in Boston more taxi drivers and construction workers at Dunkin') but internal: "a very precise set of beliefs about coffee, time, money, community, opportunity, and luxury." [Source: TIM, ch. 4]
- **Trident Booksellers and Café, Boston:** No matter how cheap and large Amazon became — "**they do something Amazon can't. They serve coffee.**" For every retailer with online competition "... and we serve coffee" is not a bad slogan, because coffee creates a **third place**. "And so Trident is actually a coffee shop that sells books. **The books we just bought are a souvenir of the personal connections we made today.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 6]
- **The shoeshine:** Nobody **needs** shined shoes, it is a want. The possible stories: look good like your father or like Michael Jackson; the status of being served (once a week on a throne while a well-dressed, respectful craftsman takes care of your appearance); or a signifier ("people like him are supposed to do that — and not any shoeshine. **This** shoeshine, in **this** public spot, from **this** respected craftsperson"). **And the duty that follows: "Knowing that this is the story your customer tells himself is insufficient. You still have to act on it, open the door to the possibility, and organize the entire experience around that story."** [Source: TIM, ch. 6]
- **Stack Overflow versus Experts Exchange:** The predecessor hosted programming answers behind a USD 300 annual barrier and **showed the answers to the Google robots but scrambled them for human visitors** — "Experts Exchange created **profit via frustration**." Joel Spolsky and Jeff Atwood made questions and answers visible and financed everything via **job ads** — "what better place to find great programmers than a website where great programmers come to ask questions?" And they treated three groups differently: programmers in a hurry (answers sorted by quality), answerers (community, ranking, levels, power over the community), job-ad customers (quick self-service, no hard sell). [Source: TIM, ch. 6]
- **Bernadette Jiwa's ten things that good stories do** (referenced in TIM): connect with purpose and vision; celebrate strengths; deepen the understanding of unique value; reinforce core values; enable value-based decision making; **respond to customers instead of reacting to the market**; attract customers with the same values; build brand loyalty and give customers a story to tell; attract like-minded employees; keep them motivated. **To note: Jiwa is named in TIM ch. 2 as one of the author's teachers — the list comes from the work of a companion, not from independent research.** [Source: TIM, ch. 6]

## Connections to other topic areas
- **brand-meaning**: The brand concept must be articulated before cues can implement it; according to KOB ch. 2, positioning is only a **description format** for the concept.
- **audience**: The target component and the question of which goal counts at all lie there.
- **diffusion**: Reframing on second entry, the me-too paradox and the differentiation distance to the pioneer belong there.
- **architecture**: Positioning decides stretchability; fit is tested against target, frame, point of difference and reasons to believe.
- **pricing**: "Your price should be aligned with the extremes you claimed as part of your positioning" (TIM, ch. 16).
- **channels**: Positioning guides media and message choice; the advertising chapter presupposes an existing position.
