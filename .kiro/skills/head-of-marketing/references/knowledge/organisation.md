# Organisation, Employees and Service

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Tybout/Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018.

## Character of the sources for this subject area
**KOB is the dominant source here, with five chapters plus two cross-sectional parts,** and supplies a complete implementation apparatus: consistency as one of the three basic challenges, the touchpoint logic, a three-phase assimilation programme, six guiding principles with documented evidence, C-level roles and five named pitfalls. **TIM addresses the same commitment question from the other side**: Godin organises the **customer community**, KOB the **company's own workforce** — and both arrive at the same core rule (commitment over time instead of a campaign).
**Important caveat on conflicts of interest: KOB's practitioner chapters (14 to 20) are the only place in the system where authors report with their own figures on their own decisions — but all seven report on the success of their own organisation, without a control group and without a dissenting voice.** And ch. 12 comes from a consultant whose firm sells exactly these programmes. **TIM carries a conflict of interest** (seminar sales).

## Core concepts and definitions

### Consistency as one of the three basic challenges of branding
"The second great challenge of branding is **consistency**, or getting an entire organization to embrace the brand and **live up to the brand promise over time.** Crafting the perfect brand positioning and developing the ideal brand portfolio are both noble tasks. **However, if the organization doesn't understand, believe in, and OWN the brand — if the message, the brand, and the product are not consistent — THE VISION WILL REMAIN UNFULFILLED.**" [Source: KOB, introduction]
**The touchpoint logic: "Brands are created through a wide range of touch points; EVERY TIME customers interact with a brand they form associations. This means that ALMOST EVERYONE IN A COMPANY HAS AN IMPACT ON THE BRAND, from the receptionist to the advertising manager to the customer service representative."**
**The quote from a marketing executive that shows the scope (the best single piece of evidence in the area):** "A brand is the feel of your business card, the way the company's phone is answered, **the assistant coordinator who's had one too many after work yet has handed out her business card while at the bar**, the disgruntled salesman who complains to his family and friends that the company he works for is really ripping people off, the tone of a letter, the employee who doesn't help the customer, **the vice president who tells too rude a joke in an inappropriate setting**, the package that's almost impossible to open, the receptionist who continues to chat with a fellow worker when a customer arrives, an over-long wait at the cash register, the instructions that are too hard to follow ... **The brand is EVERY touch point and EVERY thought the customer has about the brand.**" [Source: KOB, introduction]
**The positive evidence: "The Starbucks brand was NOT built through advertising. Indeed, the company did VIRTUALLY NO ADVERTISING FOR ITS FIRST 30 YEARS in the market. Starbucks was built through a series of outstanding experiences at STORE LEVEL. People developed a loyalty for the Starbucks brand, and this loyalty was created by DOZENS OF POSITIVE INTERACTIONS WITH STARBUCKS EMPLOYEES."**
**The negative evidence: Lands' End**, a direct marketer with a reputation for outstanding customer service, was bought **by Sears in 2002 for 1.9 billion USD**. "Sears quickly began selling Lands' End products in Sears stores. **However, the customer service provided by Sears was poor. This disappointed Lands' End customers and TARNISHED the once powerful Lands' End brand.**" [Source: KOB, introduction]

### The moment of truth
"**The true test of a brand lies LESS in a successful advertising campaign and MORE with the customer's direct interaction with the brand and the people representing it. In fact, HOW WELL EMPLOYEES FULFILL CUSTOMER EXPECTATIONS IS THE BRAND'S MOMENT OF TRUTH.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
**And the proportionality statement that gives the area its weight: "The degree to which you can expect to achieve EXTERNAL brand success is 100 PERCENT PROPORTIONAL to the degree to which you achieve INTERNAL brand success."** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
**TIM's version of the same insight: "In a world that SCANS instead of READS, that GOSSIPS instead of RESEARCHING, it turns out that the best way to earn trust is THROUGH ACTION. WE REMEMBER WHAT YOU DID LONG AFTER WE FORGET WHAT YOU SAID."** The test questions: "When we asked for a refund for a defective product, **what did you do**? When you lost our data, what did you do? **When you had to close the plant and our jobs were on the line, what did you do?**" And the consequence: "Marketers spend a lot of time talking, and on working on what we're going to say. **We need to spend far more time DOING.** Talking means focusing on holding a press conference for the masses. **Not talking means focusing on what you do WHEN NO ONE IS WATCHING, one person at a time, day by day.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 18]

### The double role of the brand, internal and external
Externally, the brand conveys the value to be expected. **Internally, it "becomes a BEACON for organizing the firm's activities."** Reasoning: "organizations consist of groups of individuals performing different functions ... **Salespeople seek volume, R&D pursues innovations, and finance seeks high return on investment. These differences, however important, CAN BE DIVISIVE as well. A brand, as a symbol of the value the organization is intent on delivering, CAN ALIGN INTERNAL EFFORTS and increase the impact of the organization's resources.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

### The four core differences of services
**Intangibility, Complexity, Heterogeneity** and **"Services Are Processes"**, with the **simultaneity of production and consumption.** The essence: "**It is well-known that a customer's PERSONAL EXPERIENCES with a company**" carry the brand. Three levers: **internal marketing, self-service** (with the recommendation that "**self-service technologies (SSTs) should also be evaluated**" as brand touchpoints) and **service networks.** [Source: KOB, ch. 10]
**The practitioner confirmation: "As a high-touch service organization, our brand is TIED TO THE EXPERIENCE."** [Source: KOB, ch. 18]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### The assimilation process — the ladder
From **Awareness ("Hear It")** via **Understanding** and **Acceptance ("Believe It")** to **Utilize and Internalize ("Live It")** and finally **Passionate Advocacy / Cultural Experience** — with the three guiding questions **"How do we best get it into their HANDS? ... into their HEADS? ... into their HEARTS?"** and the intermediate states **Ready to Promote / Ready to Defend.**
**The time horizon, explicitly named: "development of a brand assimilation plan that is implemented over an 18-MONTH TO TWO-YEAR PERIOD, and attention to the tactical components to ensure it is reinforced over the longer haul."**
**The precondition that is often missing: "effecting this sort of shift requires A REEVALUATION OF CURRENT SYSTEMS AND STRUCTURES that may present an impediment to success."**
**The first step: an employee-based segmentation, "MUCH LIKE YOU WOULD A CUSTOMER NEEDS-BASED SEGMENTATION".** Two possible axes: **by hierarchy level** or **by degree of customer interaction.** The rule: "**The more specific and tailored the behavior-change need is for each segment, the more beneficial it is to call that group out as a separate segment.**" Alternative when resources are scarce: "some firms have achieved success by executing broad education initiatives via **existing** communication vehicles or meeting schedules."
**The three phases:**
1. **Strategic Development** — design a framework that ensures the key segments adopt and understand strategy and positioning. If the internal cultural identity is not easy to communicate, "a means for codifying and communicating it should be developed through **employee focus groups or interviews**". A detailed **assimilation roadmap per segment** with goals, messages, vehicles and timing. Plus identification of the **change agents.**
2. **Foundation-Building** — "**this is the stage when much of the HEAVY LIFTING occurs.**" Education workshops with the key managers and change agents, "in order to secure buy-in and to increase understanding". **And the decisive step: "employees are also encouraged to ARTICULATE WHAT BRAND MEANS FOR THEIR RESPECTIVE AREAS."** Here it is understood "how employees' behaviors, activities, and mindsets **may need to be MODIFIED** to support the brand".
3. **Implementation** — communication, events and experiences: regional meetings, webcasts, conference calls, training, workshops, **games, competitions, team building.** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]

### The six guiding principles — each with documented evidence
**1. MAKE THE BRAND RELEVANT.** "Each employee in each functional group must understand not just what the brand stands for, **but how they as individuals can embrace its meaning and represent it publicly.** Only employees who understand the brand can help support it and **use it to guide decision making.**"
**Evidence Nordstrom:** known for a shopping experience based on outstanding service. "**Nordstrom shaped the relevance of such brand promises with employees by pioneering a unique policy: SALES ASSOCIATES WERE GIVEN THE AUTHORITY TO APPROVE CUSTOMER PURCHASE EXCHANGES WITHOUT THE STORE MANAGER'S APPROVAL. This policy helped employees understand how everyone played a role in shaping the customer experience, AND IT EMPOWERED THEM TO DO SO.**"
**2. MAKE THE BRAND ACCESSIBLE.** "Giving employees the ability to make brand-supporting decisions means that they must have **ready access to answers.** Without creating that kind of access, the organization risks creating employees who are **disinterested or frustrated** with the task."
**Evidence Ernst & Young:** "**The Branding Zone**", a central web-based information source on branding, marketing and advertising — "the site contained **a DISCUSSION AREA** where employees could access information on marketing strategy, **POSE QUESTIONS to its global branding and marketing team, and OFFER SUGGESTIONS.**" Further tools: **an elevator pitch (a two-minute description of what the brand stands for), wallet-sized brand cards, training decks.**
**3. REINFORCE THE BRAND CONTINUOUSLY.** "For brand to become a cultural underpinning, employees must be continuously exposed to its meaning, **FAR BEYOND the initial rollout.**"
**Evidence Southwest Airlines:** To apply the brand promise **"A symbol of freedom"** internally, the People department joined forces with PR and marketing in an internal campaign around **employee freedom** — "having the carrier's in-house publication **highlight employee freedoms**, having employees **write about how they personally took advantage of freedoms**, and **renaming the intranet site 'Freedom Net'.**"
**4. MAKE BRAND EDUCATION AN ONGOING PROGRAM.** "It's particularly important that NEW employees are grounded in the brand culture. ... Additionally, **the investment the company makes in training new employees SPEAKS VOLUMES ABOUT ITS LEVEL OF COMMITMENT TO THEM.**"
**Evidence Ritz-Carlton:** Every new employee went through an intensive orientation called **The Gold Standard**, whose principles were reinforced in **daily departmental meetings with all employees** and formed the basis of all further training. "**Not so coincidentally, Ritz-Carlton became a hospitality industry leader in training, providing 120 HOURS OF TRAINING PER EMPLOYEE PER YEAR.**"
**5. REWARD ON-BRAND BEHAVIORS.** "This not only helps create and maintain excitement, but it **underscores, through individual recognition, the kinds of behaviors that need to be supported.**"
**Evidence Continental Airlines:** the **"Working Together"** programme under the new brand promise of punctuality — "**each employee received $65 for every month that the airline ranked in the TOP FIVE on the U.S. Department of Transportation's list of on-time airlines.**" Further forms: linking to annual performance goals, and **employee-of-the-month awards for on-brand behaviour.**
**6. ALIGN HIRING PRACTICES.** "HR and marketing [must] work together to develop basic screening procedures that ensure new hires will **fit with and support the company's brand culture.**" And over time: "**job descriptions should be REWRITTEN to incorporate these same brand identity traits into the list of expected employee behaviors.**"
**Evidence Pret A Manger** (London sandwich chain): Not only were the selection procedures switched to on-brand cultural traits, **but also the hiring policy, "so that OTHER EMPLOYEES decided whether a store should extend an offer to a candidate. As such, the company only hired 20 PERCENT of all candidates, and it ensured that only those who shared its spirit became part of the team."** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]

### The role of top management, role by role
"Like any form of organizational change, affecting it **begins at the top and filters down.** ... it also requires marketing to play a leadership role by **STEPPING OUT OF ITS SILO** to form teams with various influential nonmarketers — from operations executives to the human resources decision-makers."
- **CEO** = "the company's **ultimate brand builder**". Evidence: **Cisco CEO John Chambers, asked who leads the brand there: "I do!"** Further CEO brand builders named: **Meg Whitman (eBay), Fred Smith (FedEx), Herb Kelleher (Southwest), Steven Jobs (Apple), Andy Grove (Intel), Jeff Bezos (Amazon), Howard Schultz (Starbucks), Richard Branson (Virgin Records).**
  **The most important finding about this group: "NONE of these individuals came into the job to build a brand; NONE came from a classical marketing position. Their common ground was the desire to build or strengthen a company and DIFFERENTIATE their organizations by creating a value proposition that could be delivered CONSISTENTLY and in a CUSTOMER-CENTRIC way."**
  And the limit of the role: "**since the CEO's reach into the organization is necessarily LIMITED, it's also incumbent on him or her to EMPOWER OTHER C-LEVEL EXECUTIVES AS CHANGE AGENTS.**"
- **COO** = "an increasingly critical **brand enabler**; this is the individual who **OWNS THE EMPLOYEE BASE** and thus has the ability and credibility to direct employees to bring the brand promise to life."
- **CFO** = holds the purse strings and "must understand not just the rationale for brand building, **but also the commensurate return on investment.**"
- **CIO** = controls information, systems and processes "to enable smart, brand-driven decisions."
- **Head of HR** = integral because of the cross-functional nature: "critical for his or her ability to **identify and reward** those employees who support the brand, to **build the brand into recruiting** initiatives, and to **facilitate the training process** throughout the organization."
- **CMO** = "the greatest enabler", but must learn to bring others along: "**framing the conversation in a JARGON-FREE context understood by all (potentially discussing CULTURAL PRACTICES rather than BRANDING APPROACHES)** and including others in any rewards and recognitions."
**The warning: "Without senior-level support and ACCOUNTABILITY across the organization, the brand strategy will likely be undersupported and assimilation WILL NOT TAKE ROOT. THE TALK MUST BE WALKED."**
**The evidence for visibly leading by example — Sheetz:** The seven most senior executives of the Pennsylvania convenience-store chain **dressed up as characters from "The Matrix" for the employee preview and went on a 26-region roadshow to present the new store concept "Sheetz Revolutionz" in person to 3,000 employees.** "**There is no more powerful way to prove to the rest of the organization the executives' investment in the branding process than to have the top seven personally carry the torch on the road.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 12]

### Executive brand councils and brand ambassadors
**EBCs:** a team of division and function heads with **cross-functional brand ownership**, serving as a **"virtual board of directors for the CMO"**. Charter: "to **guide and direct** the brand and to **manage brand impact**." Typical topics: "from **company name changes** to dealing with rationales and justifications for **acquisitions and divestitures of brands** to overseeing **new creative**."
**The signal effect that is the actual purpose: "EBCs are HIGHLY VISIBLE within the organization. As such, they help send a tangible message that brand management and ownership is an ORGANIZATION-WIDE responsibility — NOT JUST A MARKETING PRIORITY."**
Evidence **Visa**: the EBC represented the most important competitive markets and critical functional areas and met several times a year; "these meetings were **strategic, anchored around the business strategy**, and always focused on what Visa was trying to create with its brand **on a global basis.**"
**Brand ambassadors from the workforce:** "**As important as senior-level involvement is, it's EQUALLY important to identify and train key employees from the rank and file** who are best suited to becoming individual brand ambassadors. These employees are capable of imbuing the brand mindset through **the middle and lower reaches** of the organization. ... They can provide tremendous day-to-day insight." To be identified from every functional area and **brought together quarterly.** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]

### The five pitfalls — the most valuable part of the area
**1. The broadcast model.** "**The most common reason that internal branding programs do not effectively take hold is because they've been rooted in the BROADCAST — the internal communications model.** This means that the organization simply launches **brand roadshows** to get their message out to employees, **RATHER THAN SYSTEMATICALLY GOING ABOUT CREATING REAL BEHAVIORAL CHANGE.** While the brand is defined and communicated under this model, **the internal conditions, processes, and resources needed to ALLOW employees to deliver the corresponding experience HAVE NOT BEEN DEVELOPED.**" Addition: "this shortsighted approach **underestimates the TIME** that's involved to develop a genuine and ongoing commitment."
**2. Asymmetry of resources between outside and inside.** "Today's external marketing is all about intricate, segmented, and targeted communications, with considerable care given to global, cultural, and industry nuances. **But internal communications around brand tend to remain A BLUNT INSTRUMENT.** Employees are a different audience than customers, **but many of the core external marketing principles hold true in reaching them: audience segmentation, tailored messages, careful timing, multimedia channels, performance incentives, and technology utilization.**"
**3. The seduction of the campaign.** "Another reason an internal brand education may fail is **the seduction of sexy new ad campaigns, new names, or logos.** It's too easy and too common to use the EXTERNAL messaging as primary vehicles for INTERNAL brand education, **without communicating the implications in terms of employee behaviors** that will support the implicit promises of the campaign."
**With the B2B sharpening: "Particularly in the business-to-business realm, IT'S THE POST-PURCHASE USE AND SERVICE EXPERIENCE THAT CAN MAKE OR BREAK A BRAND (which hinges on the involvement and buy-in of internal employees), NOT the pre-purchase promises of advertising and imagery."**
**4. Lack of integration with other initiatives** (vision, mission, culture). "As a result, employees may well view brand building as **yet another FLAVOR-OF-THE-MONTH initiative.** Companies do a disservice to employees by **NOT CONNECTING THE DOTS for them** — they must show how the pieces fit together and clarify expectations."
**5. Overconfidence in technology.** Intranets and the web can be powerful, "**but if the technology is not supported by tools to support the vision or to TRANSLATE THE RECEIVED KNOWLEDGE INTO ACTIONABLE BEHAVIOR, then the medium only COMPOUNDS the problems of the broadcast model.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 12]

### The customer side: Godin's tribe leadership as the counterpart
- **"It's not your tribe."** The tribe does not belong to you; you may not tell the members what to do or use them for your own purposes. "**The tribe would probably survive if you went away. The goal is for them to MISS you if you did.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 20]
- **The connecting task: "Your opportunity as a marketer is the chance to CONNECT THE MEMBERS OF THE TRIBE. They're lonely and disconnected, they FEAR BEING UNSEEN, and you, as the agent of change, can make connection happen."** [Source: TIM, ch. 20]
- **The half-life rule: "There's a half-life at work. For ANY tribal behavior that's not energetically maintained, HALF OF THE ACTIVITY WILL DISAPPEAR. Every day, every month, every year."** The alternative: reinvest, "to have the guts to sit with those you have instead of always being distracted to chase the next thing". **Mnemonic: "The best marketers are FARMERS, not HUNTERS. Plant, tend, plow, fertilize, weed, repeat."** [Source: TIM, ch. 20]
- **The economy of commitment: "Most of all, the tribe is waiting for you to COMMIT. They know that most marketers are fly-by-night operators, knocking on doors and moving on. But some hunker down and commit. AND IN RETURN, THE TRIBE COMMITS TO THEM."** [Source: TIM, ch. 20]
- **The Ziglar case as evidence for presence instead of a campaign:** In the 1960s Zig Ziglar was a door-to-door salesman of pots and pans. **Most of his company's three thousand representatives** filled their car with samples, visited a town, made all the easy sales and moved on. **"Zig had a different strategy. He got in his car, found a new town, and MOVED IN. He took a room for weeks at a time. He showed up and KEPT SHOWING UP."** He made the same early-adopter sales as everyone else, "**but then people noticed HE DIDN'T LEAVE like all the other salespeople that they'd seen before. He stayed.**" Through ongoing demonstration dinners he could "engage with someone in the middle of the curve **five or six or seven times over the course of a month** — which is **precisely what this sort of person wants before they make a decision.**"
  **"Zig did the math. He understood that while most salespeople would FLEE when they hit the chasm, he could build A HUMAN BRIDGE. There'd be days with no sales at all, but that's okay, because after crossing the local chasm, the volume would more than make up for the time invested. THE EASY SALES AREN'T ALWAYS THE IMPORTANT ONES."** [Source: TIM, ch. 20]

### Emotional labour as the professional ethic of the employee side
"**Emotional labor is the work of doing what we don't feel like doing.** It's about showing up with a smile when we're wincing inside, or resisting the urge to chew someone out because you know that engaging with him will make a bigger difference."
**The myth Godin explicitly attacks: "the authentic, vulnerable hero ... THIS IS A MYTH. IT'S A DANGEROUS MYTH."** Evidence: **When James Brown fell to his knees exhausted and had to be revived by his helpers, that was "brilliant STAGECRAFT, not an authentic performance. AFTER ALL, IT HAPPENED EVERY NIGHT."** The therapist who listens patiently all day "actually might be patient, but it's more likely **he's simply doing his job**". The Starbucks barista who smiles and wishes you a good day: **"he's PRESENTING, not REVEALING."**
**The boundary: "Revealing is reserved for your family and your closest friends, NOT THE MARKETPLACE. Protect yourself. You'll be needed tomorrow."**
**The verdict: "If you NEED to be authentic to do your best work, you're not a professional, YOU'RE A FORTUNATE AMATEUR."** For everyone else: "the opportunity to be a professional, to exert emotional labor **in search of EMPATHY** — the empathy to imagine what someone else would want, what they might believe, what story would resonate with them. **We don't do this work because we FEEL LIKE IT in the moment. We do this work, this draining emotional labor, because we're professionals.**"
**And the concept of service that follows from it: "When we do the best version of our best work, our responsibility isn't to make it for OURSELVES ... it's to bring it to the person we seek to serve. Just as a three-star chef doesn't cook herself a twelve-course meal for dinner, YOU ARE NOT EXPECTED (OR WELCOME) TO BRING US EVERY ONE OF YOUR INSECURITIES, INNERMOST FEARS, AND URGENT DEMANDS. You're here to serve."** [Source: TIM, ch. 6]
**The signature test: "When you get an email from a faceless corporation, speaking in the second person, SOMEONE IS HIDING. It's slick, but it's not real. ... On the other hand, when a human being extends emotional labor to take responsibility — 'HERE, I MADE THIS' — then the door is open to connection and growth. The most effective organizations don't ALWAYS have a famous leader or a signature on every email. BUT THEY ACT LIKE THEY DO. The goal isn't to PERSONALIZE the work. It's to make it PERSONAL."** [Source: TIM, ch. 6]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **The brand as a decision lens, not as a communication topic.** "Making brand the central focus helps clarify **on-brand and off-brand behaviors and decisions** for all employees — whether they're in the field or in the executive suite." Three documented functions: **product development** (Nokia's brand historically dictated that phone design must be sleek and a point of difference from competitors); **pricing** (John Deere always charges a premium, "because its name is commensurate with the product's value and with the brand's promise"); **acquisitions** (FedEx's purchase of Kinko's in 2004, "aimed at helping FedEx expand the scope of what its brand stands for"). [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **The five benefits with which the programme is justified internally:** employees get **a tangible reason to believe**; they see **how they fit into the overall plan** and how their efforts meet the business goals; **pride** through fulfilling the brand promise; **stronger recruitment and retention**; and a shared customer and brand focus creates "a cohesive and productive environment." [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **The loyalty figures as an investment argument: "In some categories, an increase in customer loyalty of just 2 PERCENT is equivalent to a 10 PERCENT COST-REDUCTION PROGRAM, while an increase of 5 PERCENT in loyal customers can deliver 95 PERCENT GREATER PROFITABILITY over the lifetime of each customer. Moreover, customers are willing to pay AS MUCH AS 25 PERCENT MORE for a brand to which they have loyalty."** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **In customer service and service contacts, differentiate by customer value instead of "getting better" across the board.** "**Treating everyone better is a bit like treating everyone worse — given your resources, you can't treat everyone better than you already are.**" Instead: see who actually uses the platform, and invest there. For a customer with **eight times the average spend**, "the cost of being human ... easily covered by the upside of delighting an extraordinary customer" is justified — up to "**get in your car, drive across town, and show up**" or "**have the CEO pick up the phone.**" **"I know you can't do it for every customer. But you can learn to SEE and ACT accordingly."**
  **And the structural observation that explains the problem:** In banking and retail, **"the least-respected and lowest-paid person"** answers the phone — whereas for a freelancer with six clients: "when the big client calls, the freelancer INSTANTLY knows what's up." [Source: TIM, ch. 14]
- **Internal metrics must be tied to incentives.** Evidence **Itron**: one metric linked brand building to **profit sharing** — "employees created a plan for how they would live the brand, essentially setting up **A CONTRACT** with senior management ... At the end of the year, they were evaluated against this contract to determine what level of profit sharing they would receive." Second metric: regular measurement of **employee morale**, "based on the belief that brand-building initiatives should create a positive shift in employees' attitudes about and belief in the company." Results: morale and belief in the company "continued to reach **new highs**"; share price from **3.70 to over 18.50 USD**, market capitalisation from **200 to 388 million USD** — **with the explicit caveat that isolating the brand's share is generally difficult.** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **A practitioner finding on feasibility in small organisations:** "**the CLOSE-KNIT AND GEOGRAPHICALLY CONCENTRATED nature of our [firm]**" made implementation easier. And the core lesson of the same chapter: "**Manage the brand ACTIVELY with marketing professionals.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 19]
- **Create cultural artefacts on purpose — and with restraint.** Use status roles to elevate a costume, code words or the secret handshake. But: "**Don't say it all, and don't make it obvious. It's fine that there are secret handshakes, Easter eggs, and unknown features. It's fine that commitment and longevity earn an extra edge.**" And the challenge lever: When **Nike committed millions to Breaking2**, a moonshot to break the two-hour barrier in the marathon, "**they were engaging and challenging the tribe. EVEN IF THEY DON'T SUCCEED, they (and the tribe members who organize around them) will come out ahead.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 20]

## Typical problems and approaches

**"We have a new brand, but the employees do not carry it."** The worked-out case: **UBS, 9 June 2003.** The consolidation of all private bankers, wealth managers, asset managers and investment bankers acquired over ten years under the name UBS. Externally a multi-million-dollar campaign under **"You and Us"**; internally a simultaneous initiative — because in the USA the respected brand **Paine Webber** (acquired in 2000, briefly run as UBS Paine Webber) was **completely replaced.**
**The mechanism: After training on mission, vision and promise, EVERY financial advisor, money manager and financial planner in every office had ONE MONTH to contact EVERY client personally, explain the change and what it meant, and thank them for their loyalty. Incentive: 500 USD per broker to the charity of every office that reached 100 percent of its clients.**
**Results: over 4 million USD donated, plus "a wave of fresh cash flowed into UBS". In 2004 UBS was among the 50 most powerful brands in the world for the first time (Business Week).**
**The quote that names the incentive problem (Jestyn Thirkell-White, UBS): "In the wealth management business, our financial advisors are the KEY customer touch point. But the pressure to increase results can easily turn into a focus on bringing in NEW business. Brokers say, 'Why spend more time with one client when it's a numbers game? I need to be spending more time calling MORE PEOPLE to increase my base!' In fact, UBS's financial advisors found that by spending A LITTLE MORE TIME WITH THEIR EXISTING CLIENTS, they built the brand as standing for something more than just the stereotypical brokerage churning and burning, AND INCREASED BUSINESS LEVELS AT THE SAME TIME."**
**Note: empirically this is exactly Godin's superuser argument** (more time for the valuable existing customers instead of broad acquisition) **— with the addition that it worked AND increased volume.** A two-source convergence, not a contradiction. [Source: KOB, ch. 12; convergence with TIM, ch. 14]

**"We need a shared identity after a spin-off."** The case **Infineon, 1999**: Siemens spun off its semiconductor business. Workshops in Europe and the USA collected the defining attributes of the new firm, **"based on reality AS WELL AS ASPIRATIONS".** The name Infineon = a blend of *infinity*, *eternity* (eon, Greek) and *new* (neon, Greek).
**The launch as a culture catalyst: "To announce the new brand and use it as a catalyst for launching a new culture, Siemens organized A HUGE PARTY AT EACH OF ITS SITES WORLDWIDE, inviting the 25,000 Infineon employees to celebrate on March 26, 1999."** The CEO explained the name, senior managers spoke, a short spot introduced the tagline: **"Never stop thinking. Employees found the advertising tag line exciting and engaging BECAUSE IT REINFORCED THAT THE COMPANY VALUED AND ENCOURAGED THEIR THINKING."**
**Follow-up in both directions — methodologically the best thing about the case: internally, periodic qualitative and quantitative culture assessments on criteria such as internal communication, empowerment and the effect of one's own job on the company, documented in the report "Cultural Mirror of the Company", which was accessible to ALL employees; externally, an annual "Brand Tracking Survey" with customers AND managers of target companies (that is, potential customers).**
"The Infineon brand thus became **the guiding principle for internal coordination**, spanning the range of functions, and it also became **a symbol of the value customers could expect**." [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

**"How do you anchor a promise in a service organisation?"** The case **Northwestern Memorial HealthCare**: The brand anchoring was **"Patients First"**, materialised as a **lapel pin**; complemented by **"People Make the Difference"** with an annual review and goals for the next year, involving **100 percent of employees.** The redevelopment project was the implementation of the vision: **"The decision to construct a $600 MILLION [hospital]"** as an expression of the ambition. The external campaign ran under **"Enjoy Your Health"**.
**The result figures: admissions up 34 percent, emergency department visits up 27 percent, births up 47 percent — and at the same time "among the LOWEST-COST teaching hospitals".** [Source: KOB, ch. 18]

**"Our workforce is loyal, but the brand remains a marketing topic."** The case **UPS**: The goal was "**capturing the true business-building power of a brand by ENGAGING employees**"; UPS aimed at "a bigger opportunity: to establish **a deep portfolio of services**" beyond parcel delivery. The starting diagnosis: "**although UPS employees were loyal, hardworking, and [...]**" the brand connection was missing. The new brand promise became **"Synchronized Commerce"** — "In 2002, when the marketing team at UPS began the process of more thoroughly developing the company's brand". The sequence: **Marketing the Brand Promise to Employees -> Launching the Evolved Brand -> Launch Results ("this high-energy internal brand launch helped employees IMMEDIATELY [...]") -> Post-Launch Strategies.** [Source: KOB, ch. 20]

**"We have many touchpoints and do not know which ones count."** The answer is not a prioritisation but the phase logic. **Before the purchase**, "the initial phone call with a customer service representative that gains you more information" is "**JUST AS IMPORTANT AS the advertising or sponsorships** designed to build awareness." **At the purchase**, the salesperson's courtesy, speed and accuracy are "**MORE influential in shaping the customer experience than ALL THE BEST-DESIGNED point-of-purchase displays.**" **Afterwards**, the responsiveness of the call centre and "**the timely arrival of polite and efficient technicians**" are "**JUST AS INTEGRAL to cementing loyalty as are frequent buyer programs, user groups, or other value-added services.**"
And the widening of the circle: Whirlpool's touchpoint wheel lists, alongside customers, also **employers, company alumni and suppliers** as influencing touchpoints. [Source: KOB, ch. 12]

**"What does a customer experience look like as a continuous chain?"** The worked-out eight-stage decision process, phrased in the customer's voice (HP): **1. Aware** "I know who you are and what value you offer." **2. Choose** "I can easily find the right products and services for my needs." **3. Order** "I can get all HP products easily from one place at competitive prices." **4. Install** "I can easily integrate your solution into my business environment." **5. Learn** "You provide me with the information I need and allow me to learn." **6. Use** "Your solution is easy to use and manage within my business environment." **7. Support** "You provide me access to knowledge to quickly solve my problems." **8. Dispose/Upgrade** "It's easy for my business to grow with HP."
The associated experience circle comprises **Demonstration, Buying experience, Fulfillment, Web site, Billing, Promotions, Deployment, Usage, Upgrade, Service** — all around "Quality of Customer Experience", with the rule **"Consistency across buying stages."** [Source: KOB, ch. 11]

**"How do you maintain relationships systematically without treating everyone the same?"** The four-step sub-segmentation process: **(1) Develop and profile sub-segments. (2) Set MEASURABLE goals per sub-segment. (3) Design and execute contacts. (4) Measure results** — "one of the benefits of a CRM-based contact is **its ability to measure results.**"
Bases for segmentation: **frequency** (number of previous purchases), **socio- and firmographic** characteristics ("easy to implement"), **cluster analysis.**
Two kinds of contact: **quid-pro-quo** versus **discretionary rewards** — "**they are CARROTS: if the customer does something that has been agreed on.**" Evidence: **Tesco Clubcard** with quid-pro-quo rewards and the **Tesco Baby&Toddler Club** ("Help and offers every little step of the way").
And the link to experience: readers of a magazine agree that "**Reading this magazine is a quiet time**", "**It's an escape**" — "to engage in relationship branding we therefore need **contacts** [that support this experience]." [Source: KOB, ch. 8]

## Concrete examples
- **Starbucks:** virtually no advertising in the first 30 years; the brand arose through outstanding experiences at store level and **dozens of positive interactions with employees.** [Source: KOB, introduction]
- **Lands' End** as the counter-case: after the purchase by Sears in 2002 for **1.9 billion USD** the products were sold in Sears stores, **but Sears's customer service was poor** — which damaged the brand. [Source: KOB, introduction]
- **Nordstrom:** sales staff were allowed to approve exchanges **without manager approval.** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **Ritz-Carlton:** The Gold Standard plus **120 training hours per employee per year.** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **Continental Airlines:** **65 USD per employee per month** when the airline was among the five most punctual. [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **Pret A Manger:** employees decide on hiring; **an acceptance rate of only 20 percent.** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **Southwest:** the brand promise translated internally into employee freedoms, the intranet renamed **Freedom Net.** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **Sheetz:** the seven most senior executives as Matrix characters on a **26-region roadshow in front of 3,000 employees.** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **Cisco CEO John Chambers**, asked who leads the brand there: **I do!** And the group finding about the brand CEOs named: **none of them came into the job with the aim of building a brand, and none came from a classical marketing position.** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **UBS, 9 June 2003:** one month per advisor for personal contact with every client, **500 USD per broker** at 100 percent reach, **over 4 million USD donated**, a wave of fresh funds, a top-50 brand for the first time in 2004. [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **Infineon, 26 March 1999:** celebrations at **every site worldwide** with 25,000 employees, followed up via the internal report **Cultural Mirror of the Company** and an external **Brand Tracking Survey.** [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **Northwestern Memorial:** Patients First lapel pin, **600 million USD construction**, admissions **up 34 percent**, emergency department **up 27 percent**, births **up 47 percent.** [Source: KOB, ch. 18]
- **James Brown**, who fell to his knees exhausted every night: brilliant **stagecraft, not an authentic performance. It happened every night.** [Source: TIM, ch. 6]

## Open points (contradiction register)
The following points are **documented, not resolved.** Full evidence in the register in `../research/system-overview.md`. For requests that touch them, **both positions** are to be presented.

- **B4 Internal work versus advertising effect:** the proportionality statement on internal and external brand success plus the warning against new campaigns, in a book with a complete chapter on advertising effect. **Addition: the statement comes from a consultant who sells such programmes, and is a claim, not a measured result.**
- **Weakness of evidence as an open point:** This area lies mostly in KOB's practitioner part. **All seven authors report on their own successes without a control group and without a dissenting voice.** The only self-qualification in the entire part is Bernick on Static Guard. All success figures are to be read under this caveat.
