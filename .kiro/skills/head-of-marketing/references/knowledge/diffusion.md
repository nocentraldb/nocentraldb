# Diffusion, Competition and Market Development

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Tybout/Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018.

## Character of the sources for this topic area
**Both books share the same diffusion theory** (Rogers, Moore) and complement each other cleanly: **KOB supplies the market-share economics** (order of entry, consideration set, three late-entry strategies, price sensitivity) with peer-reviewed evidence; **TIM supplies the transmission mechanics** (network effects, local chasm crossing, long tail) and the realistic damping of expectations. **Important reservation: KOB ch. 4 contains an internal contradiction** — it documents the pioneer advantage at length and then cites a study whose title questions it as a "marketing legend", without naming the conflict. See contradiction register.

## Core concepts and definitions

### The adoption curve
On almost any measure, of 100 people **about 68 are close to the average, 27 are clearly further away, 4 are extreme outliers.** The **fifteen or sixteen on the left** are the neophiliacs; an equally large group on the right defends the status quo. [Source: TIM, ch. 14; basis Everett Rogers]
**Rogers' finding:** "when it comes to style, technology, or innovations, **most people like what they have.** They want to do what others are doing, and they aren't actively seeking novelty." [Source: TIM, ch. 14]

### The chasm (Geoffrey Moore)
"We have no idea who discovered the pothole, or who named the Grand Canyon, but **Geoff Moore discovered the chasm.** It's the overlooked but often fatal gap in the Rogers curve."
The problem: **"There aren't enough of these neophiliacs to go around."** Large organisations, mass movements and substantial profits depend on the mass market — that is where Heinz, Starbucks, JetBlue, the American Heart Association and Amazon live.
**The intuitive and wrong expectation** is that the early adopters carry the idea to the masses. Why not: "**the mass market wants something DIFFERENT from what the early adopters want. The mass market wants something that WORKS. Something SAFE. A pattern match, not a pattern interrupt.** They take 'people like us do things like this' very seriously."
**Moore's point in Godin's wording: "few innovations glide from one part of the market to the other. That's because IN ORDER TO SATISFY THE EARLY ADOPTERS, YOU MAY JUST NEED TO ANNOY THE MASSES. The very thing your innovation did (break things) is the one thing that the mass market doesn't want to happen."** Evidence of refusal: they do not want to swap their DVDs, learn a new software platform, or read their news online.
**The empirical advice: "go spend an hour or two at the help desk at the Apple store. Check out who's there and why. Listen to their questions, and pay attention to their facial expressions."** Conclusion: "**The middle of the curve isn't eagerly adopting. They're barely ADAPTING. That's why they've chosen to be in the middle of the curve.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

### The Gartner Hype Cycle as a second view of the same chasm
Stations: **Technology Trigger -> Peak of Inflated Expectations -> Trough of Disillusionment -> Slope of Enlightenment -> Plateau of Productivity.**
The trigger opens the door for one's own work and interrupts a pattern. At that moment marketing reaches the neophiliacs, **and inevitably they hype the work — "Of course they will. That's one of the best reasons to be an early adopter."** When the idea is presented to the rest of the market, it cannot withstand the hype. **Hence the trough.** "**It's in this moment, when the neophiliacs are bored with you and the mass market disdains you, that you will most likely lose momentum.** This is the moment when you need a bridge, a new way to step through the culture with stories that match the worldviews of this new, more conservative market." [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### Network effects as a bridge across the chasm
"**The bridge across the chasm lies in network effects.** Most of the fast-growing marketing successes of our lifetime have spread because of ideas that **work better when everyone knows them.** The early adopters have a huge incentive to bring your idea across the chasm; **it will make their lives better if everyone in their network also uses this idea.**"
**The contrast evidence that sharpens the rule: "There's no reason to talk about a new kind of chocolate you really like. It doesn't make your life better if others eat it. On the other hand, you spend a lot of time telling people about Snapchat or Instagram or Twitter, because if your friends followed you, YOUR life would improve."**
Non-technical cases of the same mechanism: organising a bus to Washington against gun violence (more people = more impact **and** a nicer day); winning friends for the local CSA farm share (the farmers cannot come for a few, but with many there is better variety for everyone).
**The two bridge questions: "(1) What will I tell my friends? (2) Why will I tell them?"** And the rule that goes with it: "**It is never the case that people will tell their friends because you want them to, or because you ask them to, or because you worked hard.** Give them a why. And that usually involves **changing what you offer. Make things better by making better things** — things that have a network effect, a ratchet, a reason for sharing." [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

### Metcalfe's Law and remarkability from design
"**The most effective remarkability comes from design.**" The fax machine did not spread because of a cleverly made ad, but because users **wanted to talk** about it — "because the fax machine works better if your colleagues have one too."
**Bob Metcalfe experienced this with Ethernet:** 3Com's original offer allowed three users to connect PCs and share a printer — "a marginal benefit, not much to talk about". When users shared data, everything changed: You were **in the network or outside it**, and outside was painful. "The more people joined the network, the more people talked about the network. **That made isolation even more painful.**"
**Metcalfe's Law**, original slide with two lines: the straight one shows that the cost per additional person rises **slowly**; the curved one that the value per additional person grows **exponentially**.
"**This simple network effect is at the heart of every mass movement and every successful culture change.** It happens when remarkable is designed right into the story of your change, and more important, **when the product or service works better when I use it with others.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 8]

### Pioneer advantage — quantification and six sources
**The core figure** (Urban/Carter/Gaskin/Mucha 1986, *Management Science*): market shares by order of entry, **adjusted for differences in strategy.** The pioneer = 100 percent relative to itself; **the second entrant receives on average only 75 percent, the third under 60 percent, the sixth under half** — despite equal attractiveness.
**The logic of the proof:** If rational consumers look at two equally attractive, equally priced, equally available, equally advertised brands, both should receive an equal share. "**But such logic is flawed** ... consumers do not judge early and late entrants alike. **Instead, consumers PREFER the pioneer.**"
**The economic consequence:** A sixth entrant would have to offer **more** — advertise more aggressively, be in more outlets or be cheaper. "**These additional costs impose a burden on later entrants that pioneers do not bear.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 4]

**The six sources:**
1. **Preference formation.** The pioneer influences which attributes matter and how much. **Levi Strauss** taught America and much of the world **one** concept of blue jeans: "a baggy cut was preferable to a slim cut, heavy denim was superior to light, **and low price was less important than owning the genuine article.**" When Wrangler and others entered, "these makers were hard pressed to redefine how Americans thought about their blue jeans."
   **The Vaseline evidence (1880, advertised as a "remedy of unsurpassed purity"):** By trying the translucent, highly pure gel, buyers learned that these attributes are effective. "They **generalized** from this observation and concluded that the effectiveness lies in its translucence and purity (in contrast to the competing black coal tar derivatives of the time). ... Thus, **translucence came to be favored over opacity** and gained more importance in brand evaluation. Moreover, because Vaseline pioneered the product, all later brands were compared to it, **and they were found wanting EVEN IF THEY WERE IDENTICAL, simply because they were not Vaseline.**"
2. **Category association.** Two standard types. **Technological standards** (QWERTY, Microsoft Windows, Lego) create architecture standards: "Brands that control the architecture can exert enormous control over competitors, as Microsoft demonstrates vividly and others such as Lego do more quietly." **Psychological standards:** Levi Strauss, Coca-Cola, Jell-O; historically Cadillac as the epitome of the luxury car, Porsche as the essence of the sports car, Volkswagen as the pure concept of the economy car. Fusions: "**For many years, Xerox has been both a brand name and a verb** ... The confection known as a Tootsie Roll is difficult to describe without using the brand name, **and in China a Motorola is a pager.**"
   **The consideration-set mechanics, the operationally most important part:** "In most product categories, buyers consider only a subset of all brands, often called a **consideration set**. This typically consists of **around three to five** brands." Evidence beer: hundreds of brands on the market, a typical consumer knows **ten** but seriously considers only **three to five**. "**a brand that is the psychological standard will, for most buyers, be included in their consideration sets. THE OTHER BRANDS OFTEN VARY.** ... it will be chosen more often **simply because it is CONSIDERED more often.**"
3. **Awareness and recall.** In the supermarket aisle only a moment remains, and the consideration set consists of the **most vivid** brands. Evidence (Kardes/Kalyanaram 1992, *JMR*): "subjects recalled the pioneer brand most often, and they also recalled the pioneer brand **MORE POSITIVELY** than others." The novelty mechanism: "A pioneer brand is novel. We devote cognitive effort to understanding it. ... **By the time we observe the sixth or tenth brand, redundancy increases, novelty decreases, and we devote much less effort.**"
4. **Preemptive positioning — the most counterintuitive result.** A me-too (identical, but cheaper) suffers **despite** its similarity: "**the more similar the me-too and pioneer, the greater the relative prominence of the pioneer.** This happens because the me-too brand **derives its identity from the pioneer**, and greater similarity reduces the distinctiveness of the me-too brand. ... it remains inferior because it has **no distinctive competence**."
   **The price experiment:** Consumers observed a pioneer that was followed by either no entry, a differentiated entry (same price) or a me-too (cheaper), and then rated the same brands at the same prices. **Result: "the pioneer followed by the me-too brand is LEAST price sensitive; the pioneer followed by NO entrant is the MOST price sensitive; and the pioneer followed by the DIFFERENTIATED entrant is midway."**
   **The explanation: "By adopting a me-too position, the later entrant SIGNALS TO BUYERS THAT THE PIONEER IS INDEED IN THE BEST POSITION. The me-too brand implicitly ADMITS this position by adopting it and then advertising it as such."**
   **The historical evidence: "One example of this occurred in the cola market, when Pepsi attacked the pioneer, Coca-Cola, in the famous Pepsi challenge. AS A RESULT OF THAT ATTACK COCA-COLA'S MARKET SHARE ROSE, despite Pepsi's claim of superior taste."**
5. **Risk.** "Inherently, a buyer has more information about the pioneer. **It works.** The other brands remain untested. That doubt makes the untried alternative risky, and thus buyers will favor the known alternatives, **even paying a premium for them.**" Particularly important for **experience goods** — products one has to experience in order to evaluate them: tyres, aspirin substitutes, barbecue sauce. With cars, performance, styling and price can be judged beforehand, "**but reliability is more difficult to determine unless the model has a long track record.**"
   **And the often overlooked retailer level: "Risk has even more dramatic consequences at the retailer level."** The retailer has to decide whether to reduce shelf space of a well-selling brand. "**The retailer faces reducing a known income stream and replacing it with an uncertain one.**"
6. **Buyer entry — how the advantage survives generations.** "**Lipton traditionally has dominated the tea business since 1893, and Gold Medal has dominated the flour market since 1890**" — although hardly any of the originally influenced consumers still buy. Two mechanisms: **distribution** ("if Coca-Cola can dominate the restaurant channel, then a greater number of new consumers will experience Coca-Cola in restaurants relative to Pepsi") and **loyal customers as an inheritance path** ("more young consumers will grow up in households where Coca-Cola is consumed regularly. Their first experience with a cola may very well be Coca-Cola, **and through that initial experience, Coca-Cola can begin to define the market for these new buyers as well**"). [Source: KOB, ch. 4]

### The three late-entry strategies
Successful overtakings as starting evidence: "**Karl Benz invented the automobile, but Ford Motor Company and later General Motors took the lead. Amana created the microwave oven, but in 2005 Samsung led the market. Star was the pioneer in safety razors, but Gillette took over. Diet Coke has dominated the diet cola market pioneered by that well-known brand Lirsch.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 4]

**(1) FAST FOLLOWING** — "playing the game the pioneer has created, but doing so better or with more resources. Fast followers do not challenge the logic of the pioneer's efforts; in fact, they **embrace** that logic and seek to capture the profits."
Point of attack: "**Many pioneers start a game without the resources to win it.**" Without means the pioneer cannot influence preference formation and does not become the standard; sometimes it establishes the position only with a small part of the market ("**e.g., Apple Computer**"). Then "a quick, well-funded later entrant ... pick up where the pioneer falters" and **"gains all of the advantages associated with pioneering without actually being the pioneer."**
**THE CENTRAL COUNTER-FIGURE** (Golder/Tellis 1993, *JMR*, title verbatim: "Pioneering Advantage: Marketing Logic or Marketing **Legend**?"), historical analysis of **50 product categories**: "**the typical pioneer retained its leadership position for just FIVE YEARS. Early followers failed only 8 PERCENT of the time. Moreover, fast followers led in 53 PERCENT of the markets examined.**"
**The three documented fast-follower advantages** (Shankar/Carpenter/Krishnamurthi 1999, *JMR*, 29 brands in six pharmaceutical markets):
 (a) **Growth rates:** Fast followers grow faster than pioneers and late entrants over the whole lifetime. "**Pioneers, in fact, grow most slowly. Quite possibly they bear the burden of building the category.**" Late entrants are "**doubly disadvantaged**" — lower level **and** slower.
 (b) **Buyer response:** Buyers respond most strongly to **quality changes** by fast followers, because their buyers are more knowledgeable than the pioneer's ("a skeptical market of relatively uninformed buyers") and less bound to routine than those of late entrants. **Important restriction: "with respect to marketing EXPENDITURES, growth-stage entrants have NO advantage relative to pioneers.**"
 (c) **Competitive impact:** Fast followers enjoy "**a unique level of insularity from competitors**". In the long run pioneers are **harmed** by the sales growth of competitors; late entrants are not helped by it.

**(2) DIFFERENTIATION** — the dilemma: "greater differentiation increases market share. But a product can also run the risk of being **so different as to be irrelevant.** Being too close to the pioneer runs the risk of being overshadowed — **or worse yet, COPIED — by the pioneer.**"
**The resolution** (Carpenter/Lehmann/Nakamoto/Walchli 1993; tested in two categories, cars with Jeep, Porsche, Mitsubishi and soft drinks with Coca-Cola, 7-Up, Hanssens, **including brand extensions of the pioneers to test their ability to imitate, e.g. a Jeep sports car**): "**the key to differentiation is to position a new entrant JUST FAR ENOUGH TO BE OUT OF THE REACH OF THE PIONEER'S IMITATION.**"
The logic: More similar extensions are rated higher for both — **but the effect is much more dramatic for the pioneer.** "For a pioneer's brand extension, being strongly associated with its original position makes it more unassailable. **But there are also drawbacks: being strongly associated with the original position also means that when the pioneer strays too far, buyers see those new products as inconsistent.**" Hence there is a range of "late entrant advantage".
**Dependence on the pioneer's strength: "If the pioneer is WEAKER, it can reach further afield, so a late entrant needs to position FURTHER from the pioneer to be safe from competitive imitation. A weaker pioneer, however, is more vulnerable to DIRECT ATTACK ... At some point, when the pioneer becomes sufficiently weak, a direct attack can be the best alternative.**"
**The price and advertising rule: "high-profit differentiation strategies are supported by HIGH PRICES (as opposed to pricing to be a me-too) and HIGH ADVERTISING SUPPORT (as opposed to being a no-name rival)."**
**The theoretical condensation: "Every competitive advantage has a corresponding competitive weakness. The pioneer's great strength is its unique, powerful association with one valuable position. ITS CORRESPONDING WEAKNESS IS STRATEGIC INFLEXIBILITY.**"

**(3) INNOVATION** — "seeks to redefine what buyers know or perceive about the established brand and the market. This strategy **renders obsolete** the knowledge created through the interaction between buyers and the pioneer."
Evidence: **Gillette overtook the pioneer Star through product innovation; the US detergent market, opened by Dreft, is dominated by Tide.** "In all these cases, these late entrants **displaced the pioneer as the standard of comparison** and created a new standard."
**The findings** (Shankar/Carpenter/Krishnamurthi 1998, *JMR*, "Late Mover Advantage: How Innovative Late Entrants Outsell Pioneers", 13 pharmaceutical brands in two markets):
 **Non-innovative late entrants:** Buyers respond more weakly to their marketing expenditures and are less loyal. "**non-innovative late movers must play by the rules created by the pioneer. By doing so, they suffer while the pioneer prospers.**"
 **Innovative late entrants: "grow faster, have larger potential markets, and have more loyal buyers THAN OTHER ENTRANTS — PIONEERS INCLUDED."** Explanation: "the innovative later entrant can take advantage of the investment others have made to establish the category and, by virtue of its innovation, **expand that market beyond what others have been able to achieve.**"
 **And the active damage mechanism: "growth of the innovative late entrant actually SLOWED THE GROWTH OF THE PIONEER AND REDUCED THE EFFECTIVENESS OF ITS MARKETING SPENDING. ... Innovation coupled with late entry enables a brand to IMPOSE COSTS ON ITS RIVALS."** With non-innovative late entrants this effect did **not** occur.
 **The risk balance: "Taking the low-risk path appears to produce relatively poor results. ... By contrast, the greater risk associated with innovation appears well rewarded."** [Source: KOB, ch. 4]

### The long tail — and Godin's correction of Chris Anderson
Anderson's structure (Rhapsody data): On the left the hits, few, but each sells a lot — **"number one sells ten times as many copies as number ten, and a hundred times as many as number one hundred. A hit is magical."** On the right the long tail: good products of specialised interest; each sells little individually, **together as much as the short head.**
Hard figures: **"Half of Amazon's sales are books that are not in the top five thousand. Half! Half of the music consumed on streaming sites isn't available in stores. Not half the titles, half the volume."**
**The decisive asymmetry: "Amazon can do great with this strategy since they sell ALL the available books. Each AUTHOR, though, is in pain: selling one or two books a day is no way to make a living."** And: "**These huge marketplaces (Amazon, Netflix, iTunes) depend on the misguided hopes and dreams of individuals way out on the long tail. Separately, each one struggles. Taken together, it's a good business.**"
**The T-shirt example as illustration:** A shirt "It's Easter, It's Passover, It's April Fools', and It's My Birthday". The calculation: only **one in 365** qualifies, perhaps **one in a thousand** would wear it, and perhaps **one in a hundred** of their friends would go looking — "so the vendor probably sold **four**." Explanation: "a long-tail business. A few companies are making **tens of thousands of different shirts**, made to order. ... **If you can aggregate a chunk of the long tail, you can make a go of it. But you can't possibly sell just one obscure shirt and have a shot.**"
**The false promise, named: "That you can be happy with a tiny slice of the long tail. That anyone can sing or write or dance or do comedy or coach or freelance ... EXCEPT YOU WON'T BE FINE, BECAUSE YOU CAN'T LIVE ON THAT. The internet can live on that, Upwork and Fiverr and Netflix and Amazon can live on that, but you can't."** And on the exceptions: "We hear about the outliers ... **But becoming an outlier isn't a strategy. It's a wish.**"
**The way out — splitting the curve: "by dividing the market into MANY CURVES, not just one, we end up with many short heads and many long tails."** Examples: literary fiction for teenagers, books about chip carving, video courses on the GH5 camera, improv theatre, "**and even the market for drone music played so loudly that the audience needs to wear ear protection.**"
**"In each of these markets, there's a need for a short head — at least once someone has CONNECTED the people in that market to one another, so they realize that they exist, so they see each other, and so they understand what the hit is. BECAUSE IT'S THE HIT THAT CONNECTS THEM."**
**The two elements of living on the long tail: (1) create the definitive, most essential, extraordinary contribution to the field. (2) CONNECT the market you designed it for and help it see that you belong in the short head — that this hit is the glue that holds them together.** Examples of short heads: **Rocky Horror. And the DeWalt 20V Max XR lithium-ion brushless rotary hammer.**
**The closing formula: "Yes, the internet is a discovery tool. But no, you're not going to get discovered that way. Instead, you will make your impact by UNITING those you seek to serve."** [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **The local chasm is the realistic target size.** "**The good news is that you don't need to set out to cross a GLOBAL chasm. A LOCAL one can change everything.**"
  **The yo-yo example:** A child brings a yo-yo on Monday — "**but he's the wrong kid on the wrong day.**" Weeks later a charismatic fifth-grader brings her yo-yo and founds the **Yo-yo Union, "an exclusive club that's open to all"**. She is good at tricks, **"but not so good that she's intimidating"** — and she brings **three more yo-yos for her friends.** "**She's chosen wisely — each of these early adopters is a leader in her own right.**" A week later thirty children are in: "the cost of entry is low, the payoff is quick, and the connection feels real." Then the whole school.
  **The restriction: "Because yo-yos are a fad, without stickiness, they cool off as fast as they spread. Of course, IT DOESN'T HAVE TO BE THAT WAY IF YOU BUILD IN IDENTITY AND PERSISTENCE."** The same crossing with Uggs, black backpacks, Penny skateboards. **And the observation error: "We only NOTICE the ones that cross a local chasm, but the early adopters are always experimenting around the edges."** [Source: TIM, ch. 19]
- **Damping expectations on the global leap:** "**In our lifetime, very few brands have crossed over fully to the mass market. Starbucks hasn't made it all the way, and neither has Heineken or even the bagel. But Facebook has.**" And the conclusion: "**As much as most marketers would like to make this last huge leap, it's unlikely. The market is just too big, and the typical network effect isn't that strong.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 19]
- **"And then a miracle happens" — the rejected alternative.** The old-fashioned dream is to turn an average "it's okay" product into a hit with PR, hype, promotion, distribution, ad buys, influencer and content marketing and a dash of spam, "popular precisely because it's popular. **Sure, every so often a superstar is born, but most of the time, this approach merely leads to failure. Expensive failure.**" Alternative: **"seek a path, not a miracle. And that path begins with customer traction."**
  The test questions: How many people outside headquarters use it daily? How often do they send improvement suggestions? How many insist that friends and colleagues use it — **now**? "Do they love it? **Do they love themselves more because they love you?**" And for a restaurant, market stall, non-profit, babysitting service: "**Who would miss it if it were gone?**"
  **The core heuristic: "If you can't succeed in the small, why do you believe you will succeed in the large?"** [Source: TIM, ch. 8]
- **Hamilton as an apparent counter-proof, defused:** For over a year only a few hundred a night saw it. Even at full capacity and Broadway records it is only a few thousand. Even while it changes culture in cities like Chicago, **fewer than 1 percent of the US population have seen it**; the bestselling soundtrack album sold only a few hundred thousand. **"Our hits aren't hits anymore, not like they used to be. Instead, they are meaningful for a few and invisible to the rest."** [Source: TIM, ch. 8]
- **"Taylor Swift is not your role model."** Scott Borchetta (Big Machine Records) has over 200 number-one singles and sold over 30 million records for Taylor Swift, whose tour revenue roughly equals that of the Grateful Dead. Godin's point: Every market needs a hit machine — **"every long tail has a short head, a place where the hits live. Hits serve a useful purpose to our culture, but the essential lesson is this: SOMEONE IS GOING TO MAKE HITS, AND IT'S PROBABLY NOT GOING TO BE YOU."** Whoever finds a playbook to the hit machine: go ahead. For everyone else: "the path of connection, empathy, and change." [Source: TIM, ch. 8]
- **Dynamics instead of statics — the methodological critique of the discipline:** "**Building a strong brand is not enough. Innovative late entrants can overtake even the most successful brands — pioneers and followers alike.** Mastering the dynamics of competition means that brands must **constantly evolve** to preempt competitors and reinforce the uniqueness of the brand." And: "these findings suggest significant limitations of traditional analyses of marketing strategy, **which are largely static.** The fact that firms' activities change consumer perceptions not only of brands but also of **categories** means that industries evolve." Christensen is cited: "**no firm that dominated the computer market during one era has successfully maintained its dominance into the next**" — with the counter-example Harley-Davidson, which "owe[s] its continued survival, in significant measure, to the strength of [its] brand." [Source: KOB, ch. 4]
- **Positioning dynamics across product generations** (not only across the adoption cycle): Technology markets require managing positioning both across the adoption lifecycle **and across successive product generations.** Evidence: SAP with "Vectors of Differentiation" and a "differentiation gap" against competitors that opens and closes over time (1972/1979/1992). [Source: KOB, ch. 11]

## Typical problems and approaches

**"We have trial, but no growth."** The documented case — **Godin's own failure with hugdug.com (Squidoo)**, the most honest section of both books, because an author reports on his own failure with a root-cause analysis.
Idea: In about four minutes build a page presenting a beloved Amazon product, plus your own review; if someone bought through it, **half of the commission went to the creator's favourite charity** — years before smile.amazon.com and **with twenty times the donation.**
The bets: Authors would promote their books this way; Pinterest fans would find it fun and satisfying; one would find the neophiliacs; **authors would be the whales.**
**"We kept at it for months, but we failed."** The causes: They got **trial** (thousands of pages built), **but did not find whales — fewer than six people built more than a dozen pages.** "**The tension dissipated too quickly. People felt like they had no good reason to return after a single visit.**" The long tail was so long that a page often sold **zero books a month.** And: "most people were hesitant to promote a page, because **it's still emotionally difficult to push your friends to visit a shopping site.**"
**The four named lessons: (1) "a Kickstarter-like success is always more difficult than it looks." (2) "We were naive in believing that FOUR MONTHS was enough time to create an overnight success." (3) "We underestimated how difficult it might be to create sufficient incentives." (4) "Most of all, we failed to create a TENSION DYNAMIC that would have turned our early users into CONNECTED AMBASSADORS who would have turned the ratchet as we crossed the chasm."** Plus: "We didn't do enough to tell a story about **status**, and we weren't nearly specific enough about **who our first customers would be**, what they might have wanted, believed, and said." [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

**"What does a successful mass leap look like from the inside?"** **Facebook.** The user graph shows 2005 to 2011 (scale up to 800 million); **around 2008 "a whole new bunch of everyone" began to use it.**
**The chain of reasoning:** The reason for signing up changed from "this is somehow interesting" to "this helps me immensely" to **"I'm the last person on earth who isn't using it".**
**The Harvard launch was part of the secret: "The insecure Harvard student had an urgent status need: to find out where he or she stood in the pecking order."** As it spread through the Ivy League, **Facebook crossed one local chasm after another.** At every university a neophiliac went first, "**but the relentless ratchet of STATUS kept it spreading. The more friends you had on Facebook, the higher your status. The other people already on Facebook had a status you admired.**"
**"Facebook was planted in the best possible spot, surrounded by insecure, high-status young people, with fast internet connections, plenty of spare time, and an insatiable desire to be seen, to connect, and to move up in some invisible hierarchy."** And the last leap: "Because Facebook was able to combine nerdiness with status, they were able to surround the middle of the market and create an irresistible ratchet. **Join in, or face your worst fear — be socially isolated.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

**"Network effects exist only in software" — wrong.** The model case without technology: **Water Health International.** For **a billion people** the norm is dirty, parasite-ridden water, often several hours' walk away.
When WHI comes to a village with a treatment kiosk, **some** inhabitants understand the effect at once, buy a special jerrycan and pay for the daily refill. "**The few pennies spent on clean water are quickly earned back in time saved, increased productivity, and reduced medical expense.**"
**"And yet, not everyone buys the water right away. MOST PEOPLE DON'T. In fact, it follows PRECISELY THE SAME ADOPTION CURVE as just about everything else, from toys to computers."** The early adopters buy first — "they may be educated enough to realize how powerful an input clean water is, **but it's more likely that they simply like buying things that are new.**"
**The semiotic observation: The garishly coloured canisters that WHI requires (so that no infected vessel is filled) are "a badge of honor and an invitation to converse."**
**The microscope lever:** WHI sends representatives with a microscope projector to the village school; the pupils bring water samples from home. **"Projecting the samples on the wall, the microscope tells a vivid story that resonates with the eight-year-olds. This is what germs look like. This is what parasites look like. Inevitably, the students go home and tell their parents."**
**And then status takes hold: "When your young child talks about his neighbors having clean water ... and you don't. When you see the respected members of the village hierarchy carrying the distinctive Jerry cans. WHEN YOU HESITATE TO HOST SOMEONE IN YOUR HOME BECAUSE YOU DON'T HAVE CLEAN WATER TO OFFER THEM."**
**"This is a ratchet, but not one based on obvious software network effects. It's based on THE ORIGINAL NETWORK EFFECT, the one built around people in proximity."** And the hard core: "**Most can afford the water (because of the time and impact savings), but the EMOTIONAL SHIFT is the difficult part.**" Result: **within months the water crossed the local chasm.** [Source: TIM, ch. 19]

**"Success needs patience that we do not have."** The model case: **Grateful Dead.** Starting paradox: **exactly ONE Top-40 Billboard hit.** "Easily dismissed as some sort of quirky hippie band."
**And yet: over USD 350 million in revenue during Jerry Garcia's lifetime and a further USD 100 million since his death — only concert tickets, without record sales, and most of it at average ticket prices of USD 23.**
The five key elements: (1) Address a relatively tiny audience and put all energy into it. (2) **No radio**, but fans passing it from hand to hand — **by encouraging them to tape the shows.** (3) Instead of moving many people too little, move a few real fans a lot. (4) Chose extremes on the XY axes and occupied **both** (live concerts versus polished records, long jams for the fan family versus short radio hits). (5) Gave the fans plenty to talk about and stand for — insiders and outsiders.
The three preconditions: **extraordinary talent** ("you can't fake your way through 146 concerts in a year"), **considerable patience** ("in 1972, considered by some to be a peak year, **only five thousand people came to a typical show. It took more than a decade before the Dead became an 'overnight' success**"), **and the courage to be quirky** ("it couldn't have been easy to watch the Zombies, the Doors, and even the Turtles sell far more records").
**The historical punchline: "In 1972, being obstinate, generous, and lucky was an ACCIDENT that led to their surprising success. Today, though, in most industries (including the music business) this sort of success is NOT an accident. IT'S THE BEST PATH TO SUCCESS, and in many ways, the most rewarding as well."** [Source: TIM, ch. 8]

**"Pioneer or fast second?" — OPEN POINT.** In its first half, KOB ch. 4 documents the pioneer advantage in detail (75/60/50 percent share ladder, six mechanisms) and in the second half cites Golder/Tellis with the counter-finding (pioneer leadership only five years, fast followers lead 53 percent of markets). **The authors set both side by side without naming the conflict** — particularly remarkable because Carpenter and Nakamoto are themselves authors of the pioneer-advantage studies and still include the counter-finding. **The chapter closes on the second position ("innovative late entrants can overtake even the most successful brands"), although its longest part documents the first.** See contradiction register.

## Concrete examples
- **The market-share ladder by order of entry:** 100 / 75 / under 60 / under 50 percent — the central pioneer finding. [Source: KOB, ch. 4]
- **The counter-finding in the same chapter:** Golder/Tellis 1993, Marketing Logic or Marketing **Legend** — pioneer leadership lasts only about **five years**, and **fast followers lead in 53 percent of 50 examined markets. See Open points.** [Source: KOB, ch. 4]
- **TiVo:** 2 percent market penetration instead of the predicted 50 percent — pioneer position without an understandable frame of reference. [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
- **Palm Pilot:** one million units in 18 months, with a frame buyers understood. [Source: KOB, ch. 1]
- **The me-too price paradox and the Pepsi Challenge:** The price attack on the pioneer **raised Coca-Cola's market share**, despite Pepsi's taste claim. By imitating, the follower itself signals to buyers that the pioneer occupies the best position. [Source: KOB, ch. 4]
- **Black & Decker/DeWalt:** Tradesmen left the brand when consumers adopted it — a documented adoption break between two groups. [Source: TIM, ch. 14]
- **LEED certification as a diffusion course:** at the start **two buildings a day**; at that rate it would have taken **a hundred years** to reach the numbers reached after **twelve**. The accelerator was a status dynamic, a race to the top. [Source: TIM, ch. 19]
- **The Ziglar case as bridging the chasm:** Move into a town and stay, instead of taking the easy sales and driving on. **The easy sales are not always the important ones.** [Source: TIM, ch. 20]
