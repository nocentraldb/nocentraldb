# Brand Architecture, Extension and Portfolio

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Tybout/Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018.

## Character of the sources for this topic area
**KOB is almost the sole source here and delivers four connected tools**: the architecture language (Primary / Sub / Endorser / Ingredient), the fit concept with seven test questions, the two portfolio models with advantages and disadvantages, and five keys to success including pruning. **TIM has no concept of architecture at all** — it always treats exactly one brand facing exactly one tribe. **The one-sidedness is justified by the content, but TIM supplies the counter-position to the basic stance** (smallest viable market against brand breadth), and that is substantial.
**Note, two internal KOB contradictions in exactly this area:** Ch. 5 closes with "a broad brand is stronger than a narrower brand", ch. 6 supplies the list of failed stretches and cites Trout against breadth. **And ch. 2 diagnoses the Porsche Cayenne case through the name, ch. 5 through the fit communication, with no cross-reference.** See the contradiction register.

## Core concepts and definitions

### Terms of extension
- **Line extension** = new product **in the same** product category as the parent or flagship brand.
- **Category extension** = new product **in a different** category. [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

### The four building blocks of brand architecture
- **Primary Brand** — the main name, the largest branding element. "This is what people refer to when they talk about the brand. **Every brand has a primary brand.**" The simplest structure is primary brand plus product descriptor: McDonald's restaurants, Starbucks coffee, Marlboro cigarettes, Prozac antidepressant.
- **Sub-Brand** — below the primary brand, above the product descriptor, "usually employed to set apart a group of products or service offerings that are different in some meaningful way". Examples: **GE Monogram** (designer appliances), **GE Profile** (high-end appliances). **The demarcation rule: "if the sub-brand is more prominent than the primary brand, then the sub-brand IS actually the primary brand."** And the cost warning: "**there must be a compelling reason to have a sub-brand; each sub-brand creates complexity and requires both attention and marketing support.**"
- **Endorser Brand** — connects the primary brand with another one, usually the parent company. "**Generally, endorsers are used to bring credibility to the primary brand while LETTING THE PRIMARY BRAND ESTABLISH ITS OWN IDENTITY.** Apple, for example, is an endorser brand on iPod." The same upper limit applies as for the sub-brand.
- **Ingredient or Service Brand** — brands a component or service and thereby lays claim to it. **The core advantage: "A vague phrase, such as 'heavy duty' or 'high quality,' is easy for competitors to copy. A branded ingredient or service is NOT, BECAUSE IT IS LEGALLY PROTECTED."** Examples: **Glad FreshScent** (scent in garbage bags), **Chrysler's Hemi** (engine), **Westin's Heavenly Bed** (service). They are not part of the official product name. [Source: KOB, ch. 6]

**The four-level hierarchy in its B2B version:** corporate brand -> family brand -> individual brand -> modifier. Example **IBM ThinkPad X30**: IBM = corporate, ThinkPad = family (all notebooks as distinct from desktops), X Series = individual (extra light, extra small, ultraportable), 30 = modifier (Ethernet models as distinct from 31 for WLAN). "**The brand hierarchy enables a firm to leverage previous investments in brand building.**" Example: **3M used the Post-it Notes equity to enter flip-chart pads with Post-it Self-Stick Easel Pads.** [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

### Fit — the decisive evaluation concept
"**Consumers' response to a brand extension depends on the level of FIT that they perceive between the parent brand and the extension.**" Fit arises from the associations to the brand, "many of which emanate from the brand's core point of differentiation. **Consumers gauge fit by determining whether these associations MAKE SENSE IN THE CONTEXT of the extension.**"
**The pairs of examples:** **Diet Coke** fits because the products differ primarily in the sweetener and not in attributes such as taste, which are central to the benefit. **Crystal Pepsi flopped** partly because of poor fit: as a clear cola, its point of differentiation contradicted **a core association — the traditional cola colour.** **BIC** was able to extend from pens into lighters and razors through "cheap", "plastic", "disposability" — "**but these same associations UNDERMINED consumer acceptance when the brand ventured into PERFUME**, where these attributes have negative connotations." [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### Fit along the four positioning components
**TARGET.** The image of the Harley-Davidson biker is an integral part of the brand; Harley can launch line extensions for this base (clothing, motor homes) and new categories such as **temporary tattoos**, "where the imagery of the tattooed biker lends **an aura of authenticity** to the product for consumers unwilling to commit to the real thing."
**But user associations also limit:** Kimberly-Clark successfully extended **Huggies** from babies to toddlers (**Huggies Pull-ups**). **For adult incontinence, by contrast, a completely new brand was developed — "while a Huggies diaper product could serve adults equally well, it was associated with CHILDREN, not adults."** Hence **Depends** and **Poise** (for women over 40 with occasional urine loss).
**FRAME OF REFERENCE.** "**Ironically, a brand's STRENGTH in the flagship category can become a LIMITATION when trying to leverage the brand in other categories.**" Coke and soft drink, McDonald's and fast food are so strongly connected that clothing would be perceived as an over-stretch.
**The counter-case Ralph Lauren:** "Although the brand was launched in the clothing category, the frame of reference has been **the goal of achieving A TIMELESS SENSE OF STYLE**, rather than a particular product category. As a result, extensions into linens, furniture, and even cuisine (Ralph Lauren restaurant) are perceived to fit."
**POINT OF DIFFERENCE.** Possibly the most potent association. **Bounty** (paper towels, "The quicker picker-upper!") could go into categories with an absorbency benefit (sponges), "**however, it is difficult to think of many such categories, especially when the hygiene category is excluded due to Bounty's association with cleaning rather than personal care.**"
**The counter-case Virgin:** "Virgin spans categories from airlines to music stores to colas. **This works because Virgin's point of difference is ITS ATTITUDE.** Virgin is the scrappy underdog that challenges the established players with an iconoclastic attitude personified by the company founder, Richard Branson. **This is equally relevant whether they're considering an airline, a music store, or a cola.**"
**REASONS TO BELIEVE.** **Coors** claims a uniquely fresh taste, supported by being brewed in the Rocky Mountains. "This association might encourage consumers to view other products that would benefit from the **Rocky Mountains point of origin** (perhaps bottled water) as fitting." [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

**The Apple illustration with a warning:** Point of difference = "empowering creativity and productivity", the basis for the extension into professional desktops and mobile computers (iMac, iBook, G3, G4) and for products that empower **play** (iPod, iTunes). Reasons to believe = user-oriented aesthetics, form, interface — used when entering monitors, potentially printers, hands-free phones and CD/DVD, "where design has traditionally taken a back seat to function".
**The warning in the same example:** Apple has particular strength in the education segment and could extend into electronic textbooks — "**however, although these extensions might be perceived to FIT the brand, they also might UNDERMINE THE GOAL of broadening the brand's reach beyond the education niche.** Thus, while an extension's fit is NECESSARY for consumers' acceptance, **consumer acceptance is only ONE consideration in the decision to launch.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

### Five levers for creating fit
1. **Advertising that discloses an unknown connection.** For Porsche Cayenne's entry into the SUV market, consumers would be more inclined to see the fit "**if they knew that the company was one of the first auto manufacturers to make an all-wheel drive vehicle (in 1904).**" Second example: **Jack Daniel's Charcoal** would fit better "if they knew that the charcoal is **a by-product of filtering the whiskey through charcoal.**"
2. **Context and media environment.** If a Guess? watch ad appears in a fashion magazine, consumers are more likely to recognise that the watch is a trendy accessory and not a precision timepiece. And: a Cadillac CTS ad in GQ or Vanity Fair "might encourage consumers to consider the **LUXURY** rather than the **GERIATRIC** associations to the parent brand."
3. **Shelf placement — the strongest single piece of evidence in the chapter.** "**Almond Accents, a line of flavored sliced almonds for use in salads and other recipes, grew to a $100 MILLION BUSINESS when placed in the PRODUCE section of grocery stores (rather than in the salad dressing aisle).**"
4. **Consumer characteristics.** "**The more involved and knowledgeable consumers are, the LESS likely they are to see a disconnect** between the parent and the extension — these consumers have a greater number of associations, so they can find rationales more easily." And: "**when consumers are in a POSITIVE MOOD, they are better able to see connections** between a parent brand and a distant extension. Consequently, marketers may enhance a distant extension's chances by using advertising strategies, **such as humor**, that both engage interest and create a positive mood."
5. **Moderate incongruity as an advantage.** "**Sometimes a MODERATE level of incongruity can be beneficial.**" On the Jack Daniel's Charcoal example: the lack of an obvious fit "can sometimes create a marketing buzz. Consumers might ask, Why did the company enter the category and what advantage might the brand offer? **If these questions can be answered to consumers' satisfaction, they may exhibit INCREASED interest and engagement — beyond what might have been achieved with an obvious fit.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

### The seven test questions before every extension
Illustrated with the Ivory case (P&G, introduced **1879**, so over 125 years old; brand value = purity, mildness, gentleness):
1. **How is the parent brand currently positioned?** *(Ivory stands for pure and gentle cleansing for sensitive skin and fabrics.)*
2. **Which elements of the positioning do I want to extend?** *(Ivory could use the association with babies, cleansing, purity or gentleness.)*
3. **Will consumers understand the logic automatically? If not, how does the marketing plan lead them there?**
4. **Will the parent brand's association be valued in the NEW category?** *(For an Ivory carpet cleaner: "Consumers might seek products that promise 'tough, deep-down cleaning' RATHER THAN GENTLENESS in this category.")*
5. **What effect does the extension have on the parent brand (backlash)?**
6. **What effect does it have on OTHER existing extensions?** *(Would a vitamin E bar only take share from the aloe bar already introduced?)*
7. **What effect does the launch have on the evaluation of COMPETING BRANDS — has the extension repositioned competitors?** *("Ivory's introduction of Liquid Hand Soap REMOVED CONVENIENT DISPENSING AS A POINT OF DIFFERENCE for competitors such as Dial.")*
"There are no particular right answers to these questions. However, their careful consideration should improve the odds." [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

### The three guidelines for extensions
**(1) GO SLOW** — "Introduce extensions that draw the brand outward from its core category **step by step**." **(2) FACILITATE PERCEPTIONS OF FIT** — use marketing mix elements so that consumers can place the extension. **(3) ASSESS EXTENSION IMPACT ON THE PARENT BRAND** — "**Any extension NECESSARILY alters consumers' perceptions of the parent brand.** Therefore, it is critical to ensure that the effects are consistent with the long-term goals." [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

### The two portfolio models
**HOUSE OF BRANDS** — "the classic and most powerful model". Each brand exists on its own; cannibalisation is minimised through **a distinct positioning per brand**; often with a separate corporate name, "**as a result, consumers are often unaware that a company's brands are all owned by the same parent.**"
Examples: **P&G** with Ivory, Tide, Pampers, Old Spice, Swiffer, Pringles, Olay — **five detergent brands in the US alone (Tide, Cheer, Gain, Dreft, Ivory)**, and "the only way a customer would know is by studying the fine print on the back of the label". **LVMH** with over 50 luxury brands — "**There is no LVMH store, no LVMH credit card, and no LVMH frequent buyer program.**"
**Four advantages:** (a) Precise target group cuts without stretching — example: **PepsiCo launched Sierra Mist as a NEW brand against Sprite; "extending the Pepsi brand into lemon-lime soda would have created confusion about the new product AND confusion about Pepsi."** (b) Easier global build-up, because brands can play where they are relevant, and "if succeeding in a country requires a low-price, low-quality product, the company can create a distinct brand **rather than risk damaging one of its more premium brands.**" (c) A corporate brand of its own — example **Altria**: until 2003 the group was called Philip Morris, with **2004 sales of over USD 89 billion and 400 brands.** The corporate identity director: "**People heard 'Philip Morris' and, rightfully so, thought 'tobacco.'**" Second problem: the group would have had to rename itself if it spun off the tobacco business. (d) **Risk spreading** — example: P&G had to take the tampon brand **Rely** off the market in the early 1980s because of its link to toxic shock syndrome; "**since Rely was a distinct brand, the crisis had relatively little impact on the rest of the P&G portfolio.**"
**Three disadvantages:** (a) Complexity — "if a company doesn't have an entrepreneurial culture, a house of brands approach can lead to **debilitating complexity**." (b) Inefficiency — "it can end up with a large number of small brands, **each one lacking the scale needed to drive substantial profits**." (c) **Risk of redundancy**, with the GM example: founded in 1908 by William Durant as a merger of leading car brands, today nine US brands (Cadillac, Chevrolet, Buick, Oldsmobile, Pontiac, GMC, Hummer, Saturn, SAAB). "At one point, each of the brands had a distinct positioning. Over time, however, the brands have become more and more similar. **In a bid to increase efficiency, the company came to sell essentially the same car under several different brand names.** ... **How is a Pontiac different from a Buick? Is Saturn really different from Chevrolet? What makes GMC unique? No one is really sure.**" Consequence: the brands compete against each other, resources fragment, **GM consistently lost market share over a decade, and Oldsmobile was discontinued.** And: **Saturn was announced in 1983 and 22 years later contributed "little to GM in the way of profitability" while adding considerably to complexity.** Plus (d): The corporate brand needs its own investment — example **Royal Bank of Scotland** with 22 brands after acquisitions: "**RBS has found that FEW PEOPLE ARE AWARE OF THE COMPANY'S SIZE AND SCOPE.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 6]

**BRANDED HOUSE** — one primary brand across several products and categories, usually with the same corporate name. Examples: **Dell** (founded 1984, 2004 sales of over USD 41 billion almost exclusively under Dell — desktops, notebooks, servers, monitors, services, printers, training programmes). **Virgin** (started in 1971 as a London record shop; according to its own website in 2005: "**We are involved in planes, trains, finance, soft drinks, music, mobile phones, holidays, cars, wines, publishing, bridal wear — the lot!**").
**Two advantages:** (a) **Focus and attention of top management** — "building the brand is essential because **without a focus on the brand, the company, BY DEFINITION, will not succeed.**" (b) **Maximum scale** — example: **Samsung spent over USD 10 billion on the Samsung brand between 1999 and 2004.** "**This would not have been possible had Samsung been splitting its spending across many individual brands.**" Plus the ability to carry out activities with high fixed costs (Samsung as main sponsor of the 2004 Summer Games).
**Three disadvantages:** (a) **Inherent stretching pressure** — "since the company has no interest in launching new brands, **EVERY new product and initiative has to fit under the primary brand.** This can lead to a weak brand positioning, with the brand **targeting essentially everyone and promising nothing in particular. This is a clear recipe for trouble.**" (b) **Slowed innovation** — "a company may not pursue good ideas simply because they don't fit." (c) **Concentration risk** — examples: **Arthur Andersen** ("when the firm was caught in a scandal due to the actions of **a few unethical employees**, the entire company was affected; **there were no other brands to fall back on**") and **Martha Stewart Living Omnimedia** with almost USD 300 million in sales after the fraud conviction. [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
**Important qualification: "most companies do NOT follow either model exclusively; they use elements of both models depending on their situations."** [Source: KOB, ch. 6]

### Sub-brands as a risk buffer — the Sony Walkman case
When the Walkman came to the US in the late 1970s, Sony was known mainly for higher-end stereo systems and televisions; the portable radio and cassette player was a new category at a **much lower price point.** The sub-brand name solved three problems: "**(1) if the Walkman failed, the sub-brand name would absorb much of the failure and PROTECT the Sony parent brand. (2) The Walkman name SEPARATED the new, lower-priced product from the higher-end Sony brand. (3) The Walkman name helped EDUCATE consumers about the nature of the new product (that it was for mobile use).**"
**And the feedback effect of success:** The Walkman became the generic category term. "As a result, Sony has been able to launch brand extensions in additional **lower-end** categories (videotapes, CDs, Discmans, Watchmans) as well as **higher-end** categories (DVD players, large-screen TVs, personal computers). **Hence, while the sub-brand strategy initially PROTECTED the Sony brand against potential failure, the success of the Walkman extension GAVE SONY PERMISSION to stretch the brand even further.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

### GE's five-level integration scheme — the only fully formalised integration tool
In 2000 and 2001 GE acquired **200 companies per year each, about four per week.** Dual purpose of the scheme: "**to PROTECT the equity of the GE brand and to LEVERAGE the brand equity of the acquired company, where appropriate.**"
| Level | Degree of connection | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | GE plus a short generic name (highest identification) | Thomson CGR became **GE Medical Systems Europe** in 1987 |
| **2** | GE with the main name of the acquisition (when the name has high brand power) | **GE Fanuc** — Fanuc had strong equity in industrial automation |
| **3** | Logo endorsement, visual association only | **Marquette Medical Systems** (1998) had so much recognition in cardiology that the existing logo was transitionally adopted into the GE format |
| **4** | verbal association only, via a tagline | **Transportation International Pool, a GE Capital Company** |
| **5** | GE invisible | **NBC** kept its own identity entirely |
**The three decision criteria as a decision tree:** **Business issues** (Does GE control the company? Is there a long-term commitment?), **Industry issues** (Is the industry seen as dynamic and innovative? What performance is expected?), **Identity issues** (Is the existing brand equity strong? What effect does the association have on GE?).
**The dynamics over time:** About two years after the acquisition Marquette was renamed GE Medical Systems Information Technologies, then run as a product umbrella brand and "**eventually phased out completely.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
**Related case:** Citigroup bought Grupo Financiero Banamex-Accival in 2001 for **USD 12.5 billion** (the largest US-Mexico merger ever) — "**less than six months later, an integrated Citibank Mexico and Banamex began operating under the BANAMEX brand name.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **The one-minute rule for complexity: "If you can't explain a brand structure in ONE MINUTE, it is too complex."** And: "**Every branding element requires investment.** Introducing a sub-brand that is meaningless to customers simply creates needless complexity. A company must invest in creating a meaning around a branding element; **otherwise it should simply use a product descriptor.**" Negative example **Purina**: in dog food alone, endorsers on Dog Chow, Alpo, Beneful, Hi Pro, Mighty Dog, Kibbles and Chunks, Moist & Meaty and One, each with flavours and many with sub-brands — "**fully understanding the options would take hours.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **The three extension test questions from the portfolio view: (1) Will the existing brand help the NEW product?** (Counter-examples: **Clorox on salad dressing** — Clorox stands for bleach; **Kikkoman soy sauce for an airline** "will simply create confusion".) **(2) Will the new product help the existing BRAND? (3) Is it a good business idea at all?** — "**Simply because a product CAN expand into a new category doesn't mean it SHOULD.** Entering an existing, well-established category with a nondifferentiated item is not likely to be successful, regardless of how compelling the brand is." [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **When a new brand instead of an extension?** When you are entering a new market and the existing brands do not fit. **Example BMW Group:** The BMW brand stands for performance ("the ultimate driving machine"); in 2001 it entered the US small-car market **not under BMW, but as the new brand Mini**; in 2003 it entered the luxury market by acquiring the rights to **Rolls-Royce**. "**The BMW Group now has three distinct brands, each positioned at a different segment.**"
  And for breakthrough products: **John Quelch, quoted: "By bringing important new products to market as line extensions, many companies LEAVE MONEY ON THE TABLE. Some product ideas are big enough to warrant a new brand."** Plus **Bob Lutz (GM): "When you're making a big change and you want to WIPE THE PSYCHOLOGICAL SLATE CLEAN, you change the name."**
  **Even the same product under two brands is possible if it is positioned against different uses:** GlaxoSmithKline introduced the molecule **bupropion** as **Wellbutrin** against depression and later as **Zyban** for smoking cessation.
  **The risk of a new brand:** "if technology rapidly changes, the primary point of difference could become irrelevant down the road, leaving the new brand **adrift**." Example **Sanka**: introduced as decaffeinated coffee; "**when the decaffeinated benefit was adopted across the category, the primary benefit of Sanka went away. Today Sanka is a weak, fading brand.**"
  **The three prerequisites before adding one:** the new brand must be distinct from the existing ones; the financial returns must be positive; **and the organisation must have the resources to manage it.** [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **Pruning is a duty, not an option.** "Brand portfolios tend to expand over time due to acquisitions and new product introductions. **Few companies DELIBERATELY set out to create unwieldy brand portfolios — but it is the natural result of expansion without disciplined pruning.**"
  **Redundancy is the worst problem: "a company should focus on taking business from its COMPETITORS, not from ITSELF. Trading sales between brands, often at great cost, is not a good way to do business."** Plus internal conflicts, because each management team fights for attention, budget and product ideas.
  **The Unilever example:** In 2000, after the Bestfoods acquisition, Unilever managed **1,600 brands worldwide.** The programme **"Path to Growth"** reduced them to **400 power brands.** From the press release: "The cornerstone of the plan is the focus of product innovation and brand development on a portfolio of around 400 leading brands, which will lead to **less fragmentation of resources and BIGGER HIT INNOVATIONS.**"
  **The incentive problem that explains why it does not happen: "There are FEW INCENTIVES to prune the brand portfolio. Pruning is not glamorous work and not the sort of activity that leads to public acclaim. At most companies, the rewards go to people who launch new initiatives and create new brands, NOT to people who identify and prune the deadwood."** John Quelch: "**In many companies, removing an SKU is HARDER than introducing a new one.**"
  **Four ways of pruning:** **divest** (sell — maximises the value, "**but it could create a new competitor. As a result, it should be done with care**"), **harvest** (manage only for short-term profit), **discontinue** (take off the market), **combine** (merge).
  **The brand protection warning with example: "Procter & Gamble LOST CONTROL of its bathroom tissue brand WHITE CLOUD when the company discontinued the brand in order to focus on Charmin. WAL-MART eventually secured rights to the White Cloud trademark and relaunched the brand. Today White Cloud remains in the market, but it is now a competitor to P&G IN THE COMPANY'S BIGGEST CUSTOMER, WAL-MART."**
  **The model for merging: "Nestlé folded its Contadina line of refrigerated pastas into the Buitoni brand. Nestlé made the change VERY GRADUALLY, first adding Buitoni to the package as an ENDORSER, then making Buitoni the PRIMARY brand and Contadina the SUB-BRAND, and finally dropping the Contadina name entirely. Nestlé MAINTAINED CATEGORY LEADERSHIP during the transition."** And the rule: "**It is easy to drive customers away from one brand, but it is far harder to ensure they are picked up by the OTHER brand in the portfolio and not by the competition.** In general, it is easier to merge brands with **frequent purchase and relatively low brand involvement**, because there is an opportunity to move consumers over time." [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **Portfolio decisions belong at top management.** Three reasons: their importance; the necessary long-term perspective ("**short-term thinking shouldn't drive a portfolio decision**"); and the trade-off between brands — "**The goal in managing a portfolio is maximizing the TOTAL, not maximizing the PARTS.** The managers responsible for delivering results for a particular brand are NOT interested in hurting their own brand's results to help another. **Senior management has to be the decision maker.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **Why these decisions weigh especially heavily — four properties:** (1) **They last for years** (Coca-Cola over 110, Ford and Harley over 100, Gucci over 80, Starbucks over 30 years old). (2) **They are hardly reversible** — "relaunching a failed new product under a different brand name is usually futile, because the opportunity to be new is gone. **A product can only be new ONCE.**" (3) **They become emotional** — "the brand may even give the team a sense of identity, just as some brands give their CUSTOMERS a sense of identity." (4) **There is no arithmetically unambiguous answer** — "there are no analyses that will conclusively prove that one decision is right ... **The decisions have to be based on strategic thinking and good judgment.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **The denial warning:** A Kraft manager often said "I don't believe in portfolio strategy". "**This, of course, ignores the obvious: Managers are ALWAYS making portfolio strategy decisions. Each time a company launches a new product it makes a portfolio decision, and each time a company sets financial targets or allocates spending it makes a portfolio decision.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 6]

## Typical problems and approaches to solutions

**"We have too many variants, and customers are confused."** The most concrete evidence: "**A plethora of versions can CONFUSE consumers about which version is the right one for them, SENDING THEM SEARCHING FOR A BRAND WITH FEWER OPTIONS.**" A woman looking for a moisturiser for dry skin has to decide at Olay between **Olay Total Effects Moisturizer, Olay Age Defying Cream, Olay Active Hydrating Fluid and Cream and Olay Pro Vital Moisture Cream.**
**The counter-design: "In contrast, SIMPLE GREEN touts itself as the strong cleaner that is good for EVERYTHING. Although it is unlikely to unseat 409 and Fantastik ... Simple Green has consistently been in the TOP-10 SKUs in sales and total units, suggesting that its simple premise holds appeal. LEVER 2000 has pursued a similar approach in the bar soap category, and it also has enjoyed success."** [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

**"The line extension brings sales in the short term — is that enough?"** Four risks:
- **Dilution:** "line extensions can possibly **dilute** associations to the parent brand and thereby make its appeal less apparent."
- **Resource diversion:** Example — switching a pharmaceutical product from three-times to once-daily (Wellbutrin-XL versus -SR) "**could take time from pursuing promising larger leaps in drug innovation.**"
- **Short life:** With fads or purely cosmetic, easily copied changes the gain is short-lived. Examples: **SpongeBob Square Pants cereals are viable only as long as the character holds interest**; and "**the competitive advantage of Pledge cleaning wipes was ERASED when Endust quickly followed with a similar product.**" Consequence: "line extensions only make good business sense when the cost structure allows profitability in the short term **and includes a clear plan for EXITING the market when profits erode.**"
- **The shelf-space trap:** More facings narrow the space for competitors (example: **General Mills' Cheerios extensions — the advertising money for the flagship brand and Honey Nut allowed Frosted Cheerios "to capture half a share point in the multibillion-dollar cereal category IN ITS FIRST WEEK of national distribution"**). But: "retailers often prefer to reserve additional facings for their OWN store-branded products. As a result, shelf facings may be **CANNIBALIZED from the existing brand allotment** and are likely to be quickly withdrawn if the extension fails. **And the space lost to a failed line extension may be DIFFICULT TO REGAIN.** Further, if the extension enjoys consumer acceptance, **the retailer may quickly introduce a store-branded version** to capture higher margins." Plus SKU management complexity (production, marketing programmes, forecasting, shelf sets, plan-o-grams, sales per SKU). [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

**"Category extension or not?"** Additional benefit: "it may bring new users to the franchise by providing **MULTIPLE POINTS OF ENTRY** into a relationship with the brand." Example **Nike**: across running shoes, basketball, golf, fashion and more — "once consumers experience the brand's performance-oriented sports gear in one category, they may be more likely to turn to the brand in others."
Additional risks: **Backlash on disappointing performance**, "because the company lacks the same level of expertise in that category"; **dilution** through weakening of the link to the original category; and the loss of efficiency: "**advertising efficiencies attributed to line extensions are LESS likely to be realized with category extensions. Category extensions typically require SEPARATE campaigns to establish brand membership in the new category and to demonstrate the new relevance of the point of difference.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 5]

**"How far is too far?" — the list of failed over-stretches.** "**Coors launched a line of spring waters. Harley-Davidson developed wine coolers and perfume. Jack Daniel's introduced Jack Daniel's mustard. Levi Strauss created Levi Strauss Tailored Classics, a line of suits. BIC developed both a perfume and a line of women's underwear. Smith and Wesson launched a line of mountain bikes. In all of these cases the extensions did not fit with the base brands, and the extensions FAILED.**"
**The positive example for contrast — Gerber:** In 1927 Dorothy and Daniel Gerber began with strained peas, prunes, carrots and spinach; today **190 different baby food products** plus baby skin care, shampoos and utensils — "**all of the products unified by A FOCUS ON INFANTS.**" The counter-test: "**Extending the Gerber brand into frozen pizzas would weaken the meaning of the brand.**"
**Jack Trout, "Differentiate or Die", quoted: "The more things you try to become and the more you lose focus, the more difficult it is to differentiate your product."** [Source: KOB, ch. 6]

**"Simple is also a strategy."** The counter-example to portfolio complexity: **Red Bull.** Founded in 1984 with **one** product (8.3-oz energy drink), no further product for almost **two decades**, Sugar Free in 2003. So: one company, one brand, one category, two products. "**In 2004 it sold over ONE BILLION CANS, providing evidence that simple brand structures can deliver strong performance.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 6]

**"Should we get broader at all?" — OPEN POINT, two positions.**
- **KOB ch. 5, formulated as a general rule and supported by two magazine sources: "When executed properly, A BROAD BRAND — one that has extended into a variety of product categories — IS STRONGER THAN A NARROWER BRAND. As brand breadth increases, favorability toward subsequent brand extensions and confidence in its benefits and associations also increase."** With the limit: "However, brand expansion cannot proceed unchecked."
- **TIM: the smallest viable market.** "**The relentless pursuit of mass will make you boring**, because mass means average" [Source: TIM, ch. 4]; "It's not for you" as a gesture of respect; and the resource rule not to waste a minute on people to the right of the adoption curve [Source: TIM, ch. 14]. Plus the Nike hotel test as a fit thought experiment without a checklist [Source: TIM, ch. 13].
- **Both positions are tenable and answer different questions: KOB speaks of established brands with existing association equity and trade access, TIM of building something new with scarce means.** Can be named as a synthesis, not as a decision.
- **Remarkable, and to be passed on when quoting: TIM's position is present inside KOB as a minority finding** — the Olay confusion, the praise for Simple Green and Lever 2000, the dilution warning, the SKU complexity, the private-label cannibalisation (all ch. 5), plus Red Bull, the over-stretch list, the Trout quote and the one-minute rule (all ch. 6). **KOB ch. 5 and ch. 6 stand directly next to each other, refer to each other and give opposite basic stances.** See the contradiction register, internal KOB contradiction.

## Concrete examples
- **Failed extensions as a collection** (KOB ch. 6): Harley wine coolers and perfume, Jack Daniel's mustard, Levi's suits, BIC perfume and women's underwear, Smith and Wesson mountain bikes, Coors spring water. **Every case shows the same mechanic: the extension was possible for the brand, but not appropriate.** [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **Red Bull as the counter-case of narrowness:** a single product over years, with market leadership as the result. [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **Porsche Cayenne**, diagnosed differently in two chapters: as a naming problem (ch. 2) and as a fit-communication problem, because Porsche has built all-wheel drive since 1904 (ch. 5). **See Open points.**
- **Ingredient brands with legal protection:** Glad FreshScent, Chrysler's Hemi, Westin's Heavenly Bed. A vague formulation is easy to copy, a marked component is not, **because it is legally protected.** [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **GM redundancy** as evidence that portfolio brands take sales from each other, often at high cost. [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **Microsoft's 2003 architecture with .NET as an ingredient brand** across several product lines; **IBM ThinkPad X30** as a fully declined name hierarchy. [Source: KOB, ch. 11]
- **The co-branding asymmetry:** HP and Dell would benefit **less** from co-branding with Intel than smaller manufacturers. **The benefit of co-branding is inversely proportional to one's own brand strength.** [Source: KOB, ch. 11]
