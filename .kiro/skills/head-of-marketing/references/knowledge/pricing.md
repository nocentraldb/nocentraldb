# Price, margin and brand-value protection

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Tybout/Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018.

## Character of the sources for this subject area
**The two books treat the same subject from two sides and support each other.** **TIM is the denser source for price as an instrument of story and growth** — with the only fully worked margin calculation in the system (the baker calculation), the free paradox and the counter-intuitive trust inversion. **KOB is the denser source for the destruction side** — the cash challenge, the price promotion doom loop and three experimentally supported price-sensitivity findings. **KOB has no chapter of its own on pricing**; the material lies in the introduction and in ch. 4 and ch. 9. **TIM carries a vested interest** (selling a seminar).

## Core concepts and definitions

### Price is a story, not just an amount
**The double rule: "Marketing changes your pricing. Pricing changes your marketing."** And the alignment prescription: "**Your price should be ALIGNED WITH THE EXTREMES you claimed as part of your positioning.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 16]
The wine test: "Are you the kind of person who buys the cheapest wine on the menu? What about the most expensive? **Notice that neither question said anything about the wine itself. Not about its taste or its value. SIMPLY THE PRICE.**"
"No one drives the cheapest possible car (**you hardly ever see a used Yugo going down the street**) and few are foolish enough to drive a Bugatti around town. **But within those extremes, countless stories are being told.**" And the signal case: "**A Porsche Cayenne has no conceivable utility proportionate to its expense. It's merely a signal, a silver or red painted flag we fly high in our driveway AND IN THE THEATER OF OUR SELF-ESTEEM.**"
**The maxim: "Better to apologize for the price ONCE than to have to excuse a hundred small slights again and again. PRICE IS A SIGNAL."** [Source: TIM, ch. 16]

### Price as the engine of growth
"The price is more than a signal. **It's also the ENGINE for our project's growth**, because price determines what we stand for, who we're designing for, and the story we tell. **And price creates (or eliminates) MARGIN, and that margin is the money that's available to spend on our outbound marketing.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 16]

### The baker calculation — the most precise margin calculation in the system
Ingredients and overhead per loaf at a reasonable volume: **USD 1.95.**
| Selling price | Profit per loaf | Ratio |
|---|---|---|
| **USD 2.00** | **5 cents** | baseline |
| **USD 2.50** | **55 cents** | **elevenfold, more than 1,000 percent more profit per loaf** |
| **USD 3.00** | **more than USD 1.00** | **more than twentyfold** |
**The consequence: "The baker who charges two dollars a loaf has to sell TWENTY-ONE LOAVES for every loaf the luxury baker sells at three dollars. Twenty-one times as many is the difference between A FEW CUSTOMERS AN HOUR OR A LINE OUT THE DOOR."**
**The reply to "our customers would rather pay less":** How do they value the spotless shop with plenty of well-paid, helpful staff, a new sign in the window, a local baseball team with jerseys carrying the shop's logo? How do they value the beautiful shopping bag with every loaf and the free samples of the little butter cookies called **punitions**? **"How does it make them feel to tell their friends that they're eating THE SAME BREAD THAT'S SERVED AT THE FANCY RESTAURANT DOWN THE STREET?"** [Source: TIM, ch. 16]

### The cash challenge — the counterpart on the destruction side
**The basic problem, named by KOB as the single biggest challenge of branding: "Executives need to deliver SHORT-TERM financial results, but brands are LONG-TERM assets."**
**The present-value statement: "Virtually all of a brand's value resides in the future; the current-year financial returns are a very small part of the total. If a brand delivers a steady stream of cash flow in perpetuity, LESS THAN 5 PERCENT of the value of the brand resides in the FIRST YEAR, assuming a discount rate of 5 percent."**
Documented brand longevity as contrast: **Harvard 1636, Moët & Chandon 1743, Pepsi 1898** — all still alive and valuable today.
**The incentive irony, verbatim: "In a supreme bit of irony of business, a manager who BOOSTS SHORT-TERM PROFITS WHILE DAMAGING THE LONG-TERM HEALTH of a brand is OFTEN REWARDED, while a manager who INVESTS in a brand at the expense of short-term results is OFTEN PENALIZED."**
**The cost-benefit asymmetry that explains it: "The BENEFITS are difficult to quantify, UNCERTAIN, and in the FUTURE. The COSTS are quantifiable, CERTAIN, and IMMEDIATE."**
Quote from a former Kraft colleague: "**Good numbers don't guarantee your success, but bad numbers will get you every time.**"
**The career warning to brand investors: "They will invest in their brand without setting proper expectations, and if short-term results are weak, THESE MANAGERS MAY NOT SURVIVE IN THEIR POSITION LONG ENOUGH TO SEE THE BENEFITS OF THEIR INVESTMENT."** [Source: KOB, introduction]

### The price promotion doom loop
The cycle: business results are weak -> cut prices -> to fund the cut, cut service and marketing -> **sales rise in the short term** -> but competitors react, price expectations shift, the customer experience deteriorates -> business results are weak again -> start over.
**The three named long-term harms:** (1) Competitive reaction. (2) **Shifted price expectation** — "**A buy-one-get-one-free offer is motivating and exciting the FIRST time, and perhaps the SECOND time. But eventually customers come to EXPECT it, so companies must cut prices FURTHER to create excitement and drive sales.**" (3) Brand weakening, because brand-building programmes were cut.
**The core mechanism: "It is ASTONISHINGLY EASY for brands to get caught in a ‚branding doom loop.'"** And the cause lies not in incompetence but in the incentives: "**It's usually the CAREER-OPTIMIZING decision.**" [Source: KOB, introduction]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### The three price-sensitivity findings from research
1. **"Brand leaders tend as a result to ask for and OBTAIN HIGHER PRICES."**
2. **"Brand leaders tend to be better able to INCREASE prices without losing business, compared to rivals with weaker brands."** (Sivakumar/Raj 1997, *Journal of Marketing*.)
3. **The counter-intuitive one: "One might predict that leading brands have the most to lose and, therefore, would lose most when a weaker rival cuts price. Yet, research shows that PRICE CUTS BY RIVALS TEND TO DRAW THE MOST SHARE NOT FROM THE POWERFUL BRANDS BUT FROM THE WEAKEST."** (Carpenter/Cooper/Hanssens/Midgley 1988, *Marketing Science*.) [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
**The reason for price insensitivity:** "Brands help reduce that subjectivity and convey to customers information that they **cannot easily observe** through testing or analysis." The example question no test answers: "**What will be the supplier's responsiveness to any unforeseen or unanticipated problems that occur in the customer's usage?**" And: "**Greater brand familiarity also creates more confidence, increases purchase intentions, and can even OVERCOME NEGATIVE EXPERIENCES.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 9]

### The me-too price paradox — the strongest single piece of evidence against the price attack
A me-too product (identical to the market leader, but cheaper) suffers **despite** its similarity: "**the more similar the me-too and pioneer, the greater the relative prominence of the pioneer.** This happens because the me-too brand **derives its identity from the pioneer**, and greater similarity reduces the distinctiveness of the me-too brand. ... it remains inferior because it has **no distinctive competence.**"
**The experiment:** Consumers observed a market pioneer followed by either no entrant, a differentiated entrant (same price) or a me-too (cheaper), and then rated the same brands at the same prices.
**Result: "the pioneer followed by the ME-TOO brand is LEAST price sensitive; the pioneer followed by NO entrant is the MOST price sensitive; and the pioneer followed by the DIFFERENTIATED entrant is midway between the two."**
**The explanation: "By adopting a me-too position, the later entrant SIGNALS TO BUYERS THAT THE PIONEER IS INDEED IN THE BEST POSITION. The me-too brand implicitly ADMITS this position by adopting it and then advertising it as such. THIS STRATEGY REINFORCES THE DOMINANCE OF THE PIONEER AND CAN LEAD TO INCREASES IN ITS MARKET SHARE."**
**The historical evidence: "One example occurred in the cola market, when Pepsi attacked the pioneer, Coca-Cola, in the famous PEPSI CHALLENGE. AS A RESULT OF THAT ATTACK COCA-COLA'S MARKET SHARE ROSE, despite Pepsi's claim of superior taste."** [Source: KOB, ch. 4]
**And the price rule for differentiators that follows from it: "High-profit differentiation strategies are supported by HIGH PRICES (as opposed to pricing to be a me-too to the pioneer) and HIGH ADVERTISING SUPPORT (as opposed to being a no-name rival)."** [Source: KOB, ch. 4]

### Price differentiation — history and today's limits
**The origin of the price tag:** "**The Quakers invented the price tag.** Before that, it was generally accepted that nothing had a firm price. **Everyone haggled.** But Macy's and Wanamaker's needed to get big, to build huge stores with low-paid staff. **There was no way to train and trust that many people to haggle.** And so they pioneered the Quaker idea at scale."
**The irony of the origin: "While the price tag was originally conceived because Quakers thought it was IMMORAL to charge different prices to different people, IT CAUGHT ON BECAUSE INDUSTRIALISTS AND BIG ORGANIZATIONS LIKED THE EFFICIENCY."**
**Two counter-examples from today: Tesla** told luxury car buyers the story "the price is the price", **"and they breathed a sigh of relief."** **Uber**, by contrast, tried to adjust prices to demand — **"and it cost their brand BILLIONS IN TRUST."**
**The diagnosis: "For most organizations, particularly small ones, THE HARD PART ISN'T THE MECHANICS of charging different amounts. IT'S THE STORYTELLING."**
**The test questions for one's own price story:** "How do you feel when you find out that you got a discount that no one else got? What if you **deserved** it? How do you feel if **other** people got that discount and you didn't? What about the scarcity and pricing built into Kickstarter? **Does the fear of missing out on a level that's almost full push you to act?**" [Source: TIM, ch. 16]

### The trust inversion — price and risk
"**The rational thing is to believe that we're more likely to require trust BEFORE we engage in risky transactions.** And it's also rational to expect that people are more likely to want more trust before spending a lot of money. **Many times, though, THE OPPOSITE IS TRUE.**"
**The mechanism: cognitive dissonance. "We INVENT a feeling of trust precisely BECAUSE we're spending a lot. ‚I'm a smart person, and the smart thing to do would be to be sure I trust someone before investing my life savings (or my life), SO I MUST TRUST THIS PERSON.'"**
**Four pieces of evidence: "That's what BOOT CAMP is for. The high cost of participation (blood, sweat, and tears) causes us to become ALIGNED with the group. That's why people change at OUTWARD BOUND. THAT'S WHY HIGH-END RESTAURANTS AND HOTELS CAN SURVIVE BAD REVIEWS."**
"When people are heavily invested (cash or reputation or effort), **they often MAKE UP A STORY to justify their commitment. And that story carries trust.**"
**The conclusion, which directly contradicts the discount reflex: "EVERY CON MAN KNOWS THIS. The irony is that marketers who need to be trusted often DON'T understand it. LOWERING YOUR PRICE DOESN'T MAKE YOU MORE TRUSTED. IT DOES THE OPPOSITE."** [Source: TIM, ch. 16]

### The free paradox and its resolution
**Two reasons against fully free:** (1) "**Engaging in a transaction is fundamentally different than encountering an apparently worthless (or at least priceless) object that's been freely shared. Scarcity, tension, and enrollment ALL exist when we must decide to make a purchase, and the marketer SACRIFICES ALL OF THESE when a purchase is truly free.**" (2) "Without cash flow, you can't invest in your product, your team, or your marketing."
**But free is a category of its own, not a low price: "Free is not simply a penny less than a penny, a dollar less than a dollar. It's an ENTIRELY DIFFERENT CATEGORY of transaction, because LIKE DIVIDING BY ZERO, IT SCALES TO INFINITY."** And: "**A free idea is far more likely to spread, and spread quickly, than an idea that's tethered to money.**"
**Two counterfactual pieces of evidence: "If Facebook cost three dollars a month to use, it would have attracted FEWER THAN A MILLION USERS. If it cost money to listen to the hits on the radio, THE TOP 40 WOULD DISAPPEAR."**
**The resolution — two offers married to each other: "(1) FREE IDEAS THAT SPREAD. (2) EXPENSIVE EXPRESSIONS of those ideas that are worth paying for."**
Evidence: The chef gives away recipes, appears on podcasts, runs online seminars — easy to find, frequently used, easy to share. "**But if you want to eat that pasta served on china on a white tablecloth at her restaurant, it's going to cost you twenty-four dollars.**" If the song on the radio is free but the concert ticket costs **eighty-four dollars**, the artist gets paid.
**The maxim: "The china and the ticket are SOUVENIRS OF IDEAS, and souvenirs are supposed to be EXPENSIVE."**
And the purpose of the free part: "There are countless ways for you to share your vision, your ideas, your digital expressions, your ability to connect — for free. **And each of them builds AWARENESS, PERMISSION, AND TRUST, which gives you a platform to sell the thing that's worth paying for.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 16]

### The tenfold question
"What's the difference between a thirty-dollar massage and a three hundred-dollar one? What could make a book worth two hundred dollars? Or a hotel room worth fifteen hundred dollars? What could cause someone to give five hundred dollars to charity instead of fifty?"
**"‚MORE OF THE SAME' IS THE WRONG ANSWER."** Reason: "**We don't pay ten times extra for more words, a bigger order of French fries, or a louder stereo.** Instead, it's **a different extreme, a different story, a different sort of scarcity.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 7]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **"‚Cheap' is another way to say ‚scared'."** "Unless you've found an extraordinary new way to deliver your service or product, **racing to be the cheapest probably means that you're NOT INVESTING SUFFICIENTLY IN CHANGE. When you're the cheapest, you're not promising CHANGE. You're promising THE SAME, BUT CHEAPER.**"
  Why it is so tempting: "**Nothing is easier to sell than cheaper.** It requires no new calculations or deep thinking on the part of your customer. **It's not cultural or emotional. It's simply cheaper.**"
  **The verdict: "LOW PRICE IS THE LAST REFUGE OF A MARKETER WHO HAS RUN OUT OF GENEROUS IDEAS."** [Source: TIM, ch. 16]
- **The limit of generosity — what is NOT generous.** "Generosity in terms of **free work, constant discounts, and plenty of uncompensated overtime** isn't really generous. **Because you can't sustain it. Because soon you'll be BREAKING THE PROMISES you made.**" Generous, by contrast, is "**showing generosity with your BRAVERY, your EMPATHY, and your RESPECT.**"
  And the consequence: "What your customers want from you is for you to **care enough to change them.** To create tension that leads to forward motion. To exert emotional labor that will open them up to what's possible. **And if you need to charge a lot to pull that off, IT'S STILL A BARGAIN.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 16]
- **Every purchase is a bargain — the basis for arguing a price.** "**Everything that we purchase — every investment, every trinket, every experience — IS A BARGAIN. That's why we bought it. Because it was worth MORE than what we paid for it. Otherwise, we wouldn't buy it.**" From this follows the seller's duty: "**if you're unwilling to have empathy for the narrative of the person you seek to serve, YOU'RE STEALING** — because you're withholding a valuable option. You're keeping someone from understanding how much they'll benefit from what you've created ... such a significant benefit that **it's a bargain.**"
  And the counterweight that protects the statement from overreach: "**If they understand what's on offer and choose NOT to buy it, then IT'S NOT FOR THEM. Not today, not at this price, not with that structure. That's okay too.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 5]
- **A discount is not competition but self-harm.** From the portfolio context: "Redundant brands inevitably compete with each other. This is inefficient — **a company should focus on taking business from its COMPETITORS, not from ITSELF. Trading sales between brands, often at great cost, is not a good way to do business.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **Irrationally making yourself irresistible can be the right price decision.** The evidence: In front of Fiona's shop at the **Opinicon** resort near Ottawa there is often a queue — delicious ice cream, enormous portions, a waffle cone under three Canadian dollars, served with a smile. After the cone you stroll around, hang out by the water "and maybe start to make plans about where to spend a week of next year's vacation."
  **"The Opinicon could charge a lot more for an ice cream cone. A team of MBAs doing a market analysis and a profit and loss report would probably pin the value at ABOUT EIGHT DOLLARS. THAT'S WHERE THE RETURN ON INVESTMENT WOULD BE AT ITS PEAK. But they're not in the business of selling ice cream cones. The ice cream cones are a SYMBOL, A BEACON, A CHANCE TO ENGAGE."**
  **The rule: "If you run everything through a spreadsheet, you might end up with a rational plan, BUT THE RATIONAL PLAN ISN'T WHAT CREATES ENERGY OR MAGIC OR MEMORIES."**
  **The counter-example that shows the decline — Stew Leonard's:** a small supermarket with a big footprint, profiled by Tom Peters, with the **highest revenue per square metre of its kind** — an experience, almost an amusement park, with remarkable service, clever merchandising, interesting products. As the company grew, a new generation of owners took over, "who seemed more intent on **short-term profit** and less focused on magic. **For a while, profits INCREASED. But now, year after year, it's a bit less crowded, a bit less energetic, a bit less interesting.** So when a new store opens nearby, they lose a few more customers, then a few more, and finally, people begin to wonder, **‚Why do I even bother coming here in the first place?'**"
  **The conclusion: "It might not be about being cheaper. It's tricky to define better. But without a doubt, THE HEART AND SOUL OF A THRIVING ENTERPRISE IS THE IRRATIONAL PURSUIT OF BECOMING IRRESISTIBLE."** [Source: TIM, ch. 7]
  **Note: this case and KOB's doom loop tell the same story** — short-term optimisation first raises profit and then destroys the substance. **TIM observes it as a loss of experience, KOB models it as a cycle. That is a two-level convergence, not a contradiction.**
- **Lifetime value as the price frame.** The supermarket calculation: the profit on a single shopping trip is only "**a dollar or two. Supermarkets have very low margins.**" But if the person becomes a regular — **twice a week, USD 100 each time, over the five years in the neighbourhood** — "that's **FIFTY THOUSAND DOLLARS OR MORE IN SALES. Even at a 2 percent profit margin, it means that there's A THOUSAND DOLLARS IN PROFIT from each new customer over time.**"
  **The two consequences for action: "This means that a supermarket should be EAGER TO SPONSOR AN EVENT FOR NEW RESIDENTS in town, because the funnel is so efficient. And it means a supermarket ought to QUICKLY GIVE AN APOLOGY AND REFUND to a customer who's upset about a FOUR-DOLLAR MELON not being ripe. IT'S HARDLY WORTH A THOUSAND DOLLARS IN LOST SALES TO HAVE AN ARGUMENT."**
  **The Slack comparison:** An early customer might have had a lifetime value of **USD 50,000 or more**, if one counts not only their payments but the effect on colleagues, the value of growth in locking out competitors, and the equity value. **"The first thousand customers, if they're the right people, are practically PRICELESS."**
  **The stamp calculation as the simplest way in:** If a letter costs 50 cents and you need a thousand letters for one order, each order costs **USD 500.** If the lifetime value is **USD 700: "buy as many stamps as you can possibly afford!"** If it is **USD 400: "you have no business buying stamps. YOU NEED A BETTER LETTER OR A BETTER BUSINESS."** "That simple analysis is why you've heard of **L.L. Bean, Lands' End, and Victoria's Secret. They bought a lot of stamps.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 19]
- **Ingredient brands as legally protected margin protection.** "**A vague phrase, such as ‚heavy duty' or ‚high quality,' is EASY for competitors to copy. A branded ingredient or service is NOT, BECAUSE IT IS LEGALLY PROTECTED.**" Evidence: **Glad FreshScent, Chrysler's Hemi, Westin's Heavenly Bed.** [Source: KOB, ch. 6]
- **When superiority cannot be sustained, bundling becomes price protection.** "**A commodity is a commodity because customers have come to believe, RIGHTLY OR WRONGLY, that there is no meaningful difference between brands. As a result, PRICE IS THE ONLY REMAINING BASIS FOR CHOICE. By bundling commodities together, ONE CREATES DIFFERENCES WHERE NONE EXISTED. Suddenly the basis of comparison MUST SHIFT FROM PRICE.**" In addition: time savings, lower transaction costs, a bigger role for the brand name, **and lower vulnerability to price competition and innovation.** [Source: KOB, ch. 9 — details in `b2b.md`]

## Typical problems and approaches to solving them

**"We have to cut the price to stay competitive."** Four counter-arguments from the system: (1) The doom loop — the cut is funded by cutting service and marketing and destroys exactly the substance that justified the price. [KOB, introduction] (2) The shift in expectation — what was once exciting becomes expected, and one has to cut further. [KOB, introduction] (3) The me-too paradox — the price attack **confirms** the position of the one attacked and can increase its share (Pepsi Challenge). [KOB, ch. 4] (4) The trust inversion — **"Lowering your price doesn't make you more trusted. It does the opposite."** [TIM, ch. 16]
And the margin calculation against it: a rise from USD 2.00 to 2.50 at a cost of USD 1.95 **multiplies** the profit per unit **elevenfold**; one would have to sell twenty-one times as much to break even at 2.00 instead of 3.00 USD. [TIM, ch. 16]

**"How do we communicate a price rise nobody asked for?"** The worked case: **Union Square Hospitality Group (USHG) abolished tipping in 2016.** Context: the **Union Square Cafe** had been the top-rated restaurant in the New York Zagat for over a decade; the group comprised almost a dozen highly regarded restaurants and had **spun out Shake Shack, a billion-dollar company.**
**The measure: prices raised by 20 percent, the additional revenue used for parental leave, fair wages and "the chance to treat their team as PROFESSIONALS".** Effect: the kitchen (which actually cooks the food) is paid better, and the service staff have an incentive to work together and swap shifts — **"to work the way a doctor, a pilot, or a teacher might — FOR THE WORK, NOT FOR A TIP."**
**"This is great leadership, BUT IT PRESENTS A HOST OF MARKETING PROBLEMS."** The three concrete communication problems:
1. To the **regular**, "who values the perception of a special relationship because he sees himself as an **above-average tipper**".
2. To the **tourist**, who compares menu prices online **"and doesn't know that having tips included makes the restaurant MUCH CHEAPER than it appears"**.
3. To the **staff**, "particularly the highest-earning servers, **who stand to see their wages GO DOWN**".
**The central insight: such a change cannot be for everyone.** "Some diners find joy in the status they get by leaving a big tip. They do it with a flourish, and, in the scheme of things for someone who's well off, **it's a cheap thrill. USHG CAN'T OFFER THAT THRILL ANY LONGER. ‚It's not for you, sorry.'**"
**The counter-calculation:** A guest who seeks **affiliation** as a form of status finds a sincere thank-you better than the fear of giving too little or too much. **And: "the diner who has a worldview that revolves around FAIRNESS AND DIGNITY now has a HARDER TIME PATRONIZING OTHER RESTAURANTS."**
And the social lever: "**Dining in a restaurant is rarely a solo endeavor.** USHG gives hosts a chance to gain status through **virtue signaling.** They give diners a story they can tell themselves (and others) — **a story about how the small act of choosing a restaurant TURNS THE RATCHET on a much larger issue around race, gender, and income disparity.**"
**The test questions at the end: "Who's it for, what's it for, and how is status changed? What will I tell the others?"** [Source: TIM, ch. 16]

**"Our margin doesn't support any marketing."** According to TIM this is not a marketing question but a pricing question: margin **is** the marketing budget ("that margin is the money that's available to spend on our outbound marketing"). And according to funnel logic: "**If you can measure the funnel and it costs too much for you to afford ads, don't buy the ads. FIX THE FUNNEL FIRST.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 16 and 19]

**"The customer is complaining about a trifle."** The lifetime-value calculation decides, not the amount in dispute: at USD 1,000 lifetime profit, an argument about a four-dollar melon is economically absurd. And with a customer at **eight times the average revenue**, "the cost of being human in this situation ... easily covered by the upside of delighting an extraordinary customer" is justified. [Source: TIM, ch. 19 and 14]

**"What is the brand actually worth?"** The answer lies in `measurement.md`; relevant as a price anchor is the range of the brand's contribution to demand: "**value can be as low as 1 PERCENT (or less) of revenue for a commoditized, industrialized product, or 50 TO 70 PERCENT (or more) for a well-known luxury brand.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 13]

## Concrete examples
- **The closing-down sale as a tension machine:** "The logic of the going-out-of-business sale is elusive. After all, **if the store was any good, it wouldn't be going out of business.** And if a customer is hoping for support, a warranty, or a chance for a return, buying something from a store that's about to disappear isn't very smart. **And yet, people can't resist a bargain.** That's because the **scarcity** of the going-out-of-business sale creates tension. The tension of ‚**What bargains did I miss?**' The best way to relieve that tension is to go to the store and check it out." [Source: TIM, ch. 10]
- **Further scarcity tensions with a price connection:** "**Supreme is only making 250 of these sneakers. I'm getting a pair — are you coming?**" And: "The last three houses that sold on our block went for less than anyone expected. **If we don't sell soon, we'll never be able to cover our mortgage.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 10]
- **Mondi Rotatrim as a price premium from genuine superiority:** In South Africa paper was a commodity until Mondi in the 1980s became **the first company to switch from guillotine to rotary cutting** (more precise, no pressure on the edge, only four or five sheets at a time) and thereby reduced copier jams. In the 1990s **all major competitors** followed, and the technical advantage was gone. **In 1997 came the next step: an alkaline-based formulation with precipitated calcium carbonate, EIGHT TIMES less abrasive** than the competitors' ground variety. The South African Bureau of Standards tested it: **Rotatrim was the ONLY brand that met the standards for superior business paper.** "Rivals, disadvantaged with **lower prices and smaller volumes**, were unable or unwilling to match Mondi's spending. ... Mondi Rotatrim enjoyed a stable and comfortable **35 percent market share.** Its perceived superior performance enabled Mondi Rotatrim to **SUSTAIN A PRICING PREMIUM** in certain markets while maintaining its customer share." [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **Texas Instruments — focus as a margin decision:** Semiconductors were considered a commodity with price and profit cycles. TI concentrated on digital signal processors for mobile phone makers such as Nokia, who were caught in a price war. "**TI recognized that FOR THEM SPEED WAS PERHAPS MORE IMPORTANT THAN PRICE**", and delivered bundles of DSPs, software, development tools and services. Former CEO Thomas Engibous: "**It's the difference between making a spark plug or building the engine.**" **The explicitly named price of the strategy: "by focusing on DSPs, TI REDUCED THE NUMBER OF CUSTOMERS IT CAN SERVE. But in exchange for those limits, its margin, sales growth, market share, and profits have all grown faster than rivals' ... its market capitalization has increased FIVEFOLD."** [Source: KOB, ch. 9]
- **The loyalty figures as a price argument: "In some categories, an increase in customer loyalty of just 2 PERCENT is equivalent to a 10 PERCENT COST-REDUCTION PROGRAM, while an increase of 5 PERCENT in loyal customers can deliver 95 PERCENT GREATER PROFITABILITY over the lifetime of each customer. Moreover, other studies have shown that customers are willing to pay AS MUCH AS 25 PERCENT MORE for a brand to which they have loyalty."** [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **John Deere as an example of the brand as price justification:** "John Deere has always charged a premium for its farm equipment **because its name is commensurate with the product's value and with the brand's promise.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 12]
- **Purchasing-power reality as the price frame in international business:** For consumers who earn **USD 200 a month**: "**they can buy ONE OR TWO COOKIES or ONE BEVERAGE**" — hence single-serve strategies. In some markets such formats made up **about 50 percent, sometimes up to 70 percent of revenue**. [Source: KOB, ch. 17]

## Open points (register of contradictions)
The following points are **documented, not resolved.** Full evidence in the register in `../research/system-overview.md`. For requests that touch them, **both positions** are to be presented.

- **None of the four contradictions between the books lies in this area.** Both sources converge strikingly on price (the price attack harms the attacker; short-term optimisation destroys substance).
- **What remains open is the shape of the source material itself:** KOB has no pricing chapter; the material comes from the introduction, ch. 4 and ch. 9. On price architecture, elasticity and the legal limits of price differentiation, neither book has anything.
