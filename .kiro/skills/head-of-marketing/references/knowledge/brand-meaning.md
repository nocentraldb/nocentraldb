# Brand, promise and meaning

## Source abbreviations
- **KOB** = *Kellogg on Branding*, Alice M. Tybout / Tim Calkins (eds.), Kellogg School of Management, 2005. An edited volume of faculty and practitioner chapters.
- **TIM** = *This is Marketing*, Seth Godin, 2018. A practitioner's manifesto, the written version of the author's own seminar.

## Character of the sources for this subject area
**KOB is the denser source here for mechanics and measurability**: it supplies three fully formulated brand definitions, the perceptual psychology of cues, an eight-element design grid, a testing methodology and a seven-part meaning audit. **TIM is the denser source for the litmus test and investment discipline**: brand value as interchangeability, frugality with logos, semiotics as an everyday phenomenon. **KOB dates from 2005 and does not know social media, smartphones or platform economics**; TIM from 2018 takes them for granted. **TIM carries a vested interest** (the author sells the seminar the book refers to). **KOB's practitioner chapters consistently report the success of the author's own organisation without a dissenting voice.**

## Core concepts and definitions

### Four competing brand definitions — deliberately not harmonised
1. **Brand as a bundle of associations.** "A brand is a set of associations linked to a name, mark, or symbol associated with a product or service. The difference between a name and a brand is that a name doesn't have associations. ... A brand is much like a reputation." Associations can be **positive or negative** (Enron: financial mismanagement, fraud, bankruptcy; ValuJet after the Everglades crash: dangerous, reckless, poor maintenance). [Source: KOB, introduction]
2. **Brand as a concept.** "Fundamentally, a brand is a concept" — a bundle of properties and associations that gives a product meaning, analogous to the way "chair" is a concept we apply to a piece of wood. Calder explicitly declares positioning, promise, essence and value to be **description formats**, not answers to the question of what a brand is. [Source: KOB, ch. 2]
3. **Brand as a repository of meaning.** "The brand is a principal repository of meaning in consumer culture, in both a residential and generative sense. It is both a storehouse and a powerhouse of meaning." [Source: KOB, ch. 3]
4. **Brand as a shorthand for expectations.** "A brand is a shorthand for the customer's expectations. What promise do they think you're making? ... That promise is your brand." And: "Hint: it's not your logo." [Source: TIM, ch. 13]

**Relationship and open point:** Definition 2 explicitly subsumes definition 4 as a mere description format, and ch. 3 opens by criticising the "cognitive or structural enterprise in models of strategic management" — that is, the approach of chapters 1 and 2 of the same book. **This is an internal contradiction in KOB that the book neither names nor resolves** (see the register of contradictions in `../research/system-overview.md`). Practical consequence: whoever uses KOB as a toolbox works with definitions 1 and 2; whoever uses it as cultural analysis works with definition 3 — and the two routes lead to different recommendations for action.

### The interchangeability test — the hardest test of brand value
"How do we know that brands like Verizon and AT&T are essentially worthless? **Because if we switched someone from one to the other, they wouldn't care.**" Consequence: "If you want to build a marketing asset, you need to invest in connection and other nontransferable properties. If people care, you've got a brand." [Source: TIM, ch. 13]
The cross-check: **the Nike hotel test.** "Nike doesn't have a hotel. If it did, you would probably have some good guesses as to what it would be like. That's Nike's brand." [Source: TIM, ch. 13]
And the boundary: **a commodity has no brand.** Whoever buys wheat by the tonne or bandwidth by the GB has no expectation beyond the specification. [Source: TIM, ch. 13]

### The brand prism and the primacy of perception
"Consumers rarely just see a product or service; they see the product together with the brand." And: "**Perceptions matter most** — how people perceive something matters far more than the absolute truth. The question generally isn't which product or service is best; the question is which product or service people **think** is best." [Source: KOB, introduction]

**The main piece of evidence in both books — the Tiffany/Wal-Mart earring study.** Three groups of MBA students were asked the expected price of a pair of 18-carat gold earrings with two 0.3-carat diamonds: unbranded, "From Tiffany", "From Wal-Mart".
- unbranded: **USD 550**
- with Tiffany: **USD 873** (plus almost 60 percent)
- with Wal-Mart: **USD 81** (minus 85 percent against unbranded, minus 91 percent against Tiffany)
The interpretation goes beyond status consumption: "‚Good quality' means something entirely different when it comes from Tiffany rather than from Wal-Mart. ... The distinction is not just conspicuous consumption; **you can't tell a Tiffany earring from a Wal-Mart earring from a distance.**" [Source: KOB, introduction]

## Frameworks, models and methods

### Perception as immediate, preconscious categorisation
**Definition:** "Perception is the immediate use of cues to form and recognize concepts (where misperception is also a possibility)." It runs very fast and normally **outside awareness**. [Source: KOB, ch. 2]
Illustration: A loud noise, flashing lights, a large red object — and one immediately perceives a fire engine. Size implies "bigger than a car", flashing lights and red imply "dangerous". And: "If you did not know what a fire truck was, **the cues would help you to acquire the concept.**" [Source: KOB, ch. 2]
**The everyday version of the same mechanism:** "Busy people don't care about your work as much as you do. ... **We scan instead of study.** And when we scan, we're asking, ‚What does this remind me of?'" [Source: TIM, ch. 13]

### The eight cue types of brand design
Every marketing description of a brand should be accompanied by a **small set of intended perceptual categories**; the cues are then designed to lead to those categories. To be included: **brand name, product category name, corporate identity, verbal lexicon, illustration, visual symbols, colour palette, functional form.** [Source: KOB, ch. 2]

**The fully worked case of Philadelphia (Kraft).** Brand concept: "Philadelphia is a touch of heaven every day." Target categories: rich, creamy, authentic, special reward, accessible every day, creative.
- **Name:** As a place name, "Philadelphia" suggests accessibility ("It is a place you can get to") and authenticity ("a real place, and a city with an important historical heritage"). "While it might not be the best name for a **new** product, it is a strong name against authenticity and accessibility."
- **Typography:** Plain, bold capitals — "the product needs no artifice; you recognize the name; you have come to the source."
- **Category name:** "cream cheese" on the brick (reinforces authenticity), "cream cheese spread" on the tub (more accessibility, "at some cost to authenticity"); the category name is set bolder on the brick.
- **Corporate identity:** Kraft historically connotes cheese and thereby contributes to authenticity.
- **Verbal lexicon:** "Original" in script on the brick versus "Regular" on the tub — the latter costs authenticity. In addition "Dairy" and "Rich, creamy taste".
- **Illustration:** A cloud (soft, natural, authentic, above the everyday). **The fine calibration: the cloud sits at the BOTTOM of the container, so that Philadelphia seems to lie above the clouds — this softens the risk of appearing too aspirational.**
- **Visual symbol:** The oval as a classic shape (authenticity) with a badge-like quality (special reward), **but executed plainly, so that everyday accessibility is preserved.**
- **Colour and form:** Silver for richness and reward, **but not gold, so that accessibility remains** — "a design that sparkled too much could inhibit the perception of everyday accessibility". White for creaminess, blue for the heavenly imagery. The brick emphasises creaminess and authenticity, the tubs everyday accessibility. [Source: KOB, ch. 2]

### Names as perceptual stimuli — two case analyses
**Negative, Porsche Cayenne:** short, memorable, durable across languages — but limited as a stimulus. "Somewhat literally the name suggests hot and spicy, but not much else. ... it may induce a negative reaction in that it may **detract from ‚high performance'**. Instead, the name should convey authenticity, speed, and flare." During development Porsche used the code name **"Sportility"**, and Calder judges: "Though obviously prosaic, this name might well have more perceptual impact ... The Porsche Sportility conveys a really sporty SUV better than Porsche Cayenne." [Source: KOB, ch. 2]
**Positive, BlackBerry:** Research in Motion internally called the device **PocketLink** and wanted to hit the categories connected, fast, friendly, fun, approachable, vital — PocketLink hit only "connected". The naming firm Lexicon supplied 25 alternatives (AirWire, Badge, Banter, Combio, ComTop, eBox, GamePlan, Geode, Hula, LiveRide, Mica, Nemo, Photon, Reon, Slide, Sling, Tailwind, Tecton, TelTop, Transite, Veon, Verb, Waterfall, Wheels, WorldTop). The rationale for BlackBerry, category by category: "something live, growing on a vine, and therefore **connected and vital**. As a fruit, a blackberry is **friendly and approachable**, not intimidating at all, and as a small, more unusual fruit, it is **fun**. Plus as a small fruit it is **fast** as well as easy to use. Even the sound of the name helps. BlackBerry is pronounced fast, and the alliteration of the b's shortens the word." [Source: KOB, ch. 2]

### The Meaning Management Audit — seven categories, seven verbs
The anthropological approach to brand meaning. Each category is a source of meaning with an associated practice. [Source: KOB, ch. 3]
| Category | Verb | What to do | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Archetypal Mythography | **cosmologize** | weave the brand into the way we grasp reality | "find the hero in Nike, the outlaw in Harley, the lover in Hallmark"; Coke as the Real Thing, Allstate as Good Hands, Visa as Everywhere You Want to Be |
| Cultural Biography | **historicize** | see the brand diachronically, as a life story amid changing social forces | Betty Crocker from the stern grandmother via the June Cleaver double and the coquettish businesswoman to the computer-morphed multi-ethnic composite — mirrors the shift from cooking from scratch via comfort food to the modular meal-solutions era |
| Everyday Ethnography | **contextualize** | understand behaviour as it actually happens, not as managers believe or customers remember it | The touching, weighing, smelling and tasting movements from the produce section are repeated covertly by anxious mothers at the baby-food shelf; doors work better in advertising **open with people behind them**, because the earliest experience of doors is isolation and fear |
| Utopian Cartography | **prophesize** | project the brand as a portal to an Arcadian ideal | retro branding: VW Beetle, Star Wars, Quisp, Airstream, Charlie, Sting Ray |
| Brand Iconography | **tangibilize** | make the virtual tangible; the more senses, the more real | "Visualize Big Blue. Smell Chanel No. 5. Touch the grips of Oxo tools. Taste Altoids. Hear the sound of Intel inside." |
| Semiotic Choreography | **customerize** | tailor it so that the customer says "It's me" | Carhartt's blue-collar integrity; J. Peterman's ironic copy; Amazon's recommendations; Starwood's preference memory |
| Moral Geography | **evangelize** | enable co-creation and foster communities | "theft of fire" (passive observing and feeding back, the group keeps its autonomy) versus "gift of starter dough" (active partnership close to sponsorship) |

### Image, essence, brandscape — the triangulation
- **Brand image** = the outer form, the artefact as offered. "the **operational** meaning ... the most susceptible to strategic manipulation." Tool: the **brand mantra** (Nike "Authentic Athletic Performance", Notre Dame "Life, Sweetness, and Hope", Burning Man "Radical Self-Expression", Starbucks "Rewarding Everyday Moments").
- **Brand essence** = the meaning that arises in the **customer's creative use**. "**A transformation occurs in the remaking of a brand from an image to an essence, from YOUR brand to MY brand.**" Evidence of appropriation: Coca-Cola becomes Coke, Federal Express becomes FedEx, McDonald's becomes Mickey D's, Target becomes Tarzhay; consumers "google" and "tivo"; they keep abandoned brands alive (Newton) and write new episodes for media brands (Star Trek, Xena).
- **Brandscape** = the web of positional meanings, "the material and symbolic environment that consumers build with marketplace products, images, and messages". Shown in three flagship stores: **Nike Town** (ascent from the natural via the cultural to the supernatural world: outdoors, street, market, gallery, museum, place of worship), **ESPN Zone** (Throne Zone, "quenching carnal desire through multiple senses"), **American Girl Place** (grandmothers dressed like mothers dressed like girls dressed like the dolls' dolls — "memory in the service of practice"). [Source: KOB, ch. 3]

## Decision criteria and heuristics

- **Logo investment rule (frugal):** "It matters less than your designer wants it to, but more than the typical committee realizes." If the brand is the mental shorthand for the promise, the logo is the sticky note that reminds you of it — **without a brand, a logo is meaningless.** Proof exercise: list five logos you admire; the prediction is that each stands for an admired **brand**. "Almost no one picks a swastika or the clever glyph of the bank who ripped them off." Counter-evidence the author names himself: a terrible logo can grace a fabulous brand, and **many of the best brands have no memorable logo (Google, Sephora, Costco).** Recommendation: "pick a logo, don't spend a ton of money or have a lot of meetings about it, and keep it for as long as you keep your first name." [Source: TIM, ch. 13]
  **Opposing position in the system:** KOB ch. 2 works through the name as a perceptual stimulus with 25 rejected alternatives, KOB ch. 16 devotes an entire chapter to the name, and KOB ch. 2 calibrates eight cue types in detail. **The two positions lead to opposite budget decisions; see the register of contradictions.** Note: Godin is talking about **logos**, Calder about **names and the whole set of cues** — the positions do not fully coincide, but both are formulated as a general rule.
- **Design test — the only concrete methodological prescription in the system:** Evaluate brand design and product design **separately**. Product design with trained panels in settings that **eliminate** perceptual stimuli. Brand design with real consumers, **exposed very briefly (a few seconds)**, so that they react "in a perceptual mode — fast and automatically". Then ask for recall of cues, optionally track eye movements, **and above all have the design rated on seven-point scales against the target categories.** **Focus groups are explicitly "one of the worst methods of brand design evaluation", because they prompt people to play design expert and thereby mask the real perceptual effect.** [Source: KOB, ch. 2]
- **Semiotics rule for choosing signs:** "Send a signal that feels like a sign we already trust, then change it enough to let us know that it's new, and that it's yours." Reason: whoever wants change must first go over an edge — but exactly this innovation reminds some people of something in the past that went wrong. [Source: TIM, ch. 13]
- **Flying no flag is a flag too:** "Not flying one is as intentional as flying one. The lazy thing to do is insist that you don't need a flag. **The foolish thing to do is pretend your features are so good that nothing else matters. Something else always matters.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 13]
- **Brand systems stretch only as far as the concept:** "What permits a brand to become a system or portfolio of products is a brand concept that is **broad enough** to allow different product versions to be categorized as fitting the concept." Chain of test questions using Philadelphia as the example: party dip yes (risk: the category name "dip" could suggest artificiality); frozen cheesecakes yes ("brand design would be easy"); a chilled canned drink problematic because of the category and the can shape; a hot drink perhaps. [Source: KOB, ch. 2]

## Typical problems and approaches to solving them

**"Our brand doesn't mean anything in particular to anyone."** That is the clutter finding: "Weak brands are bland; they don't stand for anything in particular, and so they mean essentially nothing." Named examples: **Sears** ("not particularly cheap and not particularly high quality. It's not just about tools and it's not just about apparel"), **Ford's Lincoln** ("no obvious associations; it is simply another brand"), **Charles Schwab** ("neither high service nor low cost"). Counter-images with a clear set of associations: Wal-Mart = low prices, Tiffany = luxury and exclusivity, BMW = performance driving, Vanguard = low-cost index funds, Red Bull = energy and excitement. [Source: KOB, introduction]

**"The design signals something other than intended."** Diagnosis via the cue categories: if the party dip sits in a fire-engine-red container, the colour stimulus categorises it as dangerous and spicy. "Eventually consumers **could** form the concept that the dip is a rich dairy product, but the use of red **does not facilitate** this categorization." [Source: KOB, ch. 2]

**"Why do the logos of big corporations all look the same?"** "It's not laziness. The designers are trying to remind you of a solid company." The same logic explains why car ads resemble one another: "the sameness sends a signal about the car being worth considering, a safe alternative for such a huge investment." And why fashion ads in Vogue look nothing like those in Field & Stream: "**You're not people like us if you don't talk — talk means typefaces, photo styles, copy — the way we expect you to.** This is what a good designer offers you. The chance to fit in." [Source: TIM, ch. 13]

**"Why is Nigerian spam so sloppy?"** The spelling mistakes are not a mistake. "**Because it's not for you.** Because they're sending a signal to people who are skeptical, careful, and well-informed: go away. The purpose of the email is to send a signal to the greedy and the gullible. ... **They'd rather lose you at the beginning than invest in you and lose you at the end.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 13]

**"Can good design compensate for a weak product?"** KOB explicitly answers yes, with a risk warning: "It may well be that a strong brand design can facilitate a positive brand concept **even in the face of a weak product design**. It might be possible to **cost-reduce the product quality** of Philadelphia and maintain the brand concept through perception via brand design. It is also possible in doing this to undermine the brand design so that consumers react adversely to misleading perceptions." [Source: KOB, ch. 2] **TIM contradicts this normatively** ("claims that are true, that we continually double down on in all our actions", ch. 5; "Quality ... is required but no longer sufficient", ch. 6). **The contradiction remains open; see the register.**

**"The meaning has slipped away from us and is becoming political."** KOB deals with this explicitly, TIM does not. Brand meaning is "only partially of marketers' own making", and the counter-movement is part of the phenomenon: "McDonaldization, Coca-Colonization, and Disneyfication are simultaneously courted and countered. **The swooshtika becomes a badge of infamy**, Ronald McDonald is toppled and graffitoed, and iPod adverts are morphed with images from the infamous Abu Ghraib prison." Two documented failures:
- **Toyota**, in a supposed homage to hip users, presented a golden miniature RAV4 in the front tooth of an anonymous African-American smile. "Consumers objected strongly to the rap-ethos allusion as an exercise in **stereotypification**, rather than as an exercise in insider bonding."
- **American Girl in 2005** had the mother of the new doll Marisol Luna characterise the Mexican-American Chicago neighbourhood of Pilsen as a dangerous place for children; the family moved to the suburbs. "A well-intentioned acknowledgment of demographic trends in the service of verisimilitude quickly and rightly becomes a **flashpoint for identity politics** in a plural society."
Lesson: "Marketers must recognize that meaning is highly contextual, and that **triangulation is essential** to avoid alienating those consumers they long most ardently to woo." [Source: KOB, ch. 3]

**"The brand name is actually bad — and the brand leads anyway."** The practitioner admits it himself: **Static Guard** holds **more than 90 percent market share** in its niche, and "the truth is that Static Guard is not much of a name — too easily copied, too much of a descriptor." Likewise Alberto-Culver concedes that its own naming rule is violated by successful brands. [Source: KOB, ch. 16] **This is the only self-qualification in the practitioner section and must be passed on when quoting.**

## Concrete examples
- **Harley-Davidson, 100th anniversary, August 2003:** over 100,000 bikers in Milwaukee, a parade of over 10,000 motorcycles, aficionados from 47 countries, the 18th consecutive year of revenue and earnings growth. "Harley-Davidson isn't unique because it makes good motorcycles ... **The brand transcends the product.**" [Source: KOB, introduction]
- **Brand graveyard:** Oldsmobile, Pan Am, pets.com, ValuJet, Chiffon, Yugo, Chemical Bank, MarchFirst, PaineWebber. [Source: KOB, introduction]
- **Brand longevity:** Harvard 1636, Moët & Chandon 1743, Pepsi 1898 — all still alive today. [Source: KOB, introduction]
- **Don LaFontaine** did over **five thousand** film and TV voice-overs — "not because he was more talented at speaking than anyone else, or because he was the cheapest. It's because **his head start compounded**." [Source: TIM, ch. 13]
- **Status flags without function:** Wheel-arch flares on expensive cars sit more than six inches from the wheel and have no function; the Cadillac XTS carries a tiny flare on each tail light. And driving gloves have a hole on the back because men with cars used to wear big watches — "over time, we forgot the big watch and just kept the hole." [Source: TIM, ch. 13]
- **The Apple 1984 spot:** Almost no one in the Super Bowl audience understood the Orwell references, "but the media-savvy talking heads instantly understood, and they took the bait and talked about it. ... **Apple's ad team only needed a million people to care.**" It took thirty years for the idea to travel from the million to everyone — "but it happened because of the brilliant use of **semiotics, not technology**." [Source: TIM, ch. 13] **Note: here Godin celebrates exactly the injection of borrowed meaning that he condemns in ch. 5 in 1950s soap advertising; see the register, internal contradiction in TIM.**
- **Witchsy.com ("Where's Keith?"):** Penelope Gazin and Kate Dwyer invented a fictitious third partner, "Keith", with his own email address. Emails from Keith were answered quickly, suppliers and partners addressed him by name and were more helpful. "**Denying them doesn't make them disappear.** ... To change the culture, we have no choice but to acknowledge the culture we seek to change." [Source: TIM, ch. 13]
- **19,000 drink combinations at Starbucks** and more than twenty kinds of toner for one laser printer as a measure of the glut: "**you're quite lucky if you have a brand at all.**" [Source: TIM, ch. 13]

## Connections to other subject areas
- **positioning** (`positioning.md`): The brand concept must be articulated before the design ("How do you want the consumer to categorize the product?"). Cues implement a positioning; they do not replace it.
- **architecture** (`architecture.md`): The reach of a brand concept decides how far it stretches; according to KOB ch. 6, meaning analysis is the tool for determining "how far it can extend".
- **audience** (`audience.md`): The same symbol works differently depending on worldview — the hoodie is a status sign in Silicon Valley and an alarm signal in East London.
- **organisation** (`organisation.md`): Meaning arises at all touchpoints, that is, through the behaviour of people, not only through signs.
- **measurement** (`measurement.md`): The design test from ch. 2 and the meaning audit from ch. 3 are measurement procedures; the valuation question itself lies in the measurement area.
